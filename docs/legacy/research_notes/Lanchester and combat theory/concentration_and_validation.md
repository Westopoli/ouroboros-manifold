# Concentration of force, defeat in detail and piecemeal arrival under Lanchester's square law; empirical validation of Lanchester models; Lanchester in real-time-strategy combat prediction

Confidence tags: VERIFIED (read in a primary or authoritative source by me), REPORTED (stated by a secondary source), ESTIMATED (my own derivation or arithmetic, shown), AMBIGUOUS (sources disagree; how is stated). Equations are in plain text. Notation: A, B are force sizes; alpha, beta are per-unit lethality coefficients; square law is dA/dt = -beta*B, dB/dt = -alpha*A, with state equation alpha*(A^2 - A0^2) = beta*(B^2 - B0^2). This file reports facts and what sources say; it makes no design recommendations.

Local working copies used for VERIFIED reads (scratchpad, not in repo): the OCR text of Lanchester's book, the AIIDE 2015 PDF, the Naval Research Logistics PDF of Lucas and Turkes (NPS Calhoun copy), Washburn's NPS notes, the arXiv PDF of Uriarte and Ontanon, Stanescu's 2018 thesis, and a Desmond NPS thesis that tabulates Dupuy's factors.

## 1. Lanchester's own concentration examples (the N-square law, Trafalgar, Nelson's memorandum)

### Takeaway
In "Aircraft in Warfare" (1914 serial, 1916 book) Lanchester derived the square law, showed that a force split into two halves can be beaten in detail by a force equal in total strength, and used Nelson's October 1805 memorandum (40 British ships against 46 allied) to show that Nelson's three-column plan gave a small square-law advantage (1088 vs 1058) where a conventional line gave a large deficit (1600 vs 2116). All numbers below were read in the primary text.

### Cited Findings
- Lanchester states the square-law condition in words and as N*r^2 = M*b^2: "the fighting strengths of the two forces are equal when the square of the numerical strength multiplied by the fighting value of the individual units are equal" (sections 21-26; equations (1)-(2) are dB/dt = -c*r and dr/dt = -k*b with c = k for equal units). [VERIFIED] — [Lanchester, Aircraft in Warfare (1916), archive.org full text](https://archive.org/stream/aircraftinwarfar00lancrich/aircraftinwarfar00lancrich_djvu.txt). Section 21 also footnotes that the law is not strictly true at the end of a fight, when the last few men are attacked by more than their own number.
- Ancient (one-on-one) conditions, section 20: 1000 men meet 1000 men; if the Blue 1000 instead concentrates on 500 of the Red force, half of Blue is wiped out in annihilating that half, and the second battle starts on equal terms (500 v 500). Under ancient conditions concentration therefore gives no gain beyond the linear difference. [VERIFIED] — same Lanchester text.
- Modern conditions, section 21, his own worked example: Blue 1000 meets a section of Red 500; Red is wiped out "with a loss of only about 134 men of the Blue force, leaving 866 to meet the remaining 500 of the Red force"; the victorious Blues annihilate the whole Red force of equal total strength "with a loss of only 293 men". [VERIFIED] — same Lanchester text.
- Section 22 unequal case: Red 1000 against Blue 1400 (ratio 1 : sqrt(2)). Fought as one battle, Reds are annihilated and Blues lose "only 400"; if Reds compel Blues to fight divided into two equal armies, the first battle (700 Blues) ends with Blues annihilated at a cost of "only 300" to Reds, and the second battle is between equal forces, "presumed drawn". [VERIFIED] — same Lanchester text. ESTIMATED check of his rounding: sqrt(1400^2 - 1000^2) = 980, so the Blue loss is 420, not 400; for the 700 v 1000 first battle Reds keep sqrt(1000^2 - 700^2) = 714, a loss of 286, not 300. His figures are rounded; the arithmetic is otherwise right.
- Section 38: for a battle fleet "separated into two equal parts" the required increase in total strength is "approximately 40 per cent — that is to say, in the relation of 1 to sqrt(2)" (Fig. 9, a right-angled triangle). He adds that the divided fleet's cost of victory is greater than a single fleet of equal total strength "in the proportion that the two sides of the right-angled triangle are greater than the hypotenuse". He also notes non-mathematical aggravators: the beaten enemy section may retreat to refit and return, and the first-engaged half is demoralised by knowing it is "cannon fodder". [VERIFIED] — same Lanchester text.
- Sections 40-42, Nelson's plan, numbers as Lanchester gives them: Nelson assumed 40 British sail of the line against 46 combined (the memorandum, not the actual battle). Columns: two of 16 and one of 8. The 32 ships were to envelop half (23 ships) of the combined fleet; "according to the n-square law this would give him a superiority of fighting strength of almost exactly two to one" (his footnote: 23 x sqrt(2) = 32.5). The eight-ship column was to detain the combined van. [VERIFIED] — same Lanchester text.
- Lanchester's arithmetic (section 42): British 32^2 + 8^2 = 1088; combined 23^2 + 23^2 = 1058; British advantage 30; "numerical equivalent of the remains of the British Fleet ... = sqrt(30) or 5.5 ships". Under "the usage of former days" (full lines, 46 v 40): combined 46^2 = 2116; British 40^2 = 1600; balance in favour of the enemy 516, "= sqrt(516) = 23 ships approximately" surviving. [VERIFIED] — same Lanchester text. The OCR prints the squares garbled; my recomputation matches: 1024 + 64 = 1088, 529 + 529 = 1058, 2116 - 1600 = 516, sqrt(516) = 22.7.
- Actual forces at Trafalgar were 27 British against 33 combined, "a rather less favourable ratio than assumed in the Memorandum". Lanchester says the scheme of concentration followed the original idea, that the light wind alone was enough to exclude the enemy van, and that "it is extremely doubtful whether the superior seamanship and gunnery of the British could have averted defeat" had the old method been used. He finds it "truly remarkable" that Nelson cut the enemy into two equal parts (the square-law minimum of total effective strength) and chose 32 as the nearest whole number to 23 x sqrt(2), and suggests Nelson had "some equivalent basis" for his figures even if he did not know the law. [VERIFIED] — same Lanchester text.
- Sections 40-41 (context): Lanchester quotes Villeneuve's pre-battle note predicting Nelson would "break our line, envelop our rear, and overpower with groups of his own ships as many as he can isolate and cut off", and notes a ship in the van needed hours to wear and come to the aid of the rear. [VERIFIED] — same Lanchester text.
- A later popular summary adds a 2009 Fox (Naval Postgraduate School) calculation for the actual 27 v 33: 33^2 - 27^2 = 360, so the British would have needed to be roughly 50% more efficient per ship in a straight engagement; ESTIMATED check 1089/729 = 1.49. And for 40 v 46 the British would need about 32% (2116/1600 = 1.32). [REPORTED] — [Doolanshire, "Force Concentration: Lanchester and Trafalgar"](http://www.doolanshire.net/2018/12/13/force-concentration-lanchester-and-trafalgar/). Fox's original (20th ICTCM proceedings, 2009) was not read.
- The same article reports Fox's three-phase model for the actual battle (columns of 13 and 14 cutting the allies into groups of 17 rear, 3 centre, 13 van; 13 British beat the 3, are joined by the 14, then fight the 17 and the 13) and a result of survivors "equivalent to 13 to 14 intact ships". [REPORTED] — same Doolanshire article. AMBIGUOUS: my square-law recomputation of that sequence gives about 16 ship-equivalents (13 vs 3: sqrt(169-9) = 12.65; plus 14 = 26.65; vs 17: sqrt(26.65^2 - 17^2) = sqrt(421.2) = 20.5; vs 13: sqrt(421.2 - 169) = sqrt(252.2) = 15.9). The article does not give Fox's coefficients, so the gap may be a different attrition coefficient or order of arrival; I could not resolve it.
- Lanchester's own historical examples of defeat in detail named in the text, as reported by a secondary source: the French defeat at Wurzburg (September 1796) and Napoleon's victory at Arcole two months later; the Saintes (1782) is the naval case. [REPORTED] — [Doolanshire](http://www.doolanshire.net/2018/12/13/force-concentration-lanchester-and-trafalgar/); Lanchester's text confirms the 1782 Rodney scheme in section 40.

### Inferences
- Lanchester's own framing is concentration as a quantity: the fighting strength of a set of separated forces is the sum of squares, so the same total numbers are worth less when divided (his Trafalgar sums are exactly this: 1088 + 0 versus 1058).
- The Nelson arithmetic is the "just enough" case: 32 ships against half the enemy yields roughly equality against the second half even before crediting the detached eight-ship column.

### Gaps
- I did not find Fox's 2009 proceedings paper itself, so his coefficients and the 13-14 result are unverified.
- Lanchester gives no confidence interval or sensitivity analysis; the examples are idealised (equal units, no morale, no retreat), which he acknowledges in sections 24-25.

## 2. Defeat-in-detail arithmetic under the square law

### Takeaway
Under the square law with equal unit quality, a force of size N fighting parts of an equal-total enemy in sequence ends with survivors sqrt(N^2 - sum of the squares of the parts). For two equal halves, survivors are about 0.707 N (a 29.3% loss); the same N against the unsplit enemy is a mutual-annihilation tie. Sources state the idea and several state the numbers; the sequence formula for k parts is my derivation.

### Cited Findings
- Lanchester states the sum-of-squares rule geometrically (section 34, Fig. 7): "two forces or armies a and b, if acting separately (in point of time), have only the fighting strength of a single force or army represented numerically by the line B" (the hypotenuse, sqrt(a^2 + b^2)); "the three separate forces, a, b and c, could be met on equal terms in three successive battles by a single army of the numerical strength C". [VERIFIED] — [Lanchester text](https://archive.org/stream/aircraftinwarfar00lancrich/aircraftinwarfar00lancrich_djvu.txt).
- Section 27: "the sum of the squares of the two portions of the Red force are for all values equal to the square of the Blue force" (the right-angle diagram, Fig. 5b). [VERIFIED] — same Lanchester text.
- Stanescu, Barriga and Buro summarise Lanchester's example: two identical forces of 1000; the Red force is "divided into two units of 500 men each which serially engage the single (1000 man) Blue force"; under the quadratic model "the Blue force completely destroys the Red force with only moderate loss (e.g., 30%) to itself", supporting the concentration axiom. They add that under the linear law with equal coefficients the serial split "will have the same outcome as without splitting: a draw". [VERIFIED] — [Stanescu, Barriga, Buro, AIIDE 2015 (PDF)](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12780/12780-52-16297-1-2-20201228.pdf).
- Final survivors of a square-law battle are sqrt(A0^2 - (beta/alpha)*B0^2) for the winner (Washburn's equation 2.4); example in the same notes: x0 = 1000, y0 = 800, alpha = 0.8, beta = 0.9 gives beta*y0^2 = 576,000 against alpha*x0^2 = 800,000, so the y side is eliminated and x_f = 529 although beta > alpha. [VERIFIED] — [Washburn, "Lanchester Systems", NPS notes](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf).
- Survivors form a Pythagorean relation: five units against three leave sqrt(25 - 9) = 4. [REPORTED] — [Ernest Adams, "The Designer's Notebook: Kicking Butt by the Numbers: Lanchester's Laws", Gamasutra, 6 Aug 2004](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-designer-s-notebook-kicking-butt-by-the-numbers-lanchester-s-laws).
- A popular summary gives the halving arithmetic: a force of 50 has strength proportional to 50^2 = 2500; divided into two 25s it is 25^2 + 25^2 = 1250, "one half of its value as a cohesive unit". [REPORTED] — [Doolanshire](http://www.doolanshire.net/2018/12/13/force-concentration-lanchester-and-trafalgar/).
- Mesterton-Gibbons's lecture notes give an intuition for why concentration pays: in a 1000 rifles v 200 machine guns (25x firepower) example, concentrated rifle fire puts five rifles on each machine gun, so each gun lasts a fifth as long and delivers only 5x rather than 25x the damage; the two sides are exactly equal. They conclude that concentrated fire favours a square-law outcome while positional (area) fire and one-to-one combat favour a linear-law outcome. [VERIFIED] — [Mesterton-Gibbons, "Lanchestrian Models of Human Combat", FSU MAP5932 lecture 2](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf).
- ESTIMATED derivation, force N against an enemy of total M split into sequential parts m1, m2, ... with equal alpha = beta: after part j the survivors satisfy N_j^2 = N^2 - (m1^2 + ... + mj^2), because the state quantity alpha*A^2 - beta*B^2 is conserved within each fight and the survivors carry over. N wins everything if N^2 > sum of mi^2. Worked cases:
  - N = 1000 against 500 + 500: sqrt(1000^2 - 500^2) = 866.0 survivors, then sqrt(866.0^2 - 500^2) = sqrt(500,000) = 707.1; total loss 29.3% (matches Lanchester's 293 and Stanescu's "e.g. 30%"). N = 1000 against the same 1000 unsplit: both sides reach zero (tie, per Lanchester's equal-forces curve, section 22).
  - Equal total N against k equal parts: survivors = N*sqrt(1 - 1/k): k=2 gives 0.707 N; k=3 gives 0.816 N; k=4 gives 0.866 N; k=5 gives 0.894 N; k=10 gives 0.949 N.
  - Minimum force that still wins against total M split into k equal parts: N > M/sqrt(k); k = 2 gives 0.707 M, which is Lanchester's 1 : sqrt(2).
  - Divided-attacker mirror: attacker total N arrives as two halves against a defender of N who loses nothing in between: defender survivors sqrt(N^2 - (N/2)^2) = 0.866 N after the first half, then sqrt(0.75 N^2 - 0.25 N^2) = 0.707 N after the second. Same arithmetic, defender wins.
  - Arrival in serial packets of size p, equal total 100 against a standing force of 100 (alpha = beta, each packet dies before the next arrives): standing-force survivors = sqrt(100^2 - 100*p): p = 1 gives 99.5; p = 5 gives 97.5; p = 10 gives 94.9; p = 25 gives 86.6; p = 50 gives 70.7; p = 100 gives 0. [ESTIMATED]
- Lanchester's own limit case (section 35, Fig. 8): if later arrivals are infinitesimal detachments, the vectors a, b, c, ... form a circle of radius a, "the fighting value of the added forces, no matter what their numerical aggregate ... is zero"; he reads this as "the complete impotence of small forces in the presence of one of overwhelming power", and says it does not apply to small forces concealed or "dug in", since the hypothesis that the large force can bring its weapons to bear is then broken. [VERIFIED] — [Lanchester text](https://archive.org/stream/aircraftinwarfar00lancrich/aircraftinwarfar00lancrich_djvu.txt). ESTIMATED consistency check: units arriving one at a time lose and kill 1/(2N) each in the limit, so N units cost the standing force about 0.5 unit, matching sqrt(N^2 - N) = N - 0.5.

### Inferences
- The defeat-in-detail benefit grows with the number of parts: it is 29.3% of N lost against two halves, about 18.4% against three parts (1 - 0.816), 13.4% against four, 5.1% against ten, all for equal total strength.
- The effect depends entirely on the sequential-engagement assumption (each part fully engaged and destroyed before the next arrives, with no regrouping or retreat). Lanchester himself lists retreat-and-refit as a reason the paper advantage overstates security (section 38).

### Gaps
- I did not find a source that states the general N*sqrt(1 - 1/k) formula; it is my derivation. The two-half case is stated in Lanchester (866 and 293) and Stanescu et al.
- No source found that gives numbers for unequal-quality parts (alpha != beta) in the sequential case; the generalisation is straightforward from Washburn's equation 2.4 but not sourced.

## 3. Reinforcement and staggered arrival in Lanchester-type models

### Takeaway
The classic Lanchester treatment (Lanchester sections 34-35) shows that the fighting strength of sequentially arriving forces is the root of the sum of squares, and that very small detachments add effectively nothing. Engel's Iwo Jima model is the standard example of a Lanchester model with an exogenous reinforcement rate. Formal results on optimal or staggered reinforcement exist, but I could read only abstracts of most.

### Cited Findings
- Lanchester section 34, Fig. 7 (above): a, b, c, ... arriving successively give aggregate strengths equal to the hypotenuses A, B, C, ...; section 35: infinitesimal arrivals give zero added fighting value. [VERIFIED] — [Lanchester text](https://archive.org/stream/aircraftinwarfar00lancrich/aircraftinwarfar00lancrich_djvu.txt).
- Washburn on why numbers weigh so heavily in the square law: "there are two distinct reasons for introducing a new unit into a square law battle: 1) the new unit fires at the enemy; 2) the new unit dilutes the enemy's fire against the units already in battle." A better weapon has no dilution effect, "which is why the lethality coefficient enters fighting strength only as the first power." [VERIFIED] — [Washburn, NPS notes](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf).
- Engel's model of Iwo Jima: dx/dt = r(t) - beta*y, dy/dt = -alpha*x, where x is U.S. troops ashore, y is Japanese, and r(t) is the rate at which U.S. troops were landed. [VERIFIED] — [Washburn, NPS notes](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf).
- The standard Lanchester equations contain no reinforcement or recruitment term: "Because these models do not include recruitment or reinforcement, group sizes decline from their original values." [VERIFIED] — [Mesterton-Gibbons lecture notes](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf).
- Stanescu et al. state the same limitation ("a fixed strength (no reinforcements)"), and in their StarCraft data they cut a battle record when "new units reinforce either side", noting that StarCraft battles "do not take longer than that [800 frames, about 30 seconds], except if there is a constant stream of reinforcements or one of the players keeps retreating, which is difficult to model". [VERIFIED] — [Stanescu et al. 2015](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12780/12780-52-16297-1-2-20201228.pdf).
- Uriarte and Ontanon report that of 140,797 combats extracted from 600 StarCraft replays, 99,598 "ended by reinforcement" and 40,820 "ended in peace"; they then use a subset of 6,066 combats for the winner-prediction tests. [VERIFIED] — [Uriarte and Ontanon, "Combat Models for RTS Games", arXiv:1605.05305](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.05305).
- McCartney (2022) studies Lanchester aimed-fire battles repeated with troop replenishment between engagements: constant reinforcement "consistently yields decisive victory", linear reinforcement can produce stalemate, and quadratic reinforcement yields stalemate, quasi-periodic oscillation, chaos and fractal partitioning of the strength space. [REPORTED] — [McCartney, Physica A 586 (2022), RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/phsmap/v586y2022ics0378437121007500.html); full text not read.
- Epstein's Adaptive Dynamic Model, as described in a search-result summary of his 1988 article, uses explicit models of "the mutual attrition of engaged forces, the flow of reinforcing units into the battle, and the movement of battle fronts over time" as the preferred way to assess a theatre balance. [REPORTED] — [Epstein, "Dynamic Analysis and the Conventional Balance in Europe", International Security 12(4), Spring 1988, pp. 154-165](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/446390/summary) (abstract only, via search snippet; Project MUSE blocked direct reading).
- Titles and snippets only (not read): "Direct and inverse solution of the Lanchester square law with general reinforcement schedules" (European Journal of Operational Research, 1994), "An optimal control problem in determining the optimal reinforcement schedules for the Lanchester equations" (Computers and Operations Research), and Sheeba et al., "Optimal resource allocation and redistribution strategy in military conflicts with Lanchester square law attrition" (Naval Research Logistics, 2008). [REPORTED, title level only] — [ScienceDirect listing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/037722179490412X), [Wiley listing](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/nav.20303).
- ESTIMATED, overlapping (not serial) arrival, square law, alpha = beta = 1, standing force B = 100, attacker total 120 delivered as 60 now plus 60 later (I integrated the two ODEs numerically; a unit is dead in about 0.01 time units under 100 shooters). Concentrated 120 v 100 leaves sqrt(14400 - 10000) = 66.3 attackers.
  - Second 60 arrives at t = 0: 66.3 attacker survivors.
  - Second 60 arrives at t = 0.2: 46.1 attacker survivors (same total, same win, 30% fewer survivors).
  - Second 60 arrives at t = 0.5: the attacker loses; 30.6 defenders remain.
  - Second 60 arrives at t = 0.8 or later (first 60 already dead): defenders end with 52.9, as the sequential formula gives: sqrt(100^2 - 60^2) = 80, then the second 60 loses to 80 and leaves sqrt(80^2 - 60^2) = sqrt(2800) = 52.9; the attacker is wiped out although its total is 20% larger.
- ESTIMATED invariant argument: within a fight alpha*A^2 - beta*B^2 is constant; a reinforcement of size D arriving when the receiving force has a survivors adds alpha*(2*a*D + D^2) to that quantity. If all arrive together the cross term is 2*D*(earlier units present at full strength); staggered, it is 2*D*a with a reduced by the attrition suffered in the interim, so the end-of-battle strength is strictly lower. The size of the penalty is the attrition the earlier packets take while the later packet is still absent.

### Inferences
- The unambiguous statements in the literature I could read are that (a) strength adds as squares across separated arrivals (Lanchester) and (b) the base Lanchester equations contain no reinforcement term and need an extension (Engel, McCartney, the optimal-control papers).
- Reinforcement streams are common in real RTS data: roughly 71% (99,598 of 140,797) of extracted StarCraft combats ended because new units joined, and the 2015 Lanchester predictor excludes such cases by construction.

### Gaps
- I found no source that states a general theorem "feeding units in a few at a time is worse" with worked numbers beyond Lanchester's own sections 34-35; the numerical tables above are my own.
- I could not read the full text of McCartney 2022, the EJOR 1994 reinforcement-schedule paper, the 2008 Sheeba paper or Taylor's "Lanchester-Type Models of Warfare" (DTIC), so their quantitative results are unreported here.

## 4. Empirical fit of Lanchester models to historical battle data

### Takeaway
The first validations (Engel 1954, Iwo Jima) fitted the square law to one side's time series and looked good, but every later study with two-sided daily data (Bracken 1995 and Fricker 1998 on the Ardennes, Lucas and Turkes 2004 on Kursk and the Ardennes, Hartley and Helmbold 1995 on Inchon-Seoul) found that no basic Lanchester law fits well or consistently beats the others, and that fitted parameters are very sensitive to method.

### Cited Findings
- Engel (1954), "A Verification of Lanchester's Law", Operations Research 2:163-171, fitted a model with a reinforcement term to Iwo Jima. Best fit with x as "U.S. troops not killed, wounded, or missing": beta = 0.0544 U.S. casualties per Japanese man-day and alpha = 0.0106 Japanese casualties per U.S. man-day (beta/alpha = 5.1); "the fit in both cases is quite good", with different parameters for the "U.S. troops alive" variant. There were initially 20,000 Japanese, all killed; U.S. casualties were about 20,000 of which about 25% were killed. Washburn's reading: equal total casualties despite a five-fold Japanese lethality advantage is explained by U.S. numerical superiority under the square law. Engel also fitted Crete (0.104 Allied casualties per German man-day, 0.0162 German per Allied man-day) and had trouble defining an Allied troop. [VERIFIED] — [Washburn, NPS notes](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf).
- Caveat on Engel: Lucas and Turkes note that Iwo Jima (and Inchon-Seoul) "had reliable time-phased data for only one side" (daily U.S. data plus start and end Japanese figures). [VERIFIED] — [Lucas and Turkes, "Fitting Lanchester equations to the battles of Kursk and Ardennes", Naval Research Logistics 51(1):95-116 (2004), NPS Calhoun copy](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bc44457c-0423-42cc-bd62-585149fbf95d/content).
- Bracken (1995), "Lanchester models of the Ardennes campaign", Naval Research Logistics 42(4):559-577: a detailed database was built and four Lanchester models fitted; "two-sided time histories of warfare on battles and campaigns are very rare, so Lanchester models have seldom been validated with historical data". Per Lucas and Turkes, Bracken used a constrained grid search on the first 10 days and concluded that "the Lanchester linear equation fits the campaign" with an "attacker advantage"; Lucas and Turkes caution that his near-linear results are "primarily due to his constrained grid search, which focused on parameters close to the linear law". [VERIFIED for the Lucas-Turkes description; abstract REPORTED] — [Wiley abstract page](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/1520-6750(199506)42:4%3C559::AID-NAV3220420405%3E3.0.CO;2-R) and the Calhoun PDF above.
- Fricker (1998), "Attrition models of the Ardennes campaign", Naval Research Logistics 45(1):1-22: regression on all 32 days plus air-sortie data; "neither the Lanchester linear or Lanchester square laws fit the data well"; a force's losses depended more on its own size than the opponent's, like the logarithmic law. One Fricker model has q = 4.6 and p = 0, which Lucas and Turkes call "unbelievably large": a force "could reduce its casualty rate by a factor of 24 by sending half of its troops home". [VERIFIED via Lucas and Turkes; abstract REPORTED] — [Calhoun PDF](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bc44457c-0423-42cc-bd62-585149fbf95d/content); [ResearchGate entry](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/238158798_Attrition_models_of_the_Ardennes_Campaign).
- Wiper, Pettit and Young re-analysed the Ardennes data with Bayesian methods: with an informative prior on the exponents (mean 0.5, 0.5) the posterior mean on Bracken's 10 days was (0.51, 0.48), nearly the prior; on 32 days (0.27, 0.84), concluding "the logarithmic law fits best [and] the linear laws cannot be rejected, but the square law does seem implausible"; with a 200x vaguer prior the posterior moved to (-0.08, 4.88), near Fricker. Lucas and Turkes: conclusions "depend critically on the sharpness of the prior". Clemens fitted Kursk two ways and got (p, q) = (0, 1.62) by Newton-Raphson versus (5.32, 3.63) by log regression. [VERIFIED] — [Calhoun PDF](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bc44457c-0423-42cc-bd62-585149fbf95d/content).
- Lucas and Turkes (2004) abstract, quoted: "a variety of Lanchester models fit the data about as well ... none of the basic Lanchester laws (i.e., square, linear, and logarithmic) fit the data particularly well or consistently perform better than the others ... no constant attrition coefficient Lanchester law fits very well. The failure to find a good-fitting Lanchester model suggests that it may be beneficial to look for new ways to model highly aggregated attrition." Their general model: dB/dt = a*(d or 1/d)*R^p*B^q and dR/dt = b*(1/d or d)*B^p*R^q, with d a defender or attacker tactical parameter; the square law is p = 1, q = 0, linear p = q = 1, logarithmic p = 0, q = 1. [VERIFIED] — [Calhoun PDF](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bc44457c-0423-42cc-bd62-585149fbf95d/content).
- Lucas and Turkes fit numbers (R^2, fraction of variation explained), Kursk 14 days with the tactical parameter, Bracken's weights: square 0.081, linear 0.131, logarithmic 0.085, best (p = 5.87, q = 1.01) 0.238; using manpower: square 0.074, linear 0.116, log 0.086, best (p = 7.74, q = 3.41) 0.234. For the best Kursk model the German side alone has R^2 = 0.870 and the Soviet side 0.124 (an outlier on day 8). Ardennes, Bracken's weights: square 0.367, linear 0.291, log 0.330, best (p = 0.91, q = -0.61) 0.381; manpower: square 0.079, linear -0.226, log 0.025, best 0.280. The square law is the best-fitting basic law for the Ardennes with Bracken's weights; the linear law is the best basic law for Kursk. A negative R^2 means worse than predicting the mean. [VERIFIED] — same Calhoun PDF (Tables 5 and 6).
- Hartley and Helmbold (1995), "Validating Lanchester's square law and other attrition models", Naval Research Logistics 42(4):609-633, tested the Inchon-Seoul campaign (Korean War) with regression, AIC and CAIC: the data do not fit a single constant-coefficient homogeneous square-law battle; they fit a set of three such battles (a new one every six or seven days), but also fit three constant-casualty-model battles just as well. They conclude "any square law effects are largely masked by other factors" and that without two-sided data with many periodic casualty figures the homogeneous square law cannot be validated. [REPORTED abstract; the two quotations VERIFIED via Lucas and Turkes] — [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/1520-6750(199506)42:4%3C609::AID-NAV3220420408%3E3.0.CO;2-W) and the Calhoun PDF.
- Willard (1962) and Fain (1977), engagement-level regressions. Fain's reprinted study of 60 WWII engagements from four Italian campaigns (Salerno 9, Volturno 20, Anzio 11, Rome 20) in HERO data: attacker won 34, defender 23, three ambiguous. Willard's exponent g (0 for the square law, 1 for the linear law) ranged from -0.27 to -0.87 (mostly near -0.5), which Willard called disconcerting because it implies casualty-producing power rises as force size falls; Fain's repeat gave g = -0.594, a second-order variant -0.413, and with a combat-power ratio in place of the raw size ratio +0.466. Splitting by defence posture, tentatively: against fortified positions the attacker suffers by the square law and the defender by the linear law; this is reversed against prepared positions and hasty defences. Fain also cites a Korean War study where casualties were predicted "to within 1 to 13 percent" when simulated units followed their historical tactics, and concludes the equations may be adequate "while the forces are actually engaged" but poor for large multi-day battles. [VERIFIED — text of the 1977 article as reprinted] — [Fain, "The Lanchester Equations and Historical Warfare", History, Numbers and War 1(1), 1977, reprinted by the Dupuy Institute 2 May 2018](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2018/05/02/the-lanchester-equations-and-historical-warfare/).
- Dupuy's own attrition method is not Lanchester: QJM post-1900 casualties = standard rate (attacker 2.8%, defender 1.5% per day) x force-size factor x mission factor x force-ratio factor x day/night x special conditions; the force-size multiplier was 2.0 below 5,000 men, 1.5 for 5,000-10,000 and 1.0 for 10,000-20,000. [REPORTED] — [Lawrence, "How Attrition is Calculated in the QJM vs the TNDM" (TNDM Newsletter, Dec 1996, reprinted)](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2019/12/26/how-attrition-is-calculated-in-the-qjm-vs-the-tndm/).
- A recent paper by S. K. Das (IASRI, New Delhi; Kursk, MLE fits with heterogeneous Lanchester equations; publication date not established) restates the literature the same way (Fricker: neither law adequate; Hartley and Helmbold: three-battle fits; Lucas and Turkes: parameter-search showing many exponent pairs fit) and reports that maximum-likelihood fitting beat Bracken, Fricker and Clemens' least-squares recipes on the Kursk tank data. [REPORTED, preprint-grade] — [Das, "Modeling Competitive Dynamics with Heterogeneous Lanchester Equations: Insights from Kursk Battle"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.06666) (the arXiv id resolved to this PDF; I did not verify its peer-review status).

### Inferences
- The consistent empirical story across Lucas-Turkes, Fricker, Hartley-Helmbold and Fain is that Lanchester forms fit short fully-engaged fights better than long multi-day battles, and that fitted exponents depend on the fitting method as much as on the battle.
- Engel's good fit is confirmed by the later literature only in the narrow sense that his two-parameter model (with a landing-rate term) tracks one side's time series; Lucas and Turkes treat it as one-sided evidence.

### Gaps
- I did not read Engel (1954), Bracken (1995) or Fricker (1998) in full (paywalled); their claims are from abstracts and from Lucas and Turkes' description.
- A DTIC report ("Revisiting Engel's Verification of Lanchester's Square Law", AD1201776) returned HTTP 403; one search summary describes a 2022 Stymfal re-evaluation of Iwo Jima finding "exceptional congruence" for the square law, but I could not read it. [REPORTED, unverified] — [DTIC AD1201776](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/trecms/pdf/AD1201776.pdf).
- Fain's Table 4 (how often the numerically inferior side won in the WWII and Willard data sets) was not retrievable in the reprint text.

## 5. Dupuy's Quantified Judgment Model multipliers, the 3:1 rule and its critics

### Takeaway
Dupuy's published defensive-posture factors are 1.0 offence, 1.3 hasty defence, 1.5 prepared defence and 1.6 fortified defence, with comparative defence-to-offence strength ranging from about 1.3 to more than 3.0 once terrain is added; surprise is worth about 1.10 (minor) to 2.24 or 2.5 (complete). Large historical datasets show attackers winning more than half the time at force ratios of 1.0 to 1.49, which contradicts the doctrinal reading of the 3:1 rule; the International Security debate (Epstein v Mearsheimer, Dupuy 1989) was about whether static force ratios or dynamic attrition models are the right tool.

### Cited Findings
- Posture factors in the QJM, from Dupuy's "Understanding War" (1987): offence 1.0, hasty defence 1.3, prepared defence 1.5, fortified defence 1.6. Default vulnerability factors in the same table: offensive 0.9, defensive 2.8. Operational variable factors are multiplied together: vulnerability x posture x mobility x logistic capability x surprise x air superiority; environmental factors (terrain x weather x season) are multiplied separately and combined with the operational factors, then with the Combat Effectiveness Value. [REPORTED, tabulated by a thesis from Dupuy 1987] — [Desmond, NPS thesis (Dupuy Table 3 reproduced)](https://faculty.nps.edu/nlmiller/docs/Matthew%20Desmond.pdf).
- Dupuy's statement of defensive strength (Understanding War, 1987, p. 26): "the comparative strength of defense to offense can range from a factor with a minimum value of about 1.3 to maximum value of more than 3.0", depending on preparation and terrain; his example: even a hasty defence in rolling terrain with some vegetation raises combat power "by more than 50%". Dupuy treats posture and terrain as multipliers, not additive terms (p. 2). [REPORTED] — [Dupuy Institute, "Dupuy's Verities: The Power of Defense"](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2018/05/30/dupuys-verities-the-power-of-defense/).
- The same article reports that U.S. Army and Marine doctrine "historical minimum planning ratios" require 3:1 against a prepared or fortified defender and 2.5:1 against a hasty defence. [REPORTED] — same article.
- Surprise: complete, substantial and minor categories chosen by analyst judgement; the net effect on combat power was about 2.24 (complete) to 1.10 (minor) in the original QJM (Numbers, Predictions and War, 1977, applied through vulnerability and mobility), and 2.20 to 1.10 in the simplified form of "Attrition" (1990); effects last three days "declining by one-third each day". The Dupuy Institute states that Dupuy set these "based on his judgement ... He never validated them back to his data himself". [REPORTED] — [Dupuy Institute, "The Combat Value of Surprise"](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2018/06/29/the-combat-value-of-surprise/).
- Elsewhere Dupuy writes that the side achieving surprise can multiply force strength "by a factor ranging between 1.5 and 2.5 (or even more in some cases)"; in the TNDM the complete-surprise multiplier is 2.5 (degraded by historical period), amphibious attack across a beach 2.0, and a noticeably superior CEV 1.5. [REPORTED] — [Dupuy Institute, "Trevor Dupuy and the 3-1 Rule"](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2016/07/11/trevor-dupuy-and-the-3-1-rule/) and [Lawrence, TNDM newsletter reprint](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2019/12/26/how-attrition-is-calculated-in-the-qjm-vs-the-tndm/).
- AMBIGUOUS: the complete-surprise value is quoted as 2.24 / 2.20 (QJM / simplified 1990 form) in one Dupuy Institute article and 2.5 (TNDM) and 1.5-2.5 in others. These are different model versions and calibrations by the same author, not a data conflict, but a single "Dupuy surprise multiplier" does not exist.
- The 3:1 rule: a military aphorism whose "origin is unknown and unattributed"; Dupuy asked "is it numbers of men, or weapons? Is it firepower? Is it some other calculation of a combat power ratio?" (Numbers, Predictions and War, 1979, p. 13). His own aphorism: "In the average modern battle, the attacker's numerical strength is about double the defender's." [REPORTED] — [Dupuy Institute, "Trevor Dupuy and the 3-1 Rule"](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2016/07/11/trevor-dupuy-and-the-3-1-rule/).
- CAA/HERO database of 1600-1973 battles (Helmbold and Khan, CHASE progress report, 1986), cited by Epstein 1988 p. 156: attackers with 3:1 or better manpower advantage succeeded 74% of the time; attackers won 58% to 63% of the time between a 1.5:1 disadvantage and under 3:1; only 106 of 598 cases (17.7%) had a 3:1 or better attacker advantage. [REPORTED] — same Dupuy Institute article.
- Dupuy Institute 672-case division-level database, 1904-1991, percent of cases the attacker won by force ratio (cases in brackets): 0.50-0.99: 42% (77); 1.00-1.49: 55% (150); 1.50-1.99: 59% (123); 2.00-2.49: 71% (56); 2.50-2.99: 83% (53); 3.00-3.49: 69% (48); 3.50-3.98: 77% (30); 4.06-5.87: 65% (66); 6.06-7.90: 88% (17); 8.20-17.87: 100% (22). A culled 570-case set (dropping 102 limited-attack engagements) gives 52%, 62%, 66%, 80%, 83%, 70%, 76%, 73%, 88%, 100% in the same bins. The author's conclusion: "the U.S. Army rule is wrong" (the Army reading being a 50% chance of defender success at 3:1). [REPORTED, Dupuy Institute analysis, not peer reviewed] — [Lawrence, "The U.S. Army Three-to-One Rule versus the 752 Case Division-level Data Base 1904-1991"](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2019/10/31/tests-using-the-752-case-division-level-data-base/).
- The International Security exchange (all in International Security): Mearsheimer, "Why the Soviets Can't Win Quickly in Central Europe" 7(1), Summer 1982; Epstein, "Dynamic Analysis and the Conventional Balance in Europe" 12(4), Spring 1988, pp. 154-165; Mearsheimer, "Numbers, Strategy, and the European Balance" 12(4), Spring 1988; Epstein, "The 3:1 Rule, the Adaptive Dynamic Model, and the Future of Security Studies" 13(4), Spring 1989; Mearsheimer, "Assessing the Conventional Balance: The 3:1 Rule and Its Critics" 13(4), Spring 1989, pp. 54-89; Dupuy, "Combat Data and the 3:1 Rule" 14(1), Summer 1989. [VERIFIED bibliography as printed by the Dupuy Institute; pages for Epstein 1988 and Mearsheimer 1989 from search snippets] — [Dupuy Institute, "The Great 3-1 Rule Debate"](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2018/11/23/the-great-3-1-rule-debate/).
- Positions: Epstein used Dupuy's empirical research against reliance on the 3:1 rule and argued for dynamic analysis; Mearsheimer questioned Dupuy's data and, per a search-snippet summary of his 1989 article, narrowed the claim by arguing the rule applies only to breakthrough battles and giving deductive and empirical grounds; Dupuy's 1989 article defended his data and pointed out errors in Mearsheimer's assertions, after which the editors ended the thread. [REPORTED] — [Dupuy Institute, "The Great 3-1 Rule Debate"](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2018/11/23/the-great-3-1-rule-debate/); [Mearsheimer 1989 listing](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/446798/summary) (Project MUSE page itself blocked).
- Dupuy Institute summary of the larger question the debate raised: whether military power can be studied with "static measures like force ratios" or needs "a more dynamic analysis", and the role of doctrine, politics and formal modelling (quoting Friedman, Security Studies 20, 2011). [REPORTED] — same article.

### Inferences
- The data in the Dupuy Institute tables do not show a threshold at 3:1; success rises from about 42% (0.5-1.0) to about 83% (2.5-3.0) and is noisy above that (65% to 100% across higher bins with small counts).
- Dupuy's multiplicative factors are calibrated judgement, not equation-derived; two of the three families here (surprise values and part of the terrain factors) are self-described as his judgement or unvalidated by him.

### Gaps
- The Dupuy terrain-factor table (flat, rolling, rugged, urban values) is shown only as an image in the source article; I could not extract the numeric values. A search summary mentioned a combined "rolling, gentle, mixed = 1.3" but I could not source it, so it is not asserted.
- I could not read Mearsheimer (1989), Epstein (1988, 1989) or Dupuy (1989) directly (Project MUSE and Semantic Scholar blocked automated reads); positions are from the Dupuy Institute and search snippets.
- One search summary of a Dupuy Institute tag page reported that only 19% of 3:1 engagements in a "628-engagement" set supported a RAND construct; I could not confirm this in a page I read, so I do not rely on it.
- A DTIC monograph, "An Examination of Force Ratios" (Christian, AD1083211), appeared in results but could not be downloaded.

## 6. Lanchester in real-time-strategy combat prediction (StarCraft and related)

### Takeaway
The principal work is Stanescu, Barriga and Buro (AIIDE 2015), which generalises the attrition order, learns per-unit strengths, and predicts StarCraft battle winners with 89.8% to 93.2% accuracy on SparCraft simulations, and lifted a UAlbertaBot attack/retreat rule to 69.7% (learned) against six 2014 AIIDE bots. Follow-ups (Uriarte and Ontanon 2016; Stanescu's 2018 thesis) report 91-94% winner accuracy on replay data and an attrition order of 1.7 for the microRTS game. I found no source analysing concave, surround or high-ground effects in Lanchester terms.

### Cited Findings
- Stanescu, Barriga and Buro, "Using Lanchester Attrition Laws for Combat Prediction in StarCraft", AIIDE 2015, pp. 86-92. The generalised law: dA/dt = -beta*A^(2-n)*B and dB/dt = -alpha*B^(2-n)*A, with state solution alpha*(A^n - A0^n) = beta*(B^n - B0^n); n = 1 is the linear law, n = 2 the square law, and "our experiments suggest that for StarCraft battles an attrition order of about 1.56 works best on average". They state that the square law "has nothing to do with range - what is really important is the rate of acquiring new targets". Default unit strength alpha_i = dmg(i)*HP(i) (damage per frame times hit points), later learned by maximum likelihood with gradient ascent and a regularisation term pulling toward defaults; injured units are handled by scaling with current HP, giving alpha_i = dmg(i)*currentHP(i); army remainder score = alpha*A^o - beta*B^o. [VERIFIED] — [Stanescu et al. 2015](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12780/12780-52-16297-1-2-20201228.pdf).
- Their rationale for attrition order above 1: in StarCraft focus fire matters. Worked example: n marines at 1/n health against one healthy marine; a linear model predicts a draw while in reality the single marine can focus fire and the lopsided result leaves army A with all but one marine. With o = 1.65 the strength of an injured marine is m/2.1383 at 1/2 health, m/2.9887 at 1/3 and m/3.7221 at 1/4, close to m/n. [VERIFIED] — same paper.
- Battle-prediction accuracy on SparCraft-simulated battles (population limit 100, seven unit types, tested on 500 battles, averages over 20 experiments), by number of training battles: Lanchester 89.8% (10), 91.0 (20), 91.7 (50), 92.2 (100), 93.0 (200), 93.2 (500); the earlier Bayesian model 83.0, 86.9, 88.5, 89.4, 90.3, 91.2. The Lanchester model beats the Bayesian model by 6.8 points at 10 training battles and 2 points at 500 and, with default unit strengths, needs very little training data. [VERIFIED] — same paper (Table 1). The Bayesian model is Stanescu, Hernandez, Erickson, Greiner and Buro, "Predicting army combat outcomes in StarCraft", AIIDE 2013.
- Tournament results, 200 games per match-up against six 2014 AIIDE bots (IceBot, LetaBot, Aiur, Xelnaga, original UAlbertaBot, MooseBot), average win rate: original UAlbertaBot 41.6%; simulation-based attack/retreat 60.8%; Lanchester model with default strengths 63.9%; Lanchester with strengths trained on 500 battles per opponent 69.7%. Against Aiur the learned version reached 86.0%, against MooseBot 93.0%, but only 23.5% against IceBot (bunkers, which the model handles poorly because their strength depends on loaded marines and repair). The authors note UAlbertaBot only builds zealots, so the model mainly decides "waiting for an extra zealot" or "attacking with one zealot less". [VERIFIED] — same paper (Table 2).
- Stated limitations: unit independence (no medics or repair), "completely ignores unit positions", no terrain, no upgrades beyond treating upgraded units as separate types, battles cut at 800 frames (~30 seconds) or when reinforcements join; training battles excluded if total damage < 80 hit points and both sides lost < 20% of starting total hit points. Future work named: strength matrices per unit pair, sniping, using spatial information. [VERIFIED] — same paper.
- Uriarte and Ontanon, "Combat Models for RTS Games" (arXiv:1605.05305, IEEE TCIAIG): Target-Selection Lanchester Square Law model (TS-Lanchester2), Sustained DPF and Decreasing DPF. Winner-prediction accuracy over 6,066 replay combats (full dataset) and 4,558 SparCraft-compatible combats: TS-Lanchester2 static damage-per-frame matrix 94.10% and 94.45%; learned matrix 90.66% and 91.33%; Sustained static 93.60% and 94.12%; Decreasing static 93.55% and 93.92% (random target selection); LTD2 static 92.09% and 93.22%; LTD static 90.37% and 91.82%; SparCraft scripts 84.69% to 88.02% on the compatible subset. Inside a Monte Carlo tree search bot (100 games against the built-in Terran AI on Benzene), the Decreasing model won 75% of games and lost none. They also note Stanescu et al. "only used this model to predict the winner". [VERIFIED] — [Uriarte and Ontanon, arXiv:1605.05305](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.05305). An earlier Lanchester-style combat model for StarCraft is Soemers's 2014 Maastricht master's thesis (cited there; not read).
- Stanescu's 2018 PhD thesis (University of Alberta), "Outcome Prediction and Hierarchical Models in Real-Time Strategy Games", reprints the AIIDE paper as chapter 5 and, for the microRTS game, finds "an attrition order of o = 1.7 works best on average"; the Lanchester state evaluator costs 0.087 microseconds per call against 0.012 for a simple evaluator and 147 microseconds for a neural network on CPU; winner-prediction by game stage is shown in a figure (not tabulated in text). The thesis notes its battle model does not represent reinforcements or retreating, and that terrain and high ground are a separate spatial-reasoning problem. [VERIFIED] — [Stanescu thesis (PDF)](https://skatgame.net/mburo/ps/thesis_stanescu_2018.pdf).
- Developer writing: Ernest Adams (Gamasutra, 2004) proposes square-law arithmetic for RTS unit balancing: if aliens produce units at twice the rate with equal efficiency, they field a force "twice as large, but four times as strong", so each should cost twice as much; and a technological edge must be N^2 to offset an N-fold numerical disadvantage; he argues historical accuracy is irrelevant, the model is useful for balance. No playtest data is given. [REPORTED] — [Adams 2004](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-designer-s-notebook-kicking-butt-by-the-numbers-lanchester-s-laws).

### Inferences
- The attrition order fitted to RTS data (1.56 for StarCraft, 1.7 for microRTS) lies between the linear and square law, and the authors justify it by focus fire on damaged units rather than by range, in line with Mesterton-Gibbons's point that target concentration drives square-law behaviour.
- The published predictors are position-blind: both the 2015 paper and the thesis say so, so no published accuracy number covers surround, concave, choke or high-ground effects.

### Gaps
- No published analysis found that models concave formations, surrounds, high-ground bonuses or army-size effects in StarCraft II in Lanchester terms. A paper titled "Battle Prediction System in StarCraft Combined with Topographic Considerations" (Springer 2020) exists but is paywalled and was not read. [REPORTED, title only] — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-15-0187-6_14).
- A Team Liquid forum post, "Simple Combat" (tl.net/forum/starcraft-2/465353), returned HTTP 403 and was not read. Search-summary claims on unit-count balance (for example 50 marines against a Battlecruiser) came from a non-authoritative site and are not asserted.
- No game-developer source with measured balance numbers (beyond Adams's illustrative arithmetic) was found.
