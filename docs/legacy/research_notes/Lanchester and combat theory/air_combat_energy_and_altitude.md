## 1. Boyd and Christie's Energy-Maneuverability theory: equations and what it was used for

### Takeaway
E-M theory expresses an aircraft's energy state as specific energy Es = h + V^2/(2g) and its ability to change that energy as specific excess power Ps = V(T - D)/W; altitude and speed are interchangeable currencies of the same quantity, which is why a height advantage is a stored speed advantage. It was used to compare fighters (US versus Soviet, F-4 versus MiG-21) and fed the F-15 and F-16 design debates.

### Cited Findings
- Shaw defines "kinetic energy" as the energy of speed and altitude as "potential energy," since altitude "can be readily transformed into kinetic energy." To compare two fighters he removes weight to get "specific energy" Es, where H is altitude above a reference (ft), V is true airspeed (ft/sec), and g is 32.2 ft/sec^2. The formula itself is an image that did not survive text extraction; the standard form is Es = H + V^2/(2g), in feet. [VERIFIED for definitions: Shaw, Fighter Combat: Tactics and Maneuvering (Naval Institute Press, 1985), Appendix, pp. 393-395, full text read from https://falcon.blu3wolf.com/Docs/Fighter%20Combat-Tactics%20and%20Maneuvering.pdf] [The formula form Es = h + V^2/(2g) is the task brief's own statement and matches Shaw's variable list; REPORTED/standard]
- Shaw: "many combinations of speed and altitude will yield the same specific energy"; lines of constant Es on an altitude-velocity grid describe speed-altitude combinations of equal energy state; an "ideal zoom" trades kinetic and potential energy back and forth while total energy stays constant. His worked example: a powerless aircraft in a vacuum that bled speed all the way to zero would top out at about 50,000 ft. [VERIFIED: Shaw, Appendix p. 394]
- Shaw: the rate of change of Es is specific excess power Ps = V(T - D)/W, with T total engine thrust (lbs), D total drag (lbs), W weight (lbs), V true airspeed (ft/sec). If thrust exceeds drag Ps is positive and energy increases (climb or acceleration); if drag exceeds thrust energy decreases. "Energy maneuverability" is the ability to change energy state, i.e. to climb and/or accelerate. [VERIFIED: Shaw, Appendix pp. 394-395]
- Ps = V(T - D)/W is stated identically in Wikipedia, which adds that Ps "is directly proportional to the potential climb rate (or sink rate)." [REPORTED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy%E2%80%93maneuverability_theory]
- Shaw's two-fighter example: two otherwise identical fighters begin zooms at the same Es; the lighter one has greater Ps, adds more energy during the zoom, and zooms higher. The energy gained or lost in a zoom depends on average Ps over its duration. [VERIFIED: Shaw, Appendix p. 395]
- Ps = 0 contour: described as the boundary of sustained maneuver ("the hardest you can turn indefinitely without slowing down or descending"). [REPORTED: https://migflug.com/afterburner/john-boyd-energy-maneuverability-theory-explained/]
- Authors and report: Boyd, Christie and 1st Lt James E. Gibson, "Energy-Maneuverability," Air Proving Ground Center (Eglin AFB) report APGC-TR-66-4. [REPORTED: Wikipedia citation, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy%E2%80%93maneuverability_theory; migflug page gives March 1966 and notes Christie and Gibson are routinely omitted from popular accounts]
- Date ambiguity: Wikipedia says Boyd and Christie "completed a two-volume report on their studies in 1964" but cites a 1966 report; the migflug page says Boyd was ready to release findings through briefings followed by a report in May 1964 and gives March 1966 for the APGC-TR-66-4 publication. [AMBIGUOUS: both from the same two secondary sources above; I could not read the DTIC original]
- Purpose stated: "to compare the performance envelopes of U.S. and Soviet aircraft from the Korean and Vietnam Wars"; influenced F-15 and F-16 design. [REPORTED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy%E2%80%93maneuverability_theory]
- Example finding attributed to E-M: the F-4C could be out-turned by a MiG-21 but could gain energy more rapidly across most flight regimes, which dictated tactics (use energy, avoid a slow turning fight). [REPORTED: migflug page above]
- Pre-E-M metrics were maximum speed, maximum altitude and time to altitude, which "often had little to do with the success or failure of an aircraft in a combat setting"; E-M replaced them with isoplots of Ps on a speed-versus-turn-rate graph. [REPORTED: https://john-golan.blogspot.com/2015/08/aircraft-performance-part-4-energy.html (blog, not an authoritative source; it did not print the equations)]
- Unsanctioned computer time: legend says about $1 million of stolen computer time; a later analysis says investigators found only four hours. [REPORTED, contested: migflug page above; I did not trace the primary]
- Boyd's Aerial Attack Study: written 1960, classified Secret, declassified 1964 (revised 11 Aug 1964). [REPORTED: https://www.colonelboyd.com/understanding-war lists the 1964 date; migflug and everyspec search results give 1960/1964]
- Boyd's 1976 "New Conception for Air-to-Air Combat" (dated 4 August 1976) defines maneuverability as the "Ability to change altitude, airspeed, and direction in any combination," states its original purpose as reducing ambiguity about the relative importance of turn radius, turn rate and/or g, and plots maneuver diagrams (turn rate versus Mach for an F-4E at 10,000 ft, 30,000 ft, and at constant energy level 30,000 ft). It notes that manned simulators with ambiguous altitude and attitude cues inhibit vertical maneuvers and high overtake and "suggest emphasis on horizontal, slow speed fights." [VERIFIED: slides read from https://slightlyeastofnew.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/03/newconception.pdf, 2012 edited Keynote edition by Chet Richards and Chuck Spinney]

### Inferences
- ESTIMATED, worked energy conversion (ideal, no drag, no thrust): 1 knot = 1.688 ft/s. A fighter at 400 kt = 675 ft/s has kinetic specific energy V^2/(2g) = 675^2/64.4 = 455,625/64.4 = about 7,075 ft. Descending 10,000 ft adds 10,000 ft of Es: new Es = 17,075 ft, so V = sqrt(2 x 32.2 x 17,075) = sqrt(1,099,630) = about 1,049 ft/s = about 621 kt. Ten thousand feet of height is therefore worth about 221 kt on top of a 400 kt start, or 475 kt from a standing start (sqrt(644,000) = 802 ft/s). Real values are lower because drag acts on the dive.
- Because Es is a single scalar, "energy advantage" in E-M terms is a difference in Es (height-equivalent feet), and height is only one way to hold it. Two fighters with equal Es but different mixes of h and V are equal in energy but differ in what they can do next.

### Gaps
- I could not open the original Boyd, Christie and Gibson report or the 1960/1964 Aerial Attack Study (DTIC and everyspec blocked or timed out), so the 1964 versus 1966 dating is unresolved and the quoted equations are from Shaw and secondary sources. Coram's biography was not accessed.
- No source read gave Ps values for any real aircraft that I can quote; I did not find a citable table.

## 2. Boyd's later ideas (OODA loop, Patterns of Conflict) on tempo and initiative, and the critiques

### Takeaway
Boyd generalized from air combat to the claim that the side that changes the situation faster than the opponent can comprehend ("operating inside the OODA loop") generates confusion and disorder and collapses the opponent's cohesion. Critics say the popular "faster loop" reading is a misreading, that real organizations have no sequential loop, and that faster tempo can even favor the slower side; Boyd's defenders (Osinga, Richards) say the loop and the theory are richer than "rapid cycling."

### Cited Findings
- Boyd's 1976 "New Conception" states the generalization: "in order to win or gain superiority, we should operate at a faster tempo than our adversaries or inside our adversaries' time scales," because such activity makes us appear ambiguous and generates confusion and disorder (he cites Godel's proof, the Heisenberg principle and the second law of thermodynamics). The "Message" slide: "He who can handle the quickest rate of change survives." Recipe includes "Rapid energy gain and rapid energy loss coupled with high turn rates and low turn radii," supercruise, quick-scan sensors, suppressed signatures, quick-shoot weapons. [VERIFIED: https://slightlyeastofnew.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/03/newconception.pdf, slides 19-24]
- Boyd's slide on the energy-maneuverability evidence: "Both lower turn radii/higher turn rates at higher negative energy rates, coupled with higher turn rates/lower turn radii at positive energy rates, seem to provide advantages when maneuvering the vertical plane"; the "Warp II" generalization asks for a fighter that can both lose and gain energy quickly while out-turning an adversary, "a fighter that can be used to initiate and control engagement opportunities." [VERIFIED: same slides 17-18]
- Patterns of Conflict (1986) is where Boyd defines operating inside the loop: "Observe, orient, decide and act more inconspicuously, more quickly, and with more irregularity," about 132 charts in; and elsewhere "The ability to operate at a faster tempo or rhythm than an adversary enables one to fold adversary back inside himself so that he can neither appreciate nor keep-up with what's going on." Culminating aim: "Generate uncertainty, confusion, disorder, panic, chaos ... to shatter cohesion, produce paralysis and bring about collapse" (Boyd 1986, p. 132). [REPORTED, quoted in Chet Richards, "Boyd's OODA Loop (It's Not What You Think)", 7 May 2018, https://slightlyeastofnew.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/boyds-real-ooda-loopx.pdf; Richards is a Boyd associate and editor, so he is an advocate]
- Richards notes Boyd rarely wrote "OODA loop" alone but virtually always "operating inside opponents' OODA loops," which Boyd never defined cleanly, and that Boyd himself distinguished "faster tempo" from "getting inside the loop" (1986, p. 5). Richards gives two cases (deception is one) where faster tempo does not produce the effect Boyd suggests and instead helps the slower side. [REPORTED: same Richards paper]
- Critique quoted from British officer Jim Storr via Osinga (2005): "The OODA process is not circular ... Observation, orientation and action are continuous processes, and decisions are made occasionally in consequences of them. There is no OODA loop. The idea of getting inside the enemy decision cycle is deeply flawed." [REPORTED: second-hand through Richards' paper above]
- Osinga's thesis, quoted: "Boyd's OODA loop concept as well as his entire work are more comprehensive, deeper and richer than the popular notion of 'rapid OODA looping' his work is generally equated with." Osinga, Science, Strategy and War: The Strategic Theory of John Boyd (Routledge, 2007). [REPORTED via Richards (p. 10 of Osinga as cited); Osinga's book itself not read. Leiden repository copy exists: https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:4211794/view]
- A search-result summary (not a primary) states Hammond's The Mind of War emphasizes Orientation as the schwerpunkt, not speed; and that when Boyd spoke of rapid tempo he often meant rapid changes in tempo. [REPORTED, low quality: search snippet, goodreads and blog hits; not verified in Hammond]
- CSBA observes that Hartmann's "See-Decide-Attack-Break" procedure is "strikingly similar" to Boyd's OODA loop. [VERIFIED: Stillion, Trends in Air-to-Air Combat, CSBA 2015, p. 6 footnote 9, https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/190269/Air-to-Air-Report-.pdf]

### Inferences
- The air-combat-specific core (energy state, transient capability, ambiguity inducing surprise) is the part of Boyd with direct quantitative support (E-M); the tempo-to-collapse claim for organizations rests on analogy and is where the critiques concentrate.

### Gaps
- Did not read Patterns of Conflict, Osinga's chapters, Hammond or Coram directly; the critiques above are via Richards' paper and search summaries. No quantitative test of "tempo advantage" found.

## 3. Dicta Boelcke (1916), Mannock, and Malan: exact wording on height and surprise

### Takeaway
Boelcke's dicta stress securing advantage and keeping the sun behind you rather than naming altitude outright (in the translation I could read); Mannock's rule is explicit about height ("always above"); Malan's rule is explicit that height gives the initiative and that a proportion of a diving formation must stay above as top guard.

### Cited Findings
- Dicta Boelcke, translation attributed to Professor Johannes von Werner (Wikipedia version 1), eight rules, completed June 1916 and distributed through the German Air Service: (1) "Try to secure advantages before attacking. If possible, keep the sun behind you." (2) "Always carry through an attack when you have started it." (3) "Fire only at close range, and only when your opponent is properly in your sights." (4) "Always keep your eye on your opponent, and never let yourself be deceived by ruses." (5) "In any form of attack it is essential to assail your enemy from behind." (6) "If your opponent dives on you, do not try to evade his onslaught, but fly to meet it." (7) "When over the enemy's lines never forget your own line of retreat." (8) "For the Staffel (squadron): Attack on principle in groups of four or six. When the fight breaks up into a series of single combats, take care that several do not go for the same opponent." [REPORTED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dicta_Boelcke; same text on https://www.theaerodrome.com/forum/showthread.php?t=9619 (search result)]
- Versions differ: Wikipedia states "There are various versions of the Dicta" and prints a second version with expanded language on sun, surprise and squadron coordination; I did not capture its exact text. The first-version wording contains no explicit word "height" or "altitude." [AMBIGUOUS: multiple translations; exact wording of the alternative not read]
- CSBA summarizes the advantages sought by Mannock, Boelcke and other WWI pilots as: "Greater altitude, which can be converted into speed to attack or used to avoid combat with more numerous opponents at lower altitude; approaching from 'up sun' to delay or deny detection; approaching from known 'blind spots' of a defender (e.g., behind and below a two-seat aircraft); and opening fire at short range to maximize hits while the defender is still suffering from surprise." [VERIFIED: CSBA 2015, p. 6]
- CSBA quotes Mannock's main tactical principle: "The enemy must be surprised and attacked at a disadvantage, if possible with superior numbers so the initiative was with the patrol.... The combat must continue until the enemy has admitted his inferiority, by being shot down or running away" (via Lt. Col. T. G. Bradbeer, "Always above: Major Edward 'Mick' Mannock in World War I," 2006). [VERIFIED as quoted in CSBA p. 5 and n. 8; Bradbeer original not read (the freelibrary copy returned 403)]
- Mannock: "Gentlemen, always above; seldom on the same level; never underneath" became the No. 74 Squadron mantra; his rules include "Scouts must be attacked from above and two-seaters from beneath their tails," "Utilize the sun's glare and clouds to achieve surprise," and approaching from the east (German side). Rules first issued June 1917 per a search summary. [REPORTED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mick_Mannock (the article does not cite an original document for the rules); date from search summary of aviationquotations/other, not verified]
- Malan's "Ten of My Rules for Air Fighting," exact wording as printed on Wikipedia (citing Philip Kaplan, Fighter Aces of the RAF in the Battle of Britain, 2008, ch. 3, p. 109): 1 "Wait until you see the whites of his eyes. Fire short bursts of 1 to 2 seconds and only when your sights are definitely 'ON'." 2 "Whilst shooting think of nothing else, brace the whole of the body, have both hands on the stick, concentrate on your ring sight." 3 "Always keep a sharp lookout. 'Keep your finger out!'" 4 "Height gives You the initiative." 5 "Always turn and face the attack." 6 "Make your decisions promptly. It is better to act quickly even though your tactics are not the best." 7 "Never fly straight and level for more than 30 seconds in the combat area." 8 "When diving to attack always leave a proportion of your formation above to act as top guard." 9 "INITIATIVE, AGGRESSION, AIR DISCIPLINE, and TEAM WORK are the words that MEAN something in Air Fighting." 10 "Go in quickly - Punch hard - Get out!" [REPORTED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Rules_for_Air_Fighting]
- Numbering ambiguity: a search-result summary of the same Wikipedia page numbered "Height gives You the initiative" as rule five; the fetched list above numbers it four. Malan's list is reprinted in several versions. [AMBIGUOUS: I did not see the original RAF Fighter Command card]
- "Height, sun, surprise" as a fixed doctrinal triad: I searched for it and found no primary source or originator. The three elements individually appear in Boelcke (sun), Mannock (height, sun, surprise), and Malan (height). [Not found as a citable phrase; treat as folklore shorthand until sourced]
- Hartmann's own version: "detecting the enemy first, achieving a tactical advantage, attacking from close range ... and escaping to assess the attack"; Shaw quotes Hartmann: "There are no eyes for your backside, no eyes for who is coming from below ... if you are single you have too many blind spots." [VERIFIED as quoted: CSBA p. 6; Shaw, Fighter Combat, p. 196]

### Inferences
- Taken together, the classical rules treat height as a means (to speed, to initiative, to choose engagement and to retreat) rather than an end, and pair it with concealment (sun, clouds, blind spots) and with a reserve left above (Malan 8).

### Gaps
- Exact wording of the second Dicta version and of Malan's original issue (date and publisher) not obtained; Mannock's original rules text not read in a primary source.

## 4. Data: kills against pilots unaware of the attacker (original sources, folklore flagged)

### Takeaway
The "80 to 90 percent never saw the attacker" figure traces to three distinct kinds of source: WWII ace recollection (Barkhorn 80-90 percent of his own victories; Hubbard's 1944 "90%", which a forum participant calls a figure "picked for effect"), and a USAF analysis of Vietnam air combat (80 or 81 percent of US losses "unaware or aware too late"). The Vietnam figure is the only one backed by a data set, and I could not read that data set directly. The figures measure different things and should not be merged.

### Cited Findings
- CSBA (Stillion, 2015): "More modern detailed analysis of 112 air combat engagements during the Vietnam War conducted by the U.S. Air Force (USAF) in the 1970s concluded that 80 percent of aircrew shot down were unaware of the impending attack." No footnote names the study. Elsewhere: "Many of the great aces of World War II, including Gerd Barkhorn, estimated that 80-90 percent of their victims did not realize they were under attack until after being hit. These estimates were validated by extensive USAF analysis of aerial combat during the Vietnam War." [VERIFIED as CSBA's statement: Stillion, Trends in Air-to-Air Combat: Implications for Future Air Superiority, CSBA, 2015, p. 7 and the report's concluding section (page number not captured), https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/190269/Air-to-Air-Report-.pdf]
- CSBA on Barkhorn: in postwar interviews he "characterized maneuvering combat as a high-risk, low-payoff activity and estimated that between 80 and 90 percent of his victories were against unsuspecting targets." This is a personal estimate, not a count. [VERIFIED as CSBA's statement: p. 6]
- CSBA's own air-victory database (1,467 confirmed victories 1965-2013, from the Air Combat Information Group, cross-checked with Project Red Baron accounts of US victories and losses in Vietnam) is a victory-type database; it does not itself tabulate whether victims saw the attacker. [VERIFIED: CSBA, "The Missile-Era Aerial Victory Database" section, page number not captured]
- Project Red Baron (the Weapons Systems Evaluation Group study of Southeast Asia air-to-air encounters, commissioned through DDR&E) is the likely origin of the "81 percent" Vietnam figure. A secondary article states: "Postwar analysis showed that 81 percent of all U.S. aircraft lost in combat were either unaware of an attack or became aware too late to defend themselves," without naming report or author beyond "the Air Force's 'Red Baron' study, which was written immediately after the war." [REPORTED: William A. Sayers, HistoryNet, https://historynet.com/great-kill-ratio-debate/]
- A blog that quotes the Red Baron report says: "The enemy, when not detected until he positioned himself in the rear quadrant usually succeeded in making the first attack," with first-attack tallies of 37 to 2 for MiG-17s and 9 to 5 for MiG-21s under rear-quadrant conditions; the first indication of the enemy was often "an enemy tracer fire, missile smoke trail, or a wingman in distress"; the median initial acquisition distance for F-4 and F-8 aircraft was about 3 miles; and the report identified four correlating factors (hostile aircraft type, acquisition quadrant, relative altitude, acquisition range), with altitude and range having smaller effects than quadrant. [REPORTED: http://elementsofpower.blogspot.com/2014/02/air-to-air-combat-over-southeast-asia.html; blog quoting Red Baron, quotes not checked against the report]
- A search-result summary attributes to Marshall Michel, Clashes: Air Combat over North Vietnam 1965-1972, that 62 of the VPAF's 78 confirmed kills came from fights where the winner initiated from a position of nearly unbeatable advantage. I did not open Michel. [REPORTED, unverified: search snippet only; flag as unconfirmed]
- Hubbard figure: a forum thread quotes Lt. Col. Mark E. Hubbard (P-38, 20th Fighter Group) in "The Long Reach: Deep Fighter Escort Tactics," VIII Fighter Command, 29 May 1944: "90% of all fighters shot down never saw the guy who hit them." A participant remarks the number was "more of a figure picked for effect than the exact result of diligent operations research." [REPORTED: https://theaerodrome.com/forum/showthread.php?t=76427; the existence of the 1944 booklet and Hubbard as a contributor is confirmed by search results at https://bookshop.org/books/the-long-reach-deep-fighter-escort-tactics-viii-fighter-command-29-may-1944/9781481159326 and the Kurfurst board listing, but I could not read the quote in the booklet]
- Hartmann: "sure that 80 percent of kills never knew he was there before he opened fire" and Zemke "few pilots are shot down by enemies they see." [REPORTED: search-result summary only; originating work not identified]
- Contrary figure: the same forum thread reports a questionnaire (~210 replies from American Fighter Aces Association members, WWI to Vietnam, attributed to "Barrett") finding roughly 20 percent of aerial kills involved surprise attacks. [AMBIGUOUS/unverified: forum summary only; I could not identify the survey or author (Barrett Tillman is a former AFAA executive secretary, which is a guess, not a finding), so this is weak evidence against the 80-90 percent claims]
- Definitions differ across these figures: "unaware of attack" (CSBA, Vietnam), "never saw the attacker" (Hubbard), "did not realize they were under attack until hit" (Barkhorn estimate), and "unaware or aware too late" (the 81 percent). [ESTIMATED reading: my comparison of the wording above; the figures are not interchangeable]

### Inferences
- Folklore flags: Hubbard's 90 percent and Hartmann's 80 percent are anecdotal estimates by participants, one acknowledged as rhetorical. The only dataset-based figure is the Vietnam 80/81 percent from Red Baron-era USAF analysis, an environment of GCI-vectored MiGs attacking from the rear quadrant, with 1960s radar-less US lookout, so it may not transfer to other eras.
- A 20 percent survey figure and an 80-90 percent figure cannot both be right for the same population; sample (survivors versus the shot-down, aces versus all) is the likely difference but I have no source that resolves it.

### Gaps
- Could not read Red Baron I, II or III or the underlying 112-engagement analysis (DTIC ADC003627 and the Torch/AETC fact sheet returned 403 or DNS errors). The statement "this is the 112-engagement study" is my inference only; CSBA does not say so.
- No WWII operational-research study (RAF or USAAF) giving a counted fraction was found.

## 5. Energy fighting versus angles fighting, value of altitude, and the dive-climb asymmetry

### Takeaway
Shaw frames two ways to fight a similar opponent: an angles fight that seeks position first at the expense of energy, and an energy fight that seeks an energy margin first and converts it to a lethal position later. Altitude is the limited stock of potential energy; it lets the holder trade it for speed, choose engagement or disengagement, and force the lower opponent to come up to him.

### Cited Findings
- Shaw: "two basic approaches are available: the 'angles' fight and the 'energy' fight. ... In the angles fight the tactician first seeks to gain a position advantage (angles), even at the expense of relative energy ... The purpose of the energy fight is to gain an energy advantage over the opponent while not yielding a decisive position advantage. Once a sufficient energy advantage has been attained, it must be converted to a lethal position advantage, usually without surrendering the entire energy margin." [VERIFIED: Shaw, ch. 3 intro, p. 99]
- Shaw on the cost of angles fighting: an angles fighter that pulls hard "normally will lose energy in the process. Since potential energy (altitude) is limited, this energy loss eventually will mean loss of speed. If the angles fighter becomes too slow, its maneuverability suffers." [VERIFIED: Shaw, p. 100]
- Shaw on energy fighting: "building an energy advantage and then converting that energy to a snapshot position"; an energy advantage "may be used to generate vertical separation"; the rolling scissors "result in multiple firing opportunities and disengagement opportunities while enabling the energy fighter to remain on the offensive throughout"; against a similar adversary it "may be difficult for the energy tactician to gain enough energy margin ... to avoid being menaced at the top of a zoom climb." [VERIFIED: Shaw, pp. 104-105]
- Shaw on disengagement: "If the defender has an energy advantage, he may be able to pull up at the overshoot and gain separation in the vertical." [VERIFIED: Shaw, ch. 2]
- Shaw on how much height is enough: "an energy fighter must have a substantial altitude advantage over its opponent immediately preceding an effective high-side or overhead gun pass ... in general the altitude advantage should be about equivalent to the minimum instantaneous turn radius of the energy fighter," with the example that a 2,000 ft minimum horizontal turn radius needs about a 2,000 ft altitude advantage. [VERIFIED: Shaw, ch. 4 "dissimilar aircraft," p. 150]
- Shaw on being the lower fighter against a higher-energy opponent: "The general rule here is to work below the bogey, make it come down to engage, and try to confine the fight to the horizontal plane." He adds that a higher-energy opponent with an altitude advantage "can be either faster or slower than the defender, and it may not be readily apparent which is the case." [VERIFIED: Shaw, pp. 136-137]
- Boom and zoom as defined in secondary summary: "a powered dive toward the rear quarter of a lower flying opponent" with a gun pass and "a zoom climb back to a safe altitude, restoring the potential energy"; an effective tactic "against a more maneuverable opponent, where the turning battle of a dogfight is best avoided." Wikipedia also frames energy fighters as those with high thrust-to-weight and Ps who "use climbs and dives to its advantage." [REPORTED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_fighter_maneuvers]
- CSBA: Bong and McGuire perfected "Boom and Zoom" tactics in the South Pacific in parallel with German "ambush tactics" by Hartmann and Barkhorn; common elements were "attacking unsuspecting targets from a position of advantage, usually from above, and avoiding maneuvering combat unless absolutely necessary." Sustained focus on a maneuvering target degrades the attacker's own awareness: "The longer a maneuvering fight lasts, the greater the probability the attacker will be attacked in turn by one of the defender's unseen friends." [VERIFIED: CSBA pp. 5-6]
- Chennault's AVG doctrine (P-40 versus Zero): never turn with a Zero; gain altitude, dive through the formation, fire a burst and keep diving, then climb and re-engage; the P-40 was heavier and dived faster. [REPORTED: search-result summaries of migflug and historynet pages; Chennault's own text not read]
- Shaw's turn performance appendix includes "Instantaneous Turn Performance Variation with Altitude" (Fig. A-4) and notes that an angles fighter must "accelerate to corner speed" to start; detailed values not extracted. [VERIFIED existence: Shaw, p. 393]
- Mixed-altitude fights have a vertical-plane aspect: Boyd's 1976 slides say real-world cases (F-100 vs F-86H, F-105 vs MiG-17 in 1965, YF-16/17 vs F-4E) show fighters "losing energy but gaining position" at the end game, then "gaining energy for new set-up." [VERIFIED: New Conception slide 14]
- Altitude also adds to missile range: CSBA gives first-generation IR missile maximum range as about 2.3 nm at high altitudes but less than 1 nm at low altitudes. [VERIFIED: CSBA, Figure 6 discussion, about p. 13]

### Inferences
- ESTIMATED dive-versus-climb asymmetry, illustrative numbers only. Climbing adds energy at the rate Ps (ft of Es per second), so regaining 10,000 ft of height takes 10,000/Ps seconds; with an assumed Ps = 100 ft/s (my assumption, not a sourced figure) that is 100 s. Descending spends height at the rate V x sin(dive angle): at 400 kt (675 ft/s) and a 30 degree dive, 675 x 0.5 = about 338 ft/s, so 10,000 ft goes in about 30 s (ignoring drag and acceleration). The ratio is about 3.4 to 1 for these inputs. The qualitative statement is sourced: Shaw says altitude is limited and the angles fighter's loss of energy is irreversible within the fight; Boyd's Ps is the only way to buy energy back, and zooms trade speed for height without adding energy (Shaw's "ideal zoom").
- A second asymmetry that follows from Shaw p. 150: height only converts to useful offensive position if it exceeds roughly the energy fighter's minimum turn radius at the pass, so a small height edge is worth much less than the stored-speed arithmetic above suggests.

### Gaps
- No quantitative dive and climb time-cost table for a named aircraft was found; the numbers above are arithmetic on assumed inputs. Shaw's chapter text on specific altitude thresholds for energy tactics beyond the turn-radius rule was not exhaustively read.

## 6. Formation doctrine using altitude layers: top cover, stacking, fluid four, loose deuce, Thach weave

### Takeaway
Doctrine uses height as a division of labor: one element or layer watches and covers, another attacks; the high element supplies the stored energy and the first look, the low element supplies the bait or the target, and the pair or four mutually cover blind spots. Large vertical splits trade offensive flexibility for defensive support.

### Cited Findings
- Malan rule 8: "When diving to attack always leave a proportion of your formation above to act as top guard." [REPORTED: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Rules_for_Air_Fighting]
- Shaw on why pairs exist: single-seat fighters have blind spots (underside, rear hemisphere) "that cannot be monitored visually on a continuous basis"; two fighters let "each of the two pilots ... cover the blind zone of the other" and let one pilot "prosecute an attack confident in the knowledge that his vulnerable areas are being protected by his wingman." [VERIFIED: Shaw, ch. 5, pp. 195-196]
- Shaw on vertical stacking within a section: optimum vertical separation depends on environment, performance and structure; the wingman steps up when on the leader's down-sun side and flies below when up-sun, so the leader is not looking into the sun. "Large vertical separations between fighters can reduce defensive capability, since the lower aircraft may not be able to climb up quickly to the level of the wingman to render assistance without losing so much airspeed that it becomes ineffective. Higher performance fighters can, therefore, afford larger altitude splits." Larger splits "both horizontally and vertically, provide better offensive potential" partly because "enemy fighters are less likely to see both aircraft simultaneously," but at "reduced defensive capability"; prudent sections use smaller splits in high-threat areas. [VERIFIED: Shaw, pp. 202-203]
- Shaw on combat spread in an AAM environment: typical separation "on the order of one to two miles"; higher altitudes and slower speeds require greater separation to deny a bogey a missile shot within the pair's danger zone; spacing limited by the need to see each other (bogey size is often the binding limit). [VERIFIED: Shaw, ch. 5, p. 202]
- Thach weave: devised by Lt Cdr John S. Thach in 1941 ("beam defense position") using matchsticks; two fighters in wide line abreast (about 1,000 ft for the F4F in Shaw's account) turn toward each other when one is attacked, so the free wingman gets a head-on shot at the attacker; Shaw calls the weave effective for Wildcats against Zeros because of firepower and durability, calls it a forerunner of "loose deuce," and says the secret is "initial wide line-abreast formation (separation greater than fighter turn radius) and heavy gun firepower." It is a lateral mutual support geometry; the altitude layers are not part of the weave. First combat use at Midway, June 1942. Vejtasa criticized its effectiveness against experienced attackers; O'Hare tested it in exercises; Sakai documented Japanese confusion. [VERIFIED: Shaw, ch. 5, pp. 211-212; REPORTED for history and observers: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thach_Weave]
- Loose deuce: a Navy doctrine developed in Vietnam, a variation of double attack, with an "engaged fighter" and a "free fighter" who keeps visual lookout and covers; Shaw gives a basketball analogy (ball handler and offensive-rebound man). [VERIFIED: Shaw, ch. 5 "Loose Deuce"]
- Luftwaffe four (Schwarm, finger-four): Mölders is credited with developing the Rotte (pair) and Schwarm; one source describes positions as "The second pair would fly behind the leader of the first pair, stepped up away from the sun. The leader's wingman would fly behind and low"; the leader looks ahead and the wingmen watch the rear. Wikipedia: the finger-four emerged independently, Finnish AF adopted it in 1934-35, the Luftwaffe used it first in combat (Spain, 1938); a stated weakness is that position four is "particularly vulnerable to a surprise diving attack"; a 16 to 1 Finnish kill ratio against Soviet vics is claimed there. [REPORTED: https://dingeraviation.net/bf109/bf109tactics.html; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finger-four (the 16:1 figure is unverified in my reading)]
- RAF three-ship "Vic" had a rear "Tail-end Charlie" weaver (also "Ass-End Charlie") who weaved behind the section and was often the first lost; the RAF later copied the finger-four. [REPORTED: search-result summary of Battle of Britain pages, e.g. https://warfarehistorynetwork.com/article/the-battle-of-britain-leadership-and-logistics-in-britains-skies/; exact page text not read]
- Escort geometry in Shaw (modern): "Altitude for the forward escort is typically close to that of the strike force, maybe a little higher to give quicker response to short-range gun or rocket attacks, or possibly a little lower to provide look-up for better radar detection." "Rear-element altitude is typically somewhat higher than that of the strike group to provide greater potential energy for quick reaction." Trailing "rear guard" or "top cover" elements are added because trailing aircraft are most vulnerable. [VERIFIED: Shaw, ch. 9 "Fighter Missions," pp. 340-341]
- Galland on escort doctrine: "extended protection" was a compromise between free chase and close escort. [VERIFIED as quoted in Shaw, p. 340]
- RAF 1940 division of labor: preferred tactic was Hurricanes against bomber formations and Spitfires against the escorts (implicitly a higher-performance high layer against fighters). [REPORTED: search summary of warfarehistorynetwork; not verified]

### Inferences
- What each layer provides: the high layer provides first look and stored energy (can dive on whatever attacks the low layer, Malan 8); the low layer provides bait, ground-clutter-free look-up for radar, and a target set that the high layer can see against the sky (CSBA notes blind spots are "behind and below"); the cover requires proximity (Shaw 202-203) so there is a limit to how large the vertical split can be.

### Gaps
- Did not find a primary description of USAAF or RAF four-layer or multi-altitude "stack" formations (e.g., Battle of Britain wing "stepped" layers) with exact separations; the NHHC article on Navy fighter tactics (ww2-25.pdf) failed on a certificate error. No source found for five-layer doctrine; no source found quantifying the share of encounters in which a top-cover element changed the outcome.

## 7. BVR doctrine: first look, first shot, first kill; sensor range versus weapon range; modelling detection asymmetry

### Takeaway
"First look, first shot, first kill" is the modern restatement of the ambush principle: win the detection contest, shoot first from outside the opponent's detection or weapon range, and kill before reaction. Sources give concrete detection ranges (visual about 2 nm, early fighter radars about 15 nm, 1990s radars 40 nm or more, AWACS about 225 nm against low flyers), and radar detection range depends on altitude through the radar horizon and on target signature through the fourth root of radar cross section.

### Cited Findings
- CSBA: surprise "is so important to success in air combat that it is assumed in the modern USAF air combat mantra of 'First Look, First Shot, First Kill.'" Other CSBA statements: "the fundamental goal of air combat has remained constant: leverage superior SA to sneak into firing position, destroy the opposing aircraft, and depart before other enemy aircraft can react." [VERIFIED: CSBA 2015, p. 7]
- RAND (Stillion and Perdue, "Air Combat Past, Present and Future," Aug 2008) slide 4: the concept for air superiority requires "First View, First Shot, First Kill," superior situational awareness (stealth and sensors), minimal vulnerability (stealth), and lethal BVR missiles. [VERIFIED: https://www.aereo.jor.br/wp-content/uploads//2016/02/2008_RAND_Pacific_View_Air_Combat_Briefing.pdf]
- Origin of the phrase: a search summary says the modern form emerged with the F-22 program in the 1980s and appears in AFDD 2-1.1 Counterair Operations. [REPORTED, low confidence: search snippet; not traced to a primary]
- Visual detection model: CSBA, using Schallhorn et al., Visual Search in Air Combat, finds that a pilot searching a 90 degree by 20 degree sector has per-fixation probability of an in-central-vision hit of 1/450 at 7 nm, 1/110 at 3 nm, about 1/5 at 2 nm; cumulative detection probability with 20 fixations per minute stays below 0.50 until the target is between 1.9 and 2.8 nm; CSBA therefore uses a 2 nm circle as the "effective uncued visual search radius." The model assumes only about 20 percent of the pilot's time goes to disciplined visual search. [VERIFIED: CSBA pp. 12-13 and n. 17]
- WWII closure arithmetic: visual detection range for a WWII fighter approaching head-on about 1.5 nm; cruise about 240 kt; effective gun range about 200 m; an attacker from behind crosses detection-to-weapon range at relative speed 140 kt in about 35 s; a Me-262 would do it in 21 s (40 percent less time for the victim to react). [VERIFIED: CSBA pp. 32-33 (speed chapter)] [ESTIMATED check: (1.5 nm - 0.108 nm) / 140 kt = 1.392/140 hr = 0.00994 hr = 35.8 s, which matches CSBA's 35 s]
- Early missile era: first-generation IR missiles were usable in a roughly 30 degree rear cone from about 2,500 ft minimum range to about 2.3 nm maximum at high altitude (under 1 nm at low altitude); effective gun range grew from 150 ft to about 1,500 ft over the fifty years before AAMs; early radars detected targets at about 15 nm in a roughly 110 degree frontal cone, with weapons launchable from about half that; by 1991 fighter radars detected at 40 nm or more, and look-down pulse-Doppler eliminated the "low-altitude sanctuary." [VERIFIED: CSBA executive summary p. iii, Figure 6 discussion about p. 13, speed/maneuverability chapter, and the "1990s" section; exact pages not captured]
- AWACS: in the 1991 Gulf War, E-3s detected aircraft flying at low altitude at about 225 nm; higher-flying aircraft were detectable further away; E-3 crews could track aircraft at or above 5,000 ft virtually anywhere over Iraq. [VERIFIED: CSBA, Gulf War section]
- Gulf War engagement data cited by CSBA: AWACS detected and identified enemy aircraft over 70 nm from US fighters on average; US pilots detected on their own radars at 42 nm; 16 of 33 fixed-wing engagements occurred BVR (48 percent); only 15 percent of all engagements and 38 percent of visual range engagements involved ACM. [VERIFIED: CSBA, table of 1991 Gulf War engagement characteristics; page not captured]
- Heading into Vietnam: about 75 percent of US aerial victories in Vietnam were achieved with missiles. [VERIFIED: CSBA, concluding section; page not captured]
- RAND 2008 on counter-stealth: Flanker IRST claimed capable of tracking typical fighter head-on at 50 km (27 nm) and tail-on at 90 km (50 nm); a supercruising fighter's shock cone could raise detection about 10 percent to 30 nm head-on; an AMRAAM launch signature could allow detection of the launcher at 50+ nm. A caveat on AIM-120 results: of ten US AIM-120 kills at the time, four were not BVR, 13 missiles were fired for six BVR kills (Pk = 0.46), and victims were fleeing, lacked radar or ECM, or were not expecting attack. [VERIFIED as stated on slides: RAND briefing slides 37, 38 and 22]
- Radar horizon: maximum radar line-of-sight range in nautical miles is 1.23 x (sqrt(antenna height ft) + sqrt(target height ft)). [REPORTED: Furuno/Boston Whaler-type references from search results, e.g. https://www.psicompany.com/man-prod-info/furuno/prodweb/radar/radarhorizon.pdf, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_horizon]
- Weapon engagement zone: defined by minimum range (to arm), maximum range (above which a maneuvering target will most likely evade), and a no-escape zone in between; it depends on "type of weapon, aircraft speed, relative altitudes, and geometry"; the shooter's velocity and altitude are "directly related to the energy that will be available to the missile," and launch altitude affects missile drag. [REPORTED: Dantas et al., "Weapon Engagement Zone Maximum Launch Range Estimation Using a Deep Neural Network," arXiv:2111.04474, quoting Portrey et al. and Birkmire; text read in full from https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.04474]
- Another summary says missile range improves about twofold between sea level and 20,000 ft and that stated ranges assume high-altitude launches. [REPORTED, low quality: search snippet of wionews; not verified]

### Inferences
- ESTIMATED radar horizon examples using the formula above: antenna and target both at 30,000 ft: 1.23 x (173.2 + 173.2) = about 426 nm; antenna at 30,000 ft, target at 100 ft: 1.23 x (173.2 + 10) = about 225 nm (this equals CSBA's E-3 figure for low flyers, which suggests the horizon rather than radar power was the limit there; this is my inference, not a sourced statement); antenna and target both at 100 ft: 1.23 x 20 = about 24.6 nm. So height acts on detection through the geometry of line of sight, and the benefit grows with the square root of height.
- ESTIMATED detection-range scaling with signature, from the standard radar range equation (not fetched in this session; Rmax proportional to (RCS)^(1/4) when everything else is fixed): reducing radar cross section by a factor of 10 cuts detection range to 10^(-1/4) = 0.562 (a 44 percent reduction); by a factor of 100, to 0.316 (a 68 percent reduction). This is the standard way detection asymmetry is modeled in analytic work; I did not find a citable source for the exponent in this session.

### Gaps
- Did not find a source that defines how detection asymmetry is modelled in a specific operational-analysis model (e.g., AFSIM, JTLS); the radar-equation and radar-horizon treatment above are the textbook ingredients, and the CSBA visual-search model is the only quantified human-detection model read. No source on Air Force doctrine documents (AFDD 2-1.1) was read directly (Air University Press and DTIC pages returned 403).

## 8. Formal models of three-dimensional engagement geometry

### Takeaway
The formal vocabulary of 3D engagement is a small set of geometric angles (aspect angle, antenna train angle, heading crossing angle, angle off tail), relative range and altitude difference, and an energy-state comparison (Es); weapon engagement zones tie them to altitude and speed. Search results show these used in AFTTP and recent autonomy papers; I could read little of them.

### Cited Findings
- Geometry definitions (search-result summary of AETC TTP 11-1 and a flyandwire page): range, heading crossing angle (HCA), aspect angle (angle from the target's tail to the attacker), planform, angle off tail (AOT), and antenna train angle (ATA, the angle from the nose of one aircraft to the other aircraft) describe the relative geometry; the pursuit curves are lead, pure and lag. [REPORTED: search results only; the AETC pdf and arXiv/MDPI pages were not readable, AETC TTP 11-1 URL https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/aetc/publication/aetcttp11-1/aetcttp11-1.pdf]
- Shaw treats the three-dimensional geometry of pursuit directly: nose-low turns gain "gravity assist," vertical separation gives the energy fighter a snapshot position, and the high-yo-yo and low-yo-yo use the vertical dimension to manage lag and overshoot. [VERIFIED: Shaw, ch. 2-3, e.g. pp. 71, 104-107]
- A recent reinforcement-learning paper defines 3D dogfight as orienting toward the opponent for a weapon lock modeled as a Weapon Engagement Zone: "the locus of points that lie within a spherical cone of 2 degree aperture ... that are also 500-3000 ft away." [REPORTED: search-result summary of https://www.mdpi.com/2226-4310/12/3/265 (403 on fetch)]
- Weapon engagement zone structure (Rmin, Rmax, no-escape zone) and its dependence on altitude and speed: see Section 7 (Dantas et al.). [REPORTED]
- Boyd's maneuver diagrams (turn rate versus Mach, with lines of constant g, turn radius and constant-Ps energy-rate contours) plus the "air battle arena" altitude-Mach diagram labelled with minimum sustained radius, maximum sustained turn rate, and maximum maneuver corridor, are the original formal 2D representation of altitude-dependent performance. [VERIFIED: New Conception slides 9-12]
- Research on approximate dynamic programming for air combat (MIT thesis by Roy) and differential-game formulations exist per search results; I could not fetch them, so no formulas are reported. [Not read]

### Inferences
- The 3D-specific formal content that I could verify is: energy state (Es) as a one-number summary of height plus speed, the geometry-angle set above, and a WEZ that depends on relative altitude; I found no source that gives a single closed-form "value of height" function.

### Gaps
- No readable formal model (reward or advantage function with explicit altitude term, differential game, Lanchester variant with altitude) was obtained. Candidates seen in search results but not read: Roy (MIT) ADP air combat thesis; MDPI Aerospace 12(3):265 on RL dogfight; Austin et al. and Burgin-type "adaptive maneuvering logic" literature (not searched directly).
