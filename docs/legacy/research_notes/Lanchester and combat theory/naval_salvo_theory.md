## 1. What are Hughes's salvo equations as published (Hughes 1995, Naval Research Logistics 42), what does every term mean, and what does the model say about instability, numbers versus quality, and striking first?

### Takeaway
The basic salvo model is a discrete-time, force-on-force exchange in which each side's losses (in ships put out of action) equal accurate missiles launched minus missiles destroyed by the target's counterfire, divided by hits needed to disable one ship. Hughes's published conclusions: instability arises when combat power grows relative to staying power, weak staying power is the root cause of instability, staying power is the attribute least dependent on battle particulars, and numerical superiority is the most consistently advantageous attribute. When unstable, delivering the first effective unanswered salvo dominates.

**Basic salvo equations as printed (Eq. 1)**
- Losses per salvo, in ships out of action: Delta_A = (beta*B - a3*A) / a1 ; Delta_B = (alpha*A - b3*B) / b1. — [Hughes 1995, Section III "The Model"](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED. (The OCR of the equation image is garbled; the form is confirmed by Hughes's own worked example below, and by [Kesler, Lucas and Sanchez 2019, Eq. 1](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc19papers/238.pdf) and [Wikipedia: Salvo combat model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvo_combat_model), which print ΔB = (αA − zB)/x and ΔA = (βB − yA)/w.)
- Bounds: if the defense is stronger than the attack the numerator is negative and the loss is zero, "not a negative"; losses cannot exceed the force (0 <= Delta <= force size). — [Hughes 1995, Eq. 2 discussion](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content); [Kesler et al. 2019](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc19papers/238.pdf). VERIFIED.

**Term definitions (Hughes 1995 notation, with Armstrong 2005 symbol in brackets)**
- A, B: number of units (ships or aircraft) in force A and force B. — [Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED.
- alpha, beta: number of well-aimed (accurate) missiles fired by each A unit (alpha) and each B unit (beta) per salvo; "striking power" or "fighting power" per unit. Force striking power is alpha*A or beta*B. VERIFIED. Appendix A: "FIGHTING POWER is FIREPOWER diminished for ACCURACY OF FIRE."
- a1, b1 [w, x]: staying power per unit, defined as the number of hits needed to put one unit out of action. a1 is hits by B's missiles needed to put one A out of action; b1 is hits by A's missiles needed to put one B out of action. Force staying power is a1*A or b1*B. VERIFIED. Hughes stresses a "firepower kill" or "combat kill" (unit rendered impotent for the engagement, not necessarily sunk): "the amount of ordnance required to sink a ship is on the order of 2-4 times as much as is required to achieve a firepower kill" (citing Humphrey and Hansen and Gray).
- a3, b3 [y, z]: number of well-aimed missiles destroyed by each A unit (a3) or each B unit (b3); "counterfire" or "defensive power" per unit. VERIFIED.
- Delta_A, Delta_B: units of A (B) put out of action by the opponent's salvo. In Armstrong's notation u = 1/w and v = 1/x are damage per hit (ships per hit). — [Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvo_combat_model). VERIFIED.
- Combat power P_A = beta*B - a3*A (hits per salvo on A), P_B = alpha*A - b3*B: the attacker's striking power minus hits eliminated by the target force's counteractions. Hughes: combat power "cannot be defined or measured except against a specific enemy FORCE." Combat work = combat power divided by staying power per ship, in ships out of action. VERIFIED.
- Counteraction or defensive power is a composite of counterfire (hard kill), seduction, evasion, and distraction. VERIFIED (Appendix A).

**Model assumptions stated by Hughes**
- Striking power is accurate missiles launched; shots are spread uniformly over all targets (uniform distribution "not necessarily the best"); counterfire eliminates good shots without leakers until the defense is saturated, after which all good shots hit (a "subtractive process"); hits degrade the target force linearly and in proportion to remaining hits it can take; weapon range sufficient on both sides ("neither side has a weapon range and scouting advantage such that it can detect, track, and target the other while standing safely outside the range of the enemy's weapons"). — [Hughes 1995, Model Assumptions 1-8](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED.
- Hughes calls the model "exploratory," not predictive. He argues a simulation of any complexity has "little or no predictive power because one does not know in advance what inputs to use," and that a model validates against history only "if one knows and applies inputs that were observed in the battles after the fact" (citing Beall 1990). VERIFIED.

**Measure of effectiveness: fractional exchange ratio (FER)**
- FER = (Delta_B / B) / (Delta_A / A): the fraction of B destroyed divided by the fraction of A destroyed after one salvo exchange between equal-cost forces. FER > 1: A has survivors when B is eliminated; FER < 1: B does. — [Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED.
- Hughes says FER must be used with caution under overkill (more combat work than enemy units) or negative combat power. VERIFIED.

**Fighting-strength inequality (Eq. 2)**
- Hughes: A wins a salvo exchange if a1*(alpha*A^2 - ... ) form holds. The OCR of Eq. 2 is garbled. What is legible: "If [A's term] > [B's term], then A wins a salvo exchange... These equations hold when the first term on both sides of the inequality sign is larger than the second term." Because Eq. 1 is linear in A and B, the per-salvo "fighting strength" comparison reduces to comparing the fractional losses. — [Hughes 1995, Eq. 2](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED for the statement; exact printed form of Eq. 2 not recoverable from the OCR (see Gaps).

**Hughes's worked example (instability and overkill)**
- Inputs: A = 2 ships, a1 = 2, a3 = 16, alpha = 24; B = 6 ships, b1 = 1, b3 = 1, beta = 6. Result: Delta_B/B = 7.0 (A's combat power could put all of B out of action "seven times over"), Delta_A/A = 1.0 (B's salvo still puts all of A out of action despite A's strong defense), FER = 7. Hughes: "In an exchange A's advantage in fighting strength is for naught." — [Hughes 1995, Discussion](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED.
- Arithmetic check: Delta_B = (24*2 - 1*6)/1 = 42, so Delta_B/B = 42/6 = 7. Delta_A = (6*6 - 16*2)/2 = 4/2 = 2, so Delta_A/A = 2/2 = 1. FER = 7/1 = 7. Reproduces Hughes's numbers. ESTIMATED (my arithmetic, matches the paper).
- Hughes states "not until it has 42 ships would force B have any survivors." — [Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED as a quotation. AMBIGUOUS as arithmetic: applying Eq. 1 as printed, Delta_B = (24*2 - 1*B)/1 = 48 - B, which is less than B (some B survives) once B > 24; at B = 24 Delta_B = 24 = B (exactly annihilated), at B = 25 Delta_B = 23. 42 = 6*7 follows from scaling B's size by the FER of 7 rather than re-solving Eq. 1 (Hughes frames it as "a force seven times greater in overall combat potential"). My re-solution gives a threshold of 25 ships, not 42. ESTIMATED; I did not find an erratum.

**What Hughes says about instability**
- Abstract finding 1: "Unstable circumstances arise as the combat power of the forces grows relative to their survivability. (Stable means the persistence of victory by the side with the greater combat potential.)" Finding 2: "Weak staying power is likely to be the root cause when instability is observed." — [Hughes 1995, abstract](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED.
- Section IV: the calculation "exhibits the property of instability when staying power is weak relative to combat power, that is to say, when the denominator of Eq. (1) is small compared to the numerator." If unit staying power cannot be added affordably, "force staying power can only be increased and stability restored by increasing the quantity of A or B with units that have affordable attributes." "The unstable circumstance of very strong combat power on both sides relative to their staying power argues under all circumstances in favor of delivering unanswered strikes. First effective attack is achieved by outscouting the enemy." VERIFIED.
- Conclusion 8 (Tactical Instability): the condition of instability is "characterized by opportunities for the inferior side to win or do proportionately more severe damage to the superior side in an exchange, or to defeat it with a surprise attack." Conclusion 7: fleets weak in staying power relative to combat power "are subject to destruction and defeat at the same time their ordnance is delivered. Such fleets must operate in a highly risk-averse mode, as if tactically paranoid." VERIFIED.
- Why guns did not have this property: "This was not possible with guns because the combat power of a battleship was small relative to its staying power. It becomes possible when the combat power of a warship (its striking power minus the defensive power of the target) is large relative to its staying power." VERIFIED.
- Hughes (from Fleet Tactics ch. 10, as summarized in the 1995 paper): in a "several-for-one" situation (one unit can disable several enemy units per salvo), "all classical concepts of force concentration are suspect," scouting is promoted in importance, "new tactics of dispersal and sequential engagement become attractive," while defensive power can reduce enemy striking power, so "concentration for defense makes sense tactically." VERIFIED (as Hughes's report of his own 1986 Chapter 10 conclusion).

**Numbers versus per-ship quality**
- Abstract finding 4: "Numerical superiority is the force attribute that is consistently most advantageous. For example, if A's unit striking power, staying power, and defensive power are all twice that of B, nevertheless B will achieve parity of outcome if it has twice as many units as A." — [Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED.
- Check of the parity claim: let B-units have (alpha0, d0, s0) for striking, defense, staying; A-units have (2*alpha0, 2*d0, 2*s0); B = 2A. Then Delta_B/B = (2*alpha0*A - d0*2A)/(s0*2A) = (alpha0 - d0)/s0. Delta_A/A = (alpha0*2A - 2*d0*A)/(2*s0*A) = (alpha0 - d0)/s0. Equal, so FER = 1. ESTIMATED (my algebra; confirmed numerically with alpha0=3, d0=1, s0=2, A=5: both fractions = 1.0).
- Hughes cautions that other linear trades do not preserve parity: "If one doubles the staying power of each A, for example, then one cannot expect to be able to reduce defensive power to half its former value and retain parity." VERIFIED.
- Conclusion 7 (Quantity and Quality): because more ships cost more and there are economies of scale and minimum feasible sizes, "an abstract preference for quantity may be impractical," but he lists three things to test, including that small warships capable of large salvos make unstable conditions much more likely than in the big-gun era or the era of carrier bombs and torpedoes. VERIFIED.
- Staying power: "Ship staying power is uniquely the ship design element least affected by the particulars of a battle, including poor tactics" (Conclusion 5). Whole-force staying power = unit staying power times number of units engaged. VERIFIED.

**Striking first, as stated in the model**
- The basic equations "do not provide for an advantage in scouting... so as to get off an unanswered salvo." Of the example force A: "In modern naval combat force A can only exploit its firepower advantage by teaming it with an advantage in scouting." Conclusion 7: "even a superior force must, in unstable circumstances, attack decisively first to win without severe losses." — [Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED.
- The salvo equations are simultaneous-exchange. Sequential (first-strike) versions are a later extension (Armstrong 2014; see Section 5). VERIFIED (Hughes assumption of simultaneous salvos in text; Armstrong 2014 abstract).

**Hughes's "three laws of salvo warfare" (Appendix B, after Cares 1990)**
- First law: salvos are event-stepped pulses of combat power against targets; damage is proportional to combat power over staying power: "Losses to A = [Combat power of B] / [Staying power of A]." Second law: "Combat power is the attacker's pulse of lethal energy minus the defender's actions to attenuate the energy." Third law: combat power is measured in hits, staying power in hits per ship, damage in ships. When the third law fails (suppression or demoralization decide the outcome), "the model is defective"; Hughes's example is Scheer's torpedo-boat attack at Jutland: zero hits, but the visible torpedo wakes made Jellicoe turn the British line away and let the German battleships escape. — [Hughes 1995, Appendix B](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED.
- Appendix B also states the linear-aggregation assumptions (force striking power = unit power times number of units) can be relaxed; his example is two identical warships delivering 1.7 times one ship's hits rather than 2.0 times. VERIFIED.

### Inferences
- Single-exchange outcomes in the salvo model scale linearly in unit counts, with no square term. The instability is a threshold effect from the truncation at zero and at force size, plus the fact that the denominator (staying power) is small, not a Lanchester-type squared concentration bonus. (Derived from the form of Eq. 1; consistent with Armstrong's statement that aimed-fire salvo models "exhibit square law behavior" over multiple salvos, see Section 3.)
- Hughes's "twice the attributes vs twice the numbers" parity result means that in the basic model a unit-level improvement in all three attributes by factor k is worth only a factor k in numbers, not k^2. In Lanchester's square law, fighting strength is alpha*A^2 (Hughes Appendix A), so doubling numbers is worth four times the per-unit rate. In Chase's formulation fighting strength is alpha*a1*A^2, so doubling numbers requires doubling both fighting power and staying power (a factor of four in per-unit alpha*a1).

## 2. What are the "cornerstones," constants, trends, and variables in Fleet Tactics, and what are the maxims, especially "attack effectively first," and what does Hughes say about scouting?

### Takeaway
Hughes's Fleet Tactics (1st ed. 1986; 2nd ed. 1999/2000 as Fleet Tactics and Coastal Combat; 3rd ed. 2018 as Fleet Tactics and Naval Operations, with Robert Girrier) organizes naval tactics around historical constants, trends, and variables rather than "principles of war," and the maxim "attack effectively first" is presented as the essence of naval tactical success. He ties it directly to scouting: whoever outscouts the enemy in detection, tracking, and targeting generally attacks effectively first.

### Inferences
- The brief's "cornerstones or constants/trends/variables" are two distinct organizing schemes in the 3rd edition: six cornerstones (a list of beliefs, ending in "attack effectively first") and a constants-trends-variables method for reading history. Sources I could read mention both but do not give Hughes's full definitions in his own words.
- Hughes's scouting claim and his salvo-model claim fit together: the basic salvo model has no scouting term, so first-strike advantage is imported through the embellishment multipliers (Section 3).

### Gaps

- The full text of the "Salvo Equations" chapter of the 3rd edition was not available. The Proceedings, Stratheia, and CIMSEC (distributed lethality) pages returned partial or blocked content in some fetches.

## 3. How do scouting effectiveness and defensive alertness enter the embellished salvo equations?

### Takeaway
In Hughes's Section V "embellishment," scouting effectiveness is a multiplier (0 to 1) on an attacker's striking power, and defender alertness is a multiplier (0 to 1) on counterfire. Hughes explicitly wants scouting to degrade defensive power too, because surprise (low alertness) is "likely to be fatal" in salvo warfare.

### Cited Findings

**Embellishment terms (Hughes 1995, Section V and Appendix A)**
- sigma_A, sigma_B (scouting effectiveness): value between 0 and 1 measuring "the extent to which striking power is diminished due to less-than-perfect targeting and distribution of fire against the target force." Appendix A defines it as the degradation of hits per salvo from imperfect detection or tracking, the difference between accurate shots delivered with optimal knowledge of enemy composition and location and with existing information. — [Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED.
- delta_A, delta_B (defender alertness or readiness): value between 0 and 1 measuring the extent to which counterfire is diminished "due to less-than-perfect readiness or fire control designation to destroy the missiles of an enemy attack"; normally a multiplier of a3 or b3 (caused by faulty EMCON or condition of readiness). VERIFIED.
- tau_A, tau_B (skill or training): multiplier 0 to 1, applied to firing units and target units; credited to Hatzopoulos (1990). Hughes: "the equations declare that there is no such thing as a training bonus or synergism" (multipliers only deflate). VERIFIED.
- mu_A, mu_B (distraction chaff): multiplier 0 to 1 applied to the enemy's striking power before counterfire. a4, b4 (seduction chaff and evasion): probability that an otherwise accurate shot is drawn off after counterfire has failed; "treated mathematically in the same way" for evasion (low observability, torpedo avoidance). VERIFIED.
- Modified fighting powers: a' = sigma_A * tau_A * alpha (modified also by B's distraction chaff mu_B); beta' = sigma_B * tau_B * beta (modified by A's distraction mu_A); b3' = delta_B * tau_B * b3; a3' = delta_A * tau_A * a3. The OCR of the printed embellished force-on-force equations (including exactly where a4 and b4 multiply) is garbled. — [Hughes 1995, Section V](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED for the definitions; the exact printed placement of seduction terms not recoverable (see Gaps).
- A simplified two-factor form (scouting and alertness only), from a thesis that applies the Hatzopoulos embellishment: fractional loss of B = (sigma_A * alpha * A - delta_B * b3 * B) / (b1 * B), with the symmetric expression for A. — [Sakellariou, "The Effect of Staying Power on Offensive and Defensive Power of a Modern Warship," NPS thesis, March 1993](https://ia600107.us.archive.org/6/items/theeffectofstayi1094539892/theeffectofstayi1094539892.pdf). VERIFIED for the verbal definitions; the equation image is OCR-garbled, so the algebraic form above is ESTIMATED from the thesis's definitions plus Hughes's modified-power definitions. (Sakellariou uses sigma for scouting and a tau-like symbol for alertness; Hughes uses delta for alertness and tau for training. This is a notation conflict between sources, AMBIGUOUS in symbols only.)
- Hughes's own count: the embellishments increase the number of parametric attributes "from 8 to as many as 14."

**Scouting as a term, and "scouting effectiveness reduced to four multipliers"**
- "For exploratory analysis, the whole of scouting's effect on a combat outcome can be reduced to four multipliers, sigma_A, sigma_B, delta_A, and delta_B, that take values between 0 and 1 to diminish each side's striking power and counterfire." "Both striking power and defensive power depend on good scouting. Concentration of forces (the number of participating units) depends on effective leadership and tactics. But force staying power (a1*A) is in the main a design attribute that is independent of the degree of success in scouting and tactical concentration." — [Hughes 1995, Section V conclusions 1 and 2](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED.
- "It seems particularly important to include [scouting] as a factor that degrades defensive power. Many things, including too-strict EMCON, enemy deception and stealth, and confusion over whether all enemy units have been detected and targeted, can cause a surprise attack, which in salvo warfare is likely to be fatal. We do not know in most exploratory analyses what numbers to assign to sigma and delta." VERIFIED.
- Hughes's softening of scouting weakness: "the best way to soften the consequences of scouting weakness is to increase the value of the salvo equations' denominator with either greater numbers of units or greater staying power per unit, or both." VERIFIED.
- Command and control: "Because the definitions are structured to represent performance with ideal CC, CC is always a factor deflator, never a force multiplier." CC affects participating units A, unit striking power, counterfire, and soft-kill terms, and probably least affects a1. VERIFIED.
- Anti-scouting: no quantitative term for the enemy's scouting is included; Hughes says this art "is weak and undeveloped" (above). VERIFIED.

**Scouting in the model validation lineage**
- Beall (1990) built a battle model incorporating Hughes's tactical theory in which "user variation of model inputs concerning the timing, direction and strength of each force's fire permits analysis of the impact of scouting effectiveness and C2 on battle dynamics." — [Beall thesis, NPS 1990, abstract](https://calhoun.nps.edu/handle/10945/34842) (read from the [Wikimedia-hosted copy](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fa/The_development_of_a_naval_battle_model_and_its_validation_using_historical_data_(IA_thedevelopmentof1094534842).pdf)). VERIFIED.
- Hatzopoulos (1990 NPS thesis) introduced scouting effectiveness and alertness to the basic salvo model; Lalis (1991) did sensitivity analysis on them; Sakellariou (1993) used them to show an inverse linear relationship between a ship's staying power and its defensive power and a nonlinear relationship between staying power and offensive power. — [Sakellariou 1993 abstract and Chapter III](https://ia600107.us.archive.org/6/items/theeffectofstayi1094539892/theeffectofstayi1094539892.pdf). VERIFIED.
- Sakellariou's characterization: scouting effectiveness "contributes to survivability by increasing the damage to an enemy before he attacks," and "A scouting advantage is necessary if we want to surprise the enemy and a) avoid the enemy's defensive power, or b) attack before the enemy can launch his attack." VERIFIED (a thesis, not Hughes).

### Inferences
- Because all four of sigma and delta multiply terms inside the numerator of Eq. 1, they enter the first-strike logic by shifting the sign of combat power. A side with sigma near 1 and an enemy with delta near 0 gets the effect of the "unanswered salvo" without any extra equation (my reading of Hughes's text; Hughes does not give this worked example in the paper).
- In Armstrong and Powell's Coral Sea study, the scouting term is effectively handled by the attacker's probability of "successful deployment" per aircraft (arrival at target), not by a separate sigma (see Section 4). That is a different parameterization of the same idea.

### Gaps
- The printed embellished force-on-force equations (Hughes's Eq. 5 to 6 with seduction and evasion terms) were not recoverable from the OCR; exact placement of a4/b4 and mu inside the numerator is therefore inferred from the definitions.
- No numeric values for sigma and delta are given in Hughes 1995 beyond "we do not know what numbers to assign."

## 4. Historical validation: WWII carrier battles, "one air wing sinks about one carrier," and missile-age data (Schulte 1994, Falklands, 1973 Arab-Israeli)

### Takeaway
Hughes's calibration anchor for 1942 is that one carrier air wing could, on balance, put one enemy carrier out of action; Armstrong and Powell reverse-engineer salvo parameters from that and reproduce the Coral Sea outcome. Beall (1990) validated a related model on 14 twentieth-century battles, with caveats about needing the battle's own inputs. Schulte (1994) derived missile hit probabilities from 30 incidents between 1967 and 1991: very high against defenseless merchants, about one in four against defended warships, trending upward after 1982.

### Cited Findings

**"One carrier air wing sinks about one carrier"**

- Hughes (2000, p. 100): "One carrier air wing could on balance sink or inflict crippling damage on one carrier..."; Hughes (2000, p. 268): a relatively unskilled defender "required 7 fighter squadrons to neutralize 1 attacking carrier air wing." Hughes 2000 is Fleet Tactics and Coastal Combat, 2nd ed. — as quoted in [Armstrong and Powell 2005](https://www3.carleton.ca/csds/docs/working_papers/ArmstrongWP03.pdf). VERIFIED as quoted by Armstrong and Powell (I read the quotation, not Hughes's book).
- Hughes (2000, p. 107): proposition that in 1942 naval warfare "the offense was inherently superior to the defense." — [Armstrong and Powell 2005](https://www3.carleton.ca/csds/docs/working_papers/ArmstrongWP03.pdf). VERIFIED as cited.

**Armstrong and Powell (2005) Coral Sea calibration (squadron level)**

### Inferences
- Armstrong and Powell's squadron parameters are not independent measurements. - Schulte's 0.264 versus 0.684 versus 0.913 progression is a graded measure of how much defensive alertness and counterfire reduce a missile's hit probability; the same quantity enters the salvo model as the fraction of accurate shots removed by counterfire (a3 and b3 with delta).

## 5. Later extensions and critiques: Armstrong's stochastic salvo model (2005), sequential and area-fire versions, lethality, heterogeneous models, data-farming critique

### Takeaway
Armstrong (2005) turns Hughes's fixed inputs into random variables and derives closed-form means, variances, and outcome probabilities, showing that mean ships surviving "can be highly misleading." Armstrong (2013, 2014) adds area fire and sequential fire; Armstrong (2014) finds that striking first would have helped the USN at Coral Sea more than an extra carrier. Johns, Pilnick and Hughes (2001) extend the model to heterogeneous forces.

### Cited Findings

**Armstrong (2005): the stochastic salvo model**
- Armstrong, M.J., "A Stochastic Salvo Model for Naval Surface Combat," Operations Research 53(5): 830-841 (2005). Abstract: "In this work we propose a stochastic version of the salvo model for modern naval surface combat. Numerical comparisons of the deterministic and stochastic models suggest that while the two models tend to provide similar estimates of the average number of ships surviving a salvo, this average by itself can be highly misleading with respect to the likely outcomes of the battle." — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/opre.1040.0195); [IDEAS/RePEc listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/oropre/v53y2005i5p830-841.html). VERIFIED (abstract). Note the journal is Operations Research, not Naval Research Logistics.
- Model structure: each missile launched is a Bernoulli trial (successful strike or not); the number of missiles that would hit if not intercepted, N_o, is binomial; the number of successful defensive intercepts, N_d, is binomial; missiles that hit = max(N_o - N_d, 0); damage per hit (proportion of a ship put out of action) is a random variable, assumed normal. Closed-form results use the normal approximation to the binomial to give approximate expected ship losses, variance, and probabilities such as probability of annihilation. — [Kesler, Lucas and Sanchez 2019, Section 2.2](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc19papers/238.pdf). VERIFIED (description by NPS authors who simulated the model).
- Armstrong's parameters per Kesler et al. Table 2: n_alpha, n_beta (maximum ASMs per ship per salvo); p_alpha, p_beta (probability of successful strike per ASM); n_y, n_z (maximum SAMs per ship per salvo); p_y, p_z (probability of successful intercept per SAM); u, v (mean losses per hit) with standard deviations sd_u, sd_v; A, B fixed. So 14 factors versus Hughes's 8. VERIFIED.
- Armstrong (2011, "A verification study of the stochastic salvo combat model," Annals of Operations Research) compared the closed forms to Monte Carlo simulation over 486 scenarios at 50,000 replications each; closed forms mostly perform well "except when the likelihood of offensive missiles hitting their targets has substantial positive correlation." — [Kesler et al. 2019](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc19papers/238.pdf); [Springer listing](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10479-011-0889-0). VERIFIED for the Kesler et al. description.
- Kesler, Lucas and Sanchez (2019) data-farmed the exact model: 5,120 design points times 50,000 replications (256 million simulated battles) in under half an hour on a desktop, concluding "analysts should directly use the simulation rather than resorting to approximate closed-form solutions." They add that Hughes's model "is deterministic, and therefore provides no information about the distribution of outcomes." — [Kesler et al. 2019](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc19papers/238.pdf). VERIFIED.

**Armstrong (2004): lethality**
- Armstrong, M.J. (2004), "Effects of Lethality in Naval Combat Models," Naval Research Logistics 51(1): 28-43. Abstract: examines the effect of lethality, "the relative balance between the offensive and defensive attributes of the units involved," in both discrete-time salvo models and continuous-time Lanchester models; defines three lethality levels (low, moderate, high) and finds that "more is better" varies greatly by level. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/nav.10102). VERIFIED (abstract). Armstrong and Powell characterize the Coral Sea squadron model as just barely high lethality (one CV's air strike only just knocks out one defending CV) and the aircraft model as moderate approaching high. — [Armstrong and Powell 2005](https://www3.carleton.ca/csds/docs/working_papers/ArmstrongWP03.pdf). VERIFIED.
- Bongers and Torres describe low lethality as defenses large enough to offset any attack (zero attacker losses on a side), and give the zero-loss lines of a salvo carrier model: CV_IJN/CV_USN = (p_F^USN * n_F^USN) / (p_A^IJN * n_A^IJN) for the USN-loss-zero line and (p_A^USN * n_A^USN)/(p_F^IJN * n_F^IJN) for the IJN-loss-zero line. — [Bongers and Torres 2017](https://theeconomics.uma.es/malagawpseries/Papers/METCwp2017-1.pdf). VERIFIED as printed (my transcription from OCR; subscripts garbled in extraction).

**Armstrong (2013): area fire**
- "The salvo combat model with area fire," Naval Research Logistics 60(8): 652-660. Abstract: "Although the relative size of each force is important with aimed fire, with area fire, it is the absolute size that matters. Similarly, although aimed fire exhibits square law behavior, area fire shows approximately linear behavior." Shares properties with the area-fire Lanchester model and the aimed-fire salvo model but "display[s] some interesting differences, especially over the course of several salvos." — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/nav.21559) (abstract via search result). **Armstrong (2014): sequential exchange of fire**
- Armstrong, M.J. (2014), "The salvo combat model with a sequential exchange of fire," Journal of the Operational Research Society 65(10): 1593-1601. Abstract: "This paper develops a version of the stochastic salvo combat model in which the exchange of fire is sequential, rather than simultaneous. The sequential-fire version is built by modifying the equations in the original simultaneous-fire version. The performance of the sequential model is tested by comparing its outputs with those of a Monte Carlo simulation. The fit between the model and the simulation is very close, especially for the mean and standard deviation of losses. The model is then applied to the Battle of the Coral Sea. **Heterogeneous salvo models**
- Johns, M.D., Pilnick, S.E., and Hughes, W.P., Jr., "Heterogeneous Salvo Model for the Navy After Next," NPS Technical Report NPS-IJWA-01-010, January 2001. VERIFIED.

**Critiques and limits (as stated by sources)**
- Deterministic; ignores variability and assumes homogeneous forces: [Kesler et al. 2019](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc19papers/238.pdf), [Armstrong 2005 abstract](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/opre.1040.0195), [Armstrong and Powell 2005](https://www3.carleton.ca/csds/docs/working_papers/ArmstrongWP03.pdf) ("One limitation of the basic salvo model is its deterministic nature"). VERIFIED.
- Hughes's own limits: uniform fire distribution assumed; the third law fails when suppression or demoralization decides a battle (Jutland torpedo example); salvo equations ignore undersea warfare, so "design features suggested by the salvo equations will understate the value of staying power"; the model has "nil" a priori predictive power; in littoral operations defensive effectiveness will be "curtailed because of short response time." — [Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED.
- Armstrong and Powell: the model came from missile combat; aircraft are more flexible (fighters escort and fly CAP; dive bombers scout and add air defense) and aircraft attacks are more complex (an interception can ruin an attacker's aim without shooting it down, and shot-down aircraft may have launched already); the squadron model assumes perfect within-squadron correlation, the aircraft model independence, "the most reasonable estimate probably lies in between." — [Armstrong and Powell 2005](https://www3.carleton.ca/csds/docs/working_papers/ArmstrongWP03.pdf). VERIFIED.
- Schulte's data come mostly from ships under 7,000 tons; modern large combatants with layered defense are outside his data. VERIFIED ([Schulte](https://archive.org/details/analysisofhistor00schu)).
- A 2023 to 2024 comment (Red Sea) in a secondary source claims defense success rates against missile attacks improved dramatically compared with the historical record. — [Center for Maritime Strategy](https://centerformaritimestrategy.org/publications/missile-warfare-outside-the-lab/). REPORTED, low confidence; the fetch summary of this page was internally inconsistent with Schulte's primary numbers, so I give the claim no weight.
- Statement of Armstrong 2005 and Hughes 1995 relation: Kesler et al. say the model has also been used to explore force design, warfighting tactics, and the value of information, citing Hughes (2018), Armstrong (2003), and Lucas and McGunnigle (2002). VERIFIED as a citation list.

### Inferences
- The stochastic extension's headline point for any simulation using expected-value attrition: with discrete units and a threshold at 0 and at the force size, mean survivors can hide wide outcome spreads (Coral Sea: mean about 1.5 carriers lost per side, yet 90 percent intervals of 0 to 2). This follows directly from Armstrong and Powell's histograms, in which a 54 percent chance of a total USN carrier loss coexists with a mean of 1.55.
- Armstrong 2013's statement that aimed-fire salvos "exhibit square law behavior" and area-fire salvos "approximately linear behavior" lines the salvo model up with the Lanchester square and linear laws respectively, over multi-salvo sequences.

### Gaps
- Armstrong 2005 closed-form equations, Armstrong 2011 results, Armstrong 2013 equations, and Armstrong 2014's numerical first-strike results were not accessible (paywalled; abstracts only).
- Johns, Pilnick, and Hughes's heterogeneous equations not read (DTIC fetch failed); only descriptions and citations.

- Little independent critical literature on the salvo model beyond Armstrong's stochastic and Kesler et al.'s simulation points. I found no critique of the uniform-fire-distribution or linear-damage assumptions beyond Hughes's own caveats.

## 6. Pre-Lanchester naval theory: Fiske (1905), Chase (1902), Baudry, and why crossing the T is a ratio of guns that can bear

### Takeaway
Naval officers developed quantitative concentration theory before Lanchester: Chase (1902, secret Naval War College paper) derived square-law results and a staying-power term in continuous-fire equations; Fiske (1905, USNI Prize Essay) modeled big-gun combat as discrete salvos in tables and showed a numerical or first-fire edge compounds; Baudry's The Naval Battle states the square law and the value of a few minutes of unopposed fire. Crossing the T is the geometric device that maximizes guns bearing on the enemy and minimizes the enemy's guns bearing on you.

### Cited Findings

**Chase (1902)**
- Lieutenant (later Rear Admiral) J.V. Chase, "A Mathematical Investigation of the Effect of Superiority of Force in Combats Upon the Sea," 1902, a CONFIDENTIAL paper presented to Cdr. W. McCarty Little for Naval War College war games, not declassified until 1972, reprinted in Appendix C of Fiske, The Navy as a Fighting Machine (1916; Naval Institute Press reissue 1988). — [Hughes 1995, Appendix C](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED. (The Hughes text spells the name "McCartey Little" in the OCR; I use the standard spelling "McCarty Little," ESTIMATED from memory of the historical record, not verified in a source I read.)
- Chase assumptions as restated by Hughes: continuous stream of shell fire; all ships on a side have identical unit fighting power (alpha or beta) and staying power (a1 or b1); unit fighting power constant; "square-law conditions apply: every engaged ship is able to fire at every engaged enemy ship, and as soon as a ship is out of action the fact is known and fire is shifted to a ship with fighting power remaining." — [Hughes 1995, Appendix C](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED.
- Chase's result: fighting strengths alpha*a1*A^2 versus beta*b1*B^2; "If alpha*a1*A^2 > beta*b1*B^2, A will win a battle of annihilation." Two conclusions: with twice as many units on one side, the smaller force's units must be "twice as strong in fighting power and twice as strong in staying power" for parity; equal increases in fighting power and staying power contribute equally to fighting strength. Hughes: "Chase's equations are more powerful than Lanchester's in that not only force size and fighting power variations can be explored but also staying power," and Chase's formulation gives losses (work done), "no minus sign," where Lanchester computed forces remaining. VERIFIED.
- Chase's own 1921 retrospective: the staff debate over "value of concentration of fire" was in "glittering generalities"; he introduced a "unit of destruction" delivered; worked example: eight ships a side, one masked so m = 8 and n = 7; "the eight ships will destroy the seven ships and will have the equivalent of sqrt(15) (nearly four) intact ships with which to engage the remaining one intact ship... after destroying this one intact ship there will remain sqrt(14)... the equivalent of 3.74 ships." Checking: sqrt(8^2 - 7^2) = sqrt(15) = 3.873; sqrt(15 - 1) = sqrt(14) = 3.742. ESTIMATED arithmetic confirming Hughes's quoted figures. VERIFIED as quotation; ESTIMATED as arithmetic.
- Chase on big versus small ships: "if there be twice as many units on one side as there are on the other, each unit of the force having the smaller number of units must be twice as strong offensively and twice as strong defensively as one of the hostile units. This has a bearing upon the question of large or small ships." Chase also noted the equations applied only to gunfire and that "the force inferior in gunfire should use the ram or torpedo as early as possible" because sudden destruction has least effect on accuracy if late in the engagement. VERIFIED.
- Hughes: Chase "foreshadows the Englishman F. W. Lanchester, and the Russian M. Osipov who rediscover (!) in 1915 the aggregate, greater-than-linear advantage of numerical concentration." (Osipov, Military Collection 1915; translated as US Army CAA Research Paper CAA-RP-91-2.) VERIFIED.

**Fiske (1905)**
- Bradley A. Fiske, "American Naval Policy," US Naval Institute Proceedings, March 1905 (Hughes cites March 1905; the USNI site lists a January 1905 Proceedings issue for the essay, so the month is AMBIGUOUS between sources: [Hughes 1995 reference 13](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content) versus [USNI page](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1905/january/american-naval-policy), which I could not open (403) and know only through a search summary). It was a prize essay.
- Fiske "produced a series of equations which greatly influenced the theory and practice of naval gunfire," possibly inspired by the Russo-Japanese War and Tsushima; he used discrete time periods to examine continuous fire, "functioning similarly to a digital fire control computer," and tables rather than differential equations. — [NavWeaps, "N-Squared Law" (Czarnecki)](https://www.navweaps.com/index_tech/tech-076.php). REPORTED (secondary, written by an author who applies Fiske's tables).
- Fiske's essay "contained no calculus, but rather modelled big-gun naval combat as a series of discrete salvos, with the implications being drawn from a set of tables... His conclusions were stark: the side with the greater number of big guns brought to bear would realise a disproportionate and accelerating advantage, eventually annihilating its opponent with a final remaining force much greater than the initial imbalance. Further, the side which could begin firing first would enjoy a further advantage, again out of all proportion to naive expectations, as worked out by Baudry in an example in which he gives one fleet a mere four minutes' initial unopposed fire." Footnote: "Big guns" means 11 inch or greater; Fiske notes smaller guns may give greater weight of fire, but "if the guns are too small to destroy [the enemy's] turrets and water-line, this energy is wholly wasted." — [MacKay, Price and Wood 2016, "Weight of Shell Must Tell: A Lanchestrian Reappraisal of the Battle of Jutland," History 101: 536-563](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/99759/2/Jutland_PostReview.pdf). VERIFIED (read the accepted manuscript).
- Fiske's unit: each turret and its guns "regarded as a unit of offensive and defensive power" (12-inch turrets as the unit); a force with even slight initial numerical superiority wins as salvos are traded, and an initially inferior force that could "hit first for a given period" could reverse that. — [USNI Proceedings January 1905 page summary (search result)](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1905/january/american-naval-policy) and [MacKay et al.](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/99759/2/Jutland_PostReview.pdf) ("the unit, rather, is the set of weapons which stands and falls together," quoting Fiske). REPORTED.
- Jellicoe's Grand Fleet Battle Orders before Jutland: "I attach the greatest importance to making full use of the fire of our heavier guns in the early stages at long range... [this] may give us the initial advantage in gunfire which it is so important to obtain." — [MacKay et al.](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/99759/2/Jutland_PostReview.pdf), citing Jellicoe Papers vol. I, item 226. VERIFIED as quoted there.
- Fiske's aphorism "concentration requires isolation" (Fighting Machine, p. 263) and the Fiske, Baudry, and Lanchester principle that the system "must be that which will enable the highest rate of engagement without danger of defeat in detail." — [MacKay et al.](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/99759/2/Jutland_PostReview.pdf). VERIFIED as quoted.

**Baudry**
- Lt A. Baudry (French Navy), The Naval Battle: Studies of the Tactical Factors (Hugh Rees, London, 1914). States the square law: "If two ships fight one, then not only does the lone ship receive double the rate of fire, but its own fire is divided. Thus the proportional rates of attrition are in the ratio 1:4, not 1:2. The full implication of this instantaneous truth only emerges when one sums its effects over the full battle." — [MacKay et al. 2016, note 16](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/99759/2/Jutland_PostReview.pdf); [Hughes 1995 reference 6](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED.
- Hughes also cites Bernotti, Fundamentals of Naval Tactics (1912) among the early naval officer analysts. VERIFIED (reference list).

**Lanchester and the naval link**
- Lanchester's Aircraft in Warfare: The Dawn of the Fourth Arm (London, 1916), based on 1914 articles; Jellicoe wrote to Lanchester on 15 June 1916 that "your N-square law has become famous in the Grand Fleet." — [MacKay et al. 2016, notes 7 and 16](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/99759/2/Jutland_PostReview.pdf); 1914 article date also appears in a [Roads to the Great War summary](http://roadstothegreatwar-ww1.blogspot.com/2018/08/how-analysis-of-strategy-changed-in-wwi.html). VERIFIED (Jellicoe letter, via MacKay et al. citing the Lanchester archive, Coventry); REPORTED (1914 article date).
- MacKay et al. state the Square Law "normally exclusively attributed to Lanchester" was derived with calculus independently by Lanchester in Britain, Osipov in Russia, and Chase in the USA, and is also clearly stated in Baudry. VERIFIED.
- Navweaps/Czarnecki numerical contrast: 100 units against 75 similar units: a linear theory predicts the 100 destroy the 75 at a cost of 75 and keep 25; the square law predicts the 75 are annihilated at a cost of 34, leaving 66. — [NavWeaps](https://www.navweaps.com/index_tech/tech-076.php). REPORTED. Check: sqrt(100^2 - 75^2) = sqrt(4,375) = 66.1. ESTIMATED arithmetic confirming the 66 survivors and the cost of 34 (100 - 66).
- Case One in that article: dreadnoughts with concentrated heavy broadsides versus pre-dreadnought mixed batteries: "during the initial stages of the engagement, the dreadnoughts of Fleet A have a two to one advantage." REPORTED.

**Fleet geometry: concentrating fire without massing ships; crossing the T**
- "Applied geometry": after Jutland, Plunkett-Drax (on Beatty's staff) wrote "It is 'Applied Geometry' that must ensure for us the crushing effect to be obtained by bringing all our forces into action at the same moment." MacKay et al. argue the big gun's long range meant concentration of fire no longer required massing ships; the commander's goal became a geometry in which all his big guns bear on the enemy while denying the enemy the reverse; their Figure 1 plots the log ratio of British to German big guns in action over time at Jutland, with Lanchestrian advantage achieved when the ratio is maximized. — [MacKay et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/99759/2/Jutland_PostReview.pdf). VERIFIED.
- Mechanism of crossing the T: one fleet's line passes across the head of the enemy column so its broadsides fire through the enemy's bows (enfilade) while the enemy can reply only with forward-bearing guns. "The attacking battle line is able to fire full broadsides at the enemy, who can only reply initially with forward guns." Examples: Tsushima 1905 (Togo, whole Japanese line concentrated on the lead Russian ships), Elli 13 December 1912 (Kountouriotis), Jutland 1916 (Jellicoe achieved it twice but the Germans escaped by reversing course in poor visibility), Cape Esperance 1942, Surigao Strait 1944 (last crossing between battleships; American radar-guided fire). The tactic declined with rising engagement ranges, radar, missiles, and aircraft: long-range strikes "are less dependent on the direction the ships are facing." — [Wikipedia, "Crossing the T"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossing_the_T) (as fetched; Surigao statement also in [Warfare History Network](https://warfarehistorynetwork.com/article/clash-at-surigao-strait-the-last-battle-line/) per search result). REPORTED.
- Hughes (via Beall's findings): crossing the T "was more often a consequence of accident than design." — [Hughes 1995, Section II](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content). VERIFIED.
- MacKay et al.: in symmetric, good-visibility, sufficient-time conditions "an almost textbook employment of Lanchester's equations could be played out"; a fleet that knew itself outgunned "would refuse battle." They also cite Niblack (1896) arguing for fighting en echelon, and Bacon and Jellicoe in favor of line ahead (Jellicoe: "to divide the fleet is to court disaster"). VERIFIED as quoted.

**Crossing the T as a ratio of guns that can bear (arithmetic illustration)**
- ESTIMATED illustration (my arithmetic, not from a source): suppose each ship has g guns on a broadside but only f guns that bear forward or aft (f < g), and a line of n ships crosses ahead of the head of an enemy column of n ships. If only the lead enemy ship can bear on you and only with f guns while all n of your ships bear their broadside g (counting aside range effects), the guns-bearing ratio is (n*g) : f. With n = 6, g = 10, f = 4 this is 60 : 4 = 15 : 1 at the instant of crossing, versus 60 : 60 = 1 : 1 for parallel lines. The advantage decays as the enemy turns and more of his ships bring their broadsides to bear, which is the geometry described in MacKay et al. Not a modeled result from any source; no source in this file gives specific gun counts for this illustration.
- The same ratio logic is what Lanchester's square law needs: the model requires "every engaged ship is able to fire at every engaged enemy ship" (Chase assumption 4, [Hughes 1995 Appendix C](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content)). Crossing the T is a deliberate way to violate that symmetry in your favor: a side's effective numbers in the square-law formula become the count of its guns bearing, not the count of its guns. ESTIMATED (my inference; MacKay et al. make the same "geometry dictates calculus" point in words).

### Inferences
- Fiske's tables and Baudry's example already contain both ideas later central to Hughes: concentration compounds (square-law-like) and an initial unopposed interval gives a disproportionate edge, which is the continuous-fire ancestor of "attack effectively first."
- Hughes's salvo model differs from the Chase/Lanchester line mainly in that missiles can be destroyed in flight (defensive power) and that a single salvo can exceed a unit's staying power; in gun combat, combat power was small relative to staying power, so exchanges were gradual and concentration dominated.

- No source found stating the gun-bearing ratio for any specific historical crossing (Tsushima, Surigao) in numbers.
- Crossing-the-T effectiveness doubts (for example, forum discussion) were not read beyond Wikipedia and Hughes's "accident rather than design" remark.
