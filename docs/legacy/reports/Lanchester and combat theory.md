# Frontage and Sight Bend Lanchester's Laws

Across five bodies of literature, the quantity that decides an engagement is the number of units that can actually deliver effective fire at a given moment, not the number present. Lanchester's square law holds when every unit can engage any enemy and several can focus on one; it degrades toward a linear law when fire is unaimed or when geometry caps how many units on each side can engage, and the best-developed formal treatment of a cap models it as a piecewise switch between regimes rather than a fractional exponent. Concentration of force and defeat in detail are exact consequences of the square law (two equal halves defeated in sequence by an equal total force leave the winner about 71% of its strength), but no historical dataset has cleanly validated the law, and fitted exponents swing wildly with method. Deitchman's ambush model, where one side sees and aims while the other fires blind, yields a hidden-side advantage that is bounded (a factor of 2 in kill rate, or the square root of 2 in numbers, in the standard normalization) rather than overwhelming. Hughes's naval salvo model drops the square term altogether: outcomes are linear in unit counts, and the decisive variable is who lands an effective salvo before the other's counterfire and survival margin matter, which is why "attack effectively first" and scouting dominate his tactics. In air combat, altitude is stored speed (specific energy Es = h + V^2/2g), and the sources value a height edge as initiative, choice of engagement, and first detection rather than as a single number. Many of the notes' key facts rest on second-hand or abstract-level sources; this report carries each confidence tag and each unresolved conflict forward, and it makes no design recommendations.

## The square law needs unlimited engagement; a cap on engaged units turns it linear

Lanchester derived the square law in articles dated 2 and 9 October 1914, reprinted in his 1916 book, from the premise that under modern long-range aimed fire the loss rate on one side is proportional to the number of enemy units firing: db/dt = -c r and dr/dt = -k b, from which b^2 - r^2 is constant and fighting strength is "proportional to the square of its numerical strength multiplied by the fighting value of its individual units" ([Lanchester 1916](https://archive.org/stream/aircraftinwarfa00lancgoog/aircraftinwarfa00lancgoog_djvu.txt)). [VERIFIED] He never used the word "linear." [VERIFIED] His contrast case is "ancient conditions," where "one man would ordinarily find himself opposed to one man" and "the number of men actually wielding their weapons at any given instant (so long as the fighting line was unbroken), was, roughly speaking, the same on both sides" ([Lanchester 1916](https://archive.org/stream/aircraftinwarfa00lancgoog/aircraftinwarfa00lancgoog_djvu.txt)). [VERIFIED] That phrase is the nearest Lanchester comes to naming a frontage condition. He also gave a second case, fire directed at a known area independent of force size, where "there is no direct value in concentration, qua concentration" and strength is proportional to numbers rather than their square. [VERIFIED] He wrote no equation for a constricted pass and dismissed Macaulay's "In yon strait path a thousand / May well be stopped by three" as belonging to "the methods and conditions of the past." [VERIFIED] Osipov published an independent analysis in Voenniy Sbornik in 1915; sources disagree on its content, because Helmbold's abstract says it fully analyzes the square law while the Dupuy Institute says it used a 3/2 power ([Dupuy Institute on Osipov](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2017/07/03/osipov/)). [AMBIGUOUS] The original text was not accessible.

Modern restatements make the conditions explicit. Washburn gives aimed fire as dx/dt = -beta y and dy/dt = -alpha x, with fighting strength proportional to the lethality coefficient and the square of the numbers; a new unit helps twice, by firing and by "dilut[ing] the enemy's fire against the units already in battle," which is why lethality enters only to the first power ([Washburn 2000](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf)). [VERIFIED] The linear law arises from unaimed area fire, where each shooter's kill rate scales with target density and "there is no 'dilution effect'" ([Washburn 2000](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf)). [VERIFIED] Mesterton-Gibbons documents a second route to the same linear state equation, the Franks and Partridge one-to-one model dm/dt = -a_n min(m, n), justified because "the battlefield geometry does not allow simultaneous attacks of many against one" ([Mesterton-Gibbons 2016](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf)). [VERIFIED] His summary is that "concentrated fire favors a square-law outcome, whereas positional fire and one-to-one combat favor a linear-law outcome." [VERIFIED] Atkinson, Kress and MacKay list the square law's implicit assumptions: every unit in action, fire aimed, target kills known, new targets acquired quickly, and, in their own list of unrealistic assumptions, that each side can deploy all units at the outset ([Atkinson, Kress and MacKay 2020](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/Atkinson_Kress_MacKay_2020.pdf)). [VERIFIED] Stanescu, Barriga and Buro argue the square law "has nothing to do with range - what is really important is the rate of acquiring new targets" ([Stanescu et al. 2015](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12780)); that is their stated view, not a test result. [VERIFIED]

The most direct frontage treatment is Atkinson, Kress and MacKay's deployment-constrained model. When Blue must attack along a defile so that only Bmax can engage, the other B - Bmax units sit in reserve, "neither effective nor vulnerable," and replace casualties. With caps on both sides and perfect targeting the engagement "begins as Lanchester's ancient warfare model," dB/dt = -r Rmax and dR/dt = -b Bmax, with a linear state equation; when one side falls below its cap the fight becomes mixed, and when both are below, it is pure square-law aimed fire. The one-sided parity condition is r R0^2 / (b B0^2) = mu(2 - mu), where mu = Bmax/B0. At mu = 1/2 that factor is 3/4, so Blue's kill rate must rise by 4/3 or its numbers by the square root of that ([Atkinson, Kress and MacKay 2020](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/Atkinson_Kress_MacKay_2020.pdf)). [VERIFIED] Their motivating case is Ein-A-Tinna (1982), where terrain forced Israeli tanks into a single column, turning an unconstrained 6 against 30 into an effective 6 against 1, a figure they cite to Gabriel 1984 (not read). [REPORTED] Kress's narrow-passage variant, with Red concentrating fire on a lone front weapon, has parity rho R0^2/(beta B0) = 2, so the attacker "will need an initial force size in the order of the square of the Red initial force" ([Kress 2020](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/Lanchester_Models.pdf)). [VERIFIED] Bongers and Torres (2021) report that a bottleneck on both sides yields the linear law for both aimed and unaimed fire, and that for Thermopylae the Persians would have lost had the bottleneck persisted, a result seen only in the abstract ([Operational Research 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12351-019-00513-0)). [REPORTED]

The notes' own Euler integration of a symmetric cap shows how the outcome slides between the laws (1000 Blue against 500 Red, equal quality): survivors are 865.7 with no cap (analytic 866.0), 599.9 at an engaged width of 200, 549.9 at 100, 525.0 at 50, and 505.0 at 10, approaching the pure linear answer of 500. [ESTIMATED] The notes' check of the Atkinson et al. parity formula at mu = 0.4 also reproduced it: at r/b = 0.64 the fight ended in mutual annihilation within integration error. [ESTIMATED] A single exponent does not describe the slide; the literature treats a cap as a regime change, and the notes found no source that derives a fractional exponent from a frontage width. The Epstein critique relayed by Mesterton-Gibbons makes the same point from the other side: "no crowding or force-to-space constraint ever moderates the extent to which Group 2 can concentrate its force" ([Mesterton-Gibbons 2016](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf)). [VERIFIED as a paraphrase; Epstein not read]

Fractional exponents do exist, but they come from curve-fitting, not frontage. Bracken's generalization dB/dt = a R^p B^q gives a state exponent of 1 + p - q (1 linear, 2 square, p = 0 and q = 1 the logarithmic law) ([Lucas and Turkes 2004](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bc44457c-0423-42cc-bd62-585149fbf95d/content)). [VERIFIED] Stanescu et al. fitted an attrition order of "about 1.56" to StarCraft battles and, in a later thesis, 1.7 for microRTS ([thesis](https://skatgame.net/mburo/ps/thesis_stanescu_2018.pdf)). [VERIFIED] A Wikipedia line that "an exponent of 1.5 is often used in contemporary warfare" could not be traced to a primary source. [REPORTED] The 1.5, 1.56 and Osipov's reported 3/2 share a value but no derivation, and the notes flag their common origin as AMBIGUOUS. Implied exponents from historical fits often fall below 1, not between 1 and 2: Hartley's p = 0.45, q = 0.75 implies 0.70 ([MacKay](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0606300)). [REPORTED; implied exponent ESTIMATED]

The quality-for-numbers trade follows from the exponent. Under the square law a side outnumbered 3 to 1 needs fighters at least nine times as effective; under the linear law three times ([Mesterton-Gibbons 2016](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf)). [VERIFIED] Lanchester's own example is that a machine gun worth sixteen riflemen replaces a battalion at one quarter of its numbers (n = 250 against 1,000, since 16 n^2 = 1,000^2), because the enemy concentrates on the gunner ([Lanchester 1916](https://archive.org/stream/aircraftinwarfa00lancgoog/aircraftinwarfa00lancgoog_djvu.txt)). [VERIFIED] For a generalized exponent n the required quality factor is the numerical ratio raised to n, which the notes tabulated: at 2 to 1 it is 2 (linear), 2.83 (n = 1.5), 2.95 (n = 1.56) and 4 (square). [ESTIMATED] Lanchester's heterogeneous-force rule (square of the sum of square roots) is wrong according to MacKay, who credits Lepingwell; the conserved quantity uses average effectiveness ([MacKay](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0606300)). [VERIFIED]

## Concentration compounds as a sum of squares, but history has not confirmed the law

Lanchester's concentration results are arithmetic consequences of the square law. Blue 1000 meeting half of Red's 1000 destroys it at a cost of "only about 134," then meets the other half and finishes with 707, a total loss of 293, whereas the same two forces fought undivided annihilate each other (the equal-force "prolonged indefinitely" curve) ([Lanchester 1916](https://archive.org/stream/aircraftinwarfar00lancrich/aircraftinwarfar00lancrich_djvu.txt)). [VERIFIED] The general statement is geometric: forces fighting separately in time "have only the fighting strength" of the hypotenuse, sqrt(a^2 + b^2). [VERIFIED] The notes derive survivors as N sqrt(1 - 1/k) for an equal-total enemy arriving in k equal parts: 0.707 N for two parts, 0.816 N for three, 0.894 N for five, 0.949 N for ten, and show that the minimum force still winning against total M in k parts is M/sqrt(k), which for k = 2 is Lanchester's 1 to sqrt(2). [ESTIMATED; the notes found no source stating the general formula] For packets of size p against a standing force of 100 with total attacker strength 100, standing-force survivors are 99.5 at p = 1, 94.9 at p = 10, 70.7 at p = 50, and zero at p = 100. [ESTIMATED] Lanchester's limiting case, infinitesimal arrivals, gives added fighting value of zero, "the complete impotence of small forces in the presence of one of overwhelming power," but he says explicitly that this does not hold for small forces concealed or dug in, because the premise that the large force can bring its weapons to bear fails ([Lanchester 1916](https://archive.org/stream/aircraftinwarfar00lancrich/aircraftinwarfar00lancrich_djvu.txt)). [VERIFIED] The same benefit vanishes under the linear law: Stanescu et al. note that with equal coefficients serial splitting "will have the same outcome as without splitting: a draw" ([Stanescu et al. 2015](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12780/12780-52-16297-1-2-20201228.pdf)). [VERIFIED]

Lanchester applied the arithmetic to Nelson's October 1805 memorandum: 40 British ships against 46, with two columns of 16 and one of 8. The 32 enveloped half of the allied fleet (23 ships, since 23 x sqrt(2) = 32.5), giving British 32^2 + 8^2 = 1088 against allied 23^2 + 23^2 = 1058, whereas fighting in conventional lines gave British 40^2 = 1600 against allied 46^2 = 2116, a balance of 516 for the enemy, about 23 ships surviving. He found it "truly remarkable" that Nelson split the enemy in two equal parts, the square-law minimum of total effective strength, and noted the actual forces were 27 against 33, less favorable than the memorandum ([Lanchester 1916](https://archive.org/stream/aircraftinwarfar00lancrich/aircraftinwarfar00lancrich_djvu.txt)). [VERIFIED] A popular summary of Fox's three-phase model for the actual battle reports survivors "equivalent to 13 to 14 intact ships"; the notes' own square-law recomputation of that sequence gives about 16, and the gap could not be resolved because Fox's coefficients were not available ([Doolanshire](http://www.doolanshire.net/2018/12/13/force-concentration-lanchester-and-trafalgar/)). [REPORTED; discrepancy AMBIGUOUS] Lanchester also lists non-mathematical aggravators of a divided force: the beaten section may retreat and refit, and the first-engaged half knows it is "cannon fodder." [VERIFIED]

Timing matters because the standard equations contain no reinforcement term ([Mesterton-Gibbons 2016](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf)). [VERIFIED] The notes integrated an overlapping-arrival case under the square law with equal coefficients: a standing force of 100 against 120 attackers delivered as 60 now and 60 later. With the second 60 arriving at t = 0 the attacker keeps 66.3; at t = 0.2, 46.1; at t = 0.5 the attacker loses and 30.6 defenders remain; once the first 60 are already dead (t = 0.8 or later) the defenders end with 52.9 despite the attacker's 20% larger total. [ESTIMATED] The size of the penalty is the attrition the early packet suffers while the later one is absent. For models that do add reinforcement, Engel's Iwo Jima model uses dx/dt = r(t) - beta y ([Washburn 2000](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf)) [VERIFIED], and McCartney (2022) reports that repeated aimed-fire battles with troop replenishment produce decisive victory under constant reinforcement but stalemate, oscillation, chaos and fractal partitioning of the strength space under quadratic reinforcement ([McCartney](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/phsmap/v586y2022ics0378437121007500.html)). [REPORTED; abstract only] The notes found no source that states a general theorem on feeding units in a few at a time beyond Lanchester's sections 34 and 35.

Historical fit is poor. Engel (1954) fitted Iwo Jima with a reinforcement term, obtaining beta = 0.0544 and alpha = 0.0106 (ratio 5.1) and a good fit, but Lucas and Turkes note that only one side had daily data ([Washburn 2000](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf); [Lucas and Turkes 2004](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bc44457c-0423-42cc-bd62-585149fbf95d/content)). [VERIFIED] Every later study with two-sided time-phased data found no basic law fitting well: Bracken's near-linear Ardennes result came largely from a grid search concentrated near the linear law; Fricker found neither law fit and losses depended more on a force's own size; a Bayesian refit concluded "the logarithmic law fits best [and] the linear laws cannot be rejected, but the square law does seem implausible"; Clemens got (p, q) = (0, 1.62) or (5.32, 3.63) from the same Kursk data depending on method. Lucas and Turkes report on Kursk (14 days) R^2 of 0.081 for square, 0.131 for linear, 0.085 for logarithmic and 0.238 for the best free fit, with heavily overlapping bootstrap intervals; their four-phase Kursk model explained more variance (R^2 0.832) than the mean-loss baseline (0.800), and they conclude that phase, terrain and posture explain more than the Lanchester forms ([Lucas and Turkes 2004](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bc44457c-0423-42cc-bd62-585149fbf95d/content)). [VERIFIED] On the Ardennes with Bracken's weights the square law was the best basic law (R^2 0.367). [VERIFIED] Hartley and Helmbold found Inchon-Seoul did not fit one constant-coefficient square-law battle but fit three such battles, "any square law effects are largely masked by other factors." [VERIFIED for the quotes as reproduced; abstract details REPORTED] A negative-exponent result appears in the Italian-campaign engagement data (g = -0.594, with square-law behavior for attackers and linear for defenders in fortified positions, reversed in prepared and hasty defenses) ([Fain, reprinted by the Dupuy Institute](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2018/05/02/the-lanchester-equations-and-historical-warfare/)). [VERIFIED as a reprint of the 1977 text] The Dupuy Institute, an interested party, counts nine independent tests with "basically no result" ([Dupuy Institute](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2016/02/29/lanchester-equations-have-been-weighed/)). [REPORTED] The notes' reading is that Lanchester forms fit short fully engaged fights better than long multi-day battles; Fain makes that point about engagements "while the forces are actually engaged." [VERIFIED as quoted]

Dupuy's alternative is multiplicative judgment factors, not Lanchester equations: posture 1.0 offense, 1.3 hasty defense, 1.5 prepared and 1.6 fortified, with defense-to-offense strength from about 1.3 to over 3.0 once terrain is added ([Dupuy Institute on defense](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2018/05/30/dupuys-verities-the-power-of-defense/)). [REPORTED] In his 672-case division-level database the attacker won 42% of cases at force ratios of 0.50 to 0.99, 55% at 1.00 to 1.49, 71% at 2.00 to 2.49, 83% at 2.50 to 2.99 and 69% at 3.00 to 3.49, with small, noisy counts above that ([Dupuy Institute](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2019/10/31/tests-using-the-752-case-division-level-data-base/)). [REPORTED; not peer reviewed] The notes see no step at 3 to 1 in that table. Hughes, writing about naval combat, holds that "the square law conditions rarely hold for ground combat, but usually do for naval combat," since there is no terrain or fortification advantage at sea ([Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content)). [VERIFIED]

For real-time strategy, the applied literature is predictive, not explanatory. Stanescu et al. predicted SparCraft battle winners with 89.8% accuracy from 10 training battles up to 93.2% from 500 (the earlier Bayesian model scored 83.0% to 91.2%), and a Lanchester-based attack/retreat rule lifted UAlbertaBot's average win rate from 41.6% to 63.9% with default strengths and 69.7% with learned ones, though only 23.5% against IceBot's bunkers ([Stanescu et al. 2015](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12780/12780-52-16297-1-2-20201228.pdf)). [VERIFIED] They justify an attrition order above 1 by focus fire on damaged units, not by range. [VERIFIED] Uriarte and Ontanon report 94.10% winner accuracy over 6,066 StarCraft replay combats for a target-selection Lanchester model, and that 99,598 of 140,797 extracted combats (about 71%) "ended by reinforcement" ([Uriarte and Ontanon](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.05305)). [VERIFIED] The 2015 predictor excludes reinforcement cases by construction, and both it and the 2018 thesis "completely ignore" unit positions, so no published accuracy number covers surround, choke, concave or high-ground effects; a topographic-considerations StarCraft paper exists but was paywalled ([Stanescu thesis](https://skatgame.net/mburo/ps/thesis_stanescu_2018.pdf)). [VERIFIED as stated limits; gap noted] Adams's developer essay offers square-law balance arithmetic but no playtest data ([Adams 2004](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-designer-s-notebook-kicking-butt-by-the-numbers-lanchester-s-laws)). [REPORTED]

## When one side cannot be seen, the hidden side's edge is real but bounded

Deitchman (1962) asked what force ratios guerrillas can overcome, and the abstract states that "an attacking guerrilla force can, by using tactics that compensate for its weaknesses, defeat a force of defending regulars that has over-all superiority in numbers and weapons" ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/oropre/v10y1962i6p818-827.html)). [VERIFIED for the abstract; the paper body was paywalled and not read] The model, as reproduced by later authors, pairs aimed fire from a hidden side against an exposed one with area fire in the other direction. In Kress's normalization the exposed force B loses at B' = -rho R, the hidden force R at R' = -beta B (R/R0), the state equation is (beta/2)(B0^2 - B^2) = rho R0 (R0 - R), and parity is rho R0^2/(beta B0^2) = 1/2: the exposed side must "double its per-capita effectiveness (kill rate) or increase its initial force size by sqrt(2)" ([Kress 2020](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/Lanchester_Models.pdf)). [VERIFIED] Atkinson, Kress and MacKay derive the same result as the case with no battle damage assessment, and add that poor targeting "would classically be thought of as leading to the Linear Law ... but, in fact, ... leads to a Square Law with a penalty factor," with parity sigma/2 for imperfect acquisition ([preprint](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/Atkinson_Kress_MacKay_2020.pdf)). [VERIFIED] Mesterton-Gibbons writes the raw form dm/dt = -alpha_n n m, dn/dt = -alpha_m m, with state equation 2 alpha_m (m0 - m) = alpha_n (n0^2 - n^2), so the ambusher wins iff alpha_m/alpha_n > n0^2/(2 m0); his worked case is 10 hidden fighters with a thousandfold effectiveness edge beating 100, with 5 surviving ([Mesterton-Gibbons 2016](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf)). [VERIFIED] The two presentations differ in what the coefficient means (a per-shooter rate at initial target density versus a raw coefficient on target count), so any quoted force ratio needs its normalization; the notes flag this as AMBIGUOUS in presentation, not in the mathematics. The notes' arithmetic shows how steep the requirement is: beating a 3 to 1 numerical disadvantage needs a per-capita rate ratio above 4.5 and beating 10 to 1 needs above 50. [ESTIMATED] Epstein called the ambush variant "may well be the most plausible of all Lanchester variants" ([Mesterton-Gibbons 2016](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf)). [REPORTED]

The successors replace the binary hidden/exposed split with a continuum. Kress and MacKay's generalized Deitchman model gives the exposed side information mu between 0 and 1 (0 is Deitchman, 1 is aimed fire), with parity ((1 - mu)/2)(1 + mu ln(mu)/(1 - mu))^(-1) ([Kress 2020](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/Lanchester_Models.pdf); [Kress and MacKay 2014](https://pure.york.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/bits-or-shots-in-combat-the-generalized-deitchman-model-of-guerri/)). [VERIFIED] The notes' computed values give a parity of 0.500 at mu near 0, 0.605 at 0.1, 0.815 at 0.5, 0.931 at 0.8 and 1.000 at 1, so the required kill-rate multiplier falls from 2.00 to 1.65, 1.23, 1.07 and 1.00, reproducing Kress's quoted "more than 65%," "less than 25%" and "less than 8%." [ESTIMATED] Kress states that "a small increase in kill-rate is more valuable than an equivalent proportional increase in target information," and Atkinson et al. conclude that more units and better weapons beat improved targeting or relaxed deployment limits unless those are poor ([Kress 2020](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/Lanchester_Models.pdf); [Atkinson, Kress and MacKay 2020](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/Atkinson_Kress_MacKay_2020.pdf)). [VERIFIED] Kaplan, Kress and Szechtman generalize the acquisition factor to an arbitrary per-shot probability p(R), with parity beta B0^2 = 2 rho times the integral of x/p(x) over 0 to R0, which recovers both Deitchman (p = x/R0) and aimed fire (p = 1) ([Kress 2020](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/Lanchester_Models.pdf)). [VERIFIED as stated by Kress; original not read] Schramm models network shocks that switch a force from aimed to area fire ([Schramm 2012](https://hdl.handle.net/10945/37797)). [VERIFIED] Schaffer (1968) added supporting weapons, morale and time-dependent coefficients, but only the abstract was read. [REPORTED]

Acquisition-time work supplies the mechanism behind these factors. Taylor and Neta describe a Lanchester coefficient as "the ratio of the expected number of kills for a given firer type against a specific target type per target-engagement cycle divided by the expected length of this target-engagement cycle," with acquisition rate proportional to the number of visible targets and a two-state line-of-sight process ([Taylor and Neta 2001](https://faculty.nps.edu/bneta/papers/LanchesterAttrition-RateCoefficientTaylor&Neta.pdf)). [VERIFIED] The notes' reduction of that form for one target type gives a kill rate alpha L/(alpha + mu + L), which saturates at a constant per-shooter rate (square law) when targets are plentiful and scales with target count (linear law) when acquisition is the bottleneck. [ESTIMATED; the printed equation was garbled and the reduction is the notes' own algebra] This is the formal sense in which "how many units can get shots off" appears in these models: as the acquisition term, not as a headcount.

Duel models treat sensing as a random time instead of a standing probability. Li and Liu's many-on-one duel with information sharing assumes defenders begin firing once the attacker is detected and that the detected attacker "will be killed before he can return fire" ([Li and Liu 2012](https://content.scirp.org/pdf/am20120600021_62499088.pdf)). [VERIFIED] The notes' own continuous-time algebra with exponential kill times gives A a win probability alpha/(alpha + beta) with no head start, rising to 0.697, 0.816 and 0.932 for an undetected head start worth 0.5, 1 and 2 mean kill times with equal rates, so the value of sensing first is the one-sided window relative to the kill time, and it disappears as a persistent multiplier once the window closes. [ESTIMATED] For alternating shots with equal hit chance p, the first shooter wins 1/(2 - p): 0.526 at p = 0.1, 0.667 at 0.5. [ESTIMATED] The notes observe that this differs structurally from Deitchman, whose advantage persists as long as the hiding does. [ESTIMATED reading]

Empirical or doctrinal surprise values are judgment calls. Dupuy's QJM gives a net combat-power factor of about 2.24 for complete surprise to 1.10 for minor, 2.20 to 1.10 in the simplified 1990 form, and 2.5 in the TNDM casualty multiplier; the effect lasts three days "declining by one-third each day," and the Dupuy Institute states Dupuy "never validated them back to his data himself" ([Dupuy Institute on surprise](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2018/06/29/the-combat-value-of-surprise/); [TNDM comparison](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2019/12/26/how-attrition-is-calculated-in-the-qjm-vs-the-tndm/)). [REPORTED] The three headline numbers come from different model versions and quantities, so no single Dupuy surprise multiplier exists, and the decay wording admits at least two readings. [AMBIGUOUS] A blog summary credits Storr with the claim that tactical surprise produced effects "greater than a 10:1 force ratio" and a 2004 study with finding that the main value of knowledge advantage lay in preventing surprise ([Dupuy Institute](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2019/04/08/dupuys-verities-surprise/)). [REPORTED; neither original read] The notes found no independent empirical ambush casualty-exchange statistics and no source that integrates time-varying revelation of the hidden side into a Lanchester system beyond Schaffer's time-dependent coefficients.

## The naval salvo model replaces squared concentration with the first effective salvo

Naval officers quantified concentration before Lanchester. Chase's secret 1902 Naval War College paper derived fighting strengths alpha a1 A^2 against beta b1 B^2 under the square-law condition that "every engaged ship is able to fire at every engaged enemy ship," adding a staying-power term Lanchester lacked; Hughes notes it "foreshadows" Lanchester and Osipov ([Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content)). [VERIFIED] Chase's worked example, eight ships against seven, leaves sqrt(15) = 3.873 ship-equivalents before a further engagement. [VERIFIED as quotation; arithmetic ESTIMATED] Fiske's 1905 prize essay used discrete salvo tables and concluded that the side with more big guns bearing gains "a disproportionate and accelerating advantage," and that the side that fires first gains a further advantage, as Baudry's example of four minutes of unopposed fire shows ([MacKay, Price and Wood 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/99759/2/Jutland_PostReview.pdf)). [VERIFIED] Baudry states the ratio directly: two ships against one gives "the proportional rates of attrition ... in the ratio 1:4, not 1:2." [VERIFIED] Jellicoe wrote to Lanchester in June 1916 that "your N-square law has become famous in the Grand Fleet." [VERIFIED] The month of Fiske's essay is disputed between sources (March per Hughes, January per a USNI index known only from a search summary). [AMBIGUOUS] Crossing the T is the geometric device that makes the square-law premise asymmetric: the crossing line's broadsides bear while the enemy "can only reply initially with forward guns" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossing_the_T)). [REPORTED] MacKay, Price and Wood describe Jutland doctrine as "Applied Geometry," in which long gun range removed the need to mass ships and made the goal a geometry where all one's big guns bear while the enemy's do not ([MacKay et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/99759/2/Jutland_PostReview.pdf)). [VERIFIED] The notes' own illustration with 6 ships, 10-gun broadsides and 4 guns bearing forward gives 60 guns against 4 at the instant of crossing, a ratio decaying as the enemy turns; the notes label this their arithmetic, not a sourced result. [ESTIMATED] Hughes, via Beall, says crossing the T "was more often a consequence of accident than design." [VERIFIED]

Hughes's salvo model (1995; first developed in Fleet Tactics, 1986) is discrete and linear: Delta_A = (beta B - a3 A)/a1 and Delta_B = (alpha A - b3 B)/b1, where alpha and beta are well-aimed missiles per unit per salvo, a1 and b1 are hits needed to put a unit out of action, a3 and b3 are missiles destroyed by a unit's counterfire, and losses are bounded between zero and the force size ([Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content)). [VERIFIED] Because the exchange is linear in unit counts, doubling every per-unit attribute is offset by doubling numbers, not quadrupling them: Hughes states that if A's striking, staying and defensive power are all twice B's, "B will achieve parity of outcome if it has twice as many units," which the notes confirmed algebraically. [VERIFIED; algebra ESTIMATED] Instability, meaning that the side with greater combat potential can lose or be badly damaged, "arise[s] as the combat power of the forces grows relative to their survivability," and weak staying power is the likely root cause; in that regime "even a superior force must ... attack decisively first" ([Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content)). [VERIFIED] Hughes explains why guns lacked this property: battleship combat power was small relative to staying power. He says that where one unit can disable several enemy units per salvo "all classical concepts of force concentration are suspect," scouting rises in importance, and dispersal and sequential engagement become attractive, while concentration for defense still makes sense. [VERIFIED as Hughes's report of his own 1986 chapter] The model assumes simultaneous salvos and "does not provide for an advantage in scouting"; first-strike effects enter through extra terms or later extensions ([Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content)). [VERIFIED] Armstrong (2013) states that aimed-fire salvos "exhibit square law behavior" and area-fire salvos "approximately linear behavior" over multiple salvos, and that with area fire it is absolute, not relative, force size that matters ([abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/nav.21559)). [REPORTED; abstract only]

Scouting and alertness enter as multipliers. Hughes's embellishment defines scouting effectiveness sigma, between 0 and 1, as the extent to which striking power is diminished by imperfect targeting and distribution of fire, and defender alertness delta, also 0 to 1, as the reduction of counterfire from imperfect readiness; "the whole of scouting's effect on a combat outcome can be reduced to four multipliers" (sigma and delta for each side). He wants scouting to degrade defensive power too because "a surprise attack ... in salvo warfare is likely to be fatal," and he notes that command and control "is always a factor deflator, never a force multiplier" ([Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content)). [VERIFIED] The printed embellished equations were OCR-garbled, and Hughes offers no numbers for sigma or delta. In Fleet Tactics, "attack effectively first" is the sixth of six cornerstones and the "essence of tactical success"; Hughes writes that winners "have outscouted the enemy in detection, in tracking, and in targeting" and that better scouting, "more than maneuver, as much as weapons range," has determined who attacks "decisively first" ([Rocky Mountain Navy quoting the 2nd edition](https://rockymountainnavy.com/2020/02/12/wargamewednesday-using-captain-hughes-fleet-tactics-to-consider-a-modern-naval-wargame-part-5-battle-stations-an-operational-game-of-modern-seapower-simulations-canada-1984/); [Marine Corps Gazette review of the 3rd edition](https://www.mca-marines.org/wp-content/uploads/Fleet-Tactics-and-Naval-Operations-BR.pdf)). [REPORTED; no edition of the book was read] In the same review Jellicoe is said to have committed about 25% of heavy firepower to scouting at Jutland. [REPORTED] The notes found Hughes's "mathematics of diminishing enemy scouting capability" described by him as "weak and undeveloped." [VERIFIED]

Validation is mostly calibration. Hughes's anchor is that "in 1942 an attack by one carrier airwing rather consistently resulted in one enemy carrier being put out of action," and Armstrong and Powell set Coral Sea parameters so that this holds, obtaining mean losses of 1.55 US and 1.45 Japanese carriers, a 54% probability that the US loses both, and a 90% interval of 0 to 2 ships for each side, so that "anything was possible" with identical forces ([Armstrong and Powell 2005](https://www3.carleton.ca/csds/docs/working_papers/ArmstrongWP03.pdf)). [VERIFIED] The notes flag that the prose gives 1.45 for the Japanese mean while Table 3 gives 1.48, that the paper cites "Hughes, 2001" for the offense-superiority proposition while its reference list gives 2000, and that the close match to the real battle is by construction, since the authors themselves note the actual battle is the source of most inputs. [AMBIGUOUS] Their what-if runs find that more fighters at the expense of bombers (65% fighters, the 1944 level) was a net disadvantage in 1942, and that to reach 95% probability of zero US losses the US would need 21 carriers (squadron model) or 11 (aircraft model). [VERIFIED] Armstrong's stochastic salvo model (2005) reports that the deterministic and stochastic versions give similar average survivors but that the average "can be highly misleading" ([INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/opre.1040.0195)). [VERIFIED for the abstract] Armstrong (2014) develops a sequential-fire version and applies it to Coral Sea; the abstract says attacking first "would have given the American force a larger advantage than that provided by an extra aircraft carrier" ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/jors.2013.115)). [REPORTED; abstract only] The brief's "Armstrong (2014) on Midway" does not match anything the notes found: the 2014 paper treats Coral Sea, and the Midway sequential study is by Bongers and Torres, who conclude a Japanese win is "very low and indeed close to zero" in probability and that even their most favorable Japanese scenario "still would remain an American victory" ([Bongers and Torres 2017](https://theeconomics.uma.es/malagawpseries/Papers/METCwp2017-1.pdf)). [VERIFIED] Beall's 1990 thesis validated a related model on 14 twentieth-century battles, finding only 2 of 40 loss intervals significantly off, and Hughes reads it as validating only "if one knows and applies inputs that were observed in the battles after the fact" ([Beall](https://calhoun.nps.edu/handle/10945/34842); [Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content)). [VERIFIED]

Missile-era data give a graded view of what alertness is worth. Schulte's thesis tabulates hit probabilities of 0.913 against defenseless targets, 0.684 against "defendable" ones (not defended or not alert; 26 of 38) and 0.264 against defended ones (32 of 121), with post-1982 values of 0.981, 0.630 and 0.450; ships had to shoot down or deceive about three of four missiles ([Schulte 1994](https://archive.org/details/analysisofhistor00schu)). [VERIFIED] Secondary sources print 0.907, 0.286, 0.438 and 0.196 for the same table, and the notes could not trace those values to the thesis, so Schulte's primary numbers stand and the others remain unresolved. [AMBIGUOUS] In the 1973 Arab-Israeli war 19 of 50 Gabriel missiles hit and zero of 47 Styx did, a gap Schulte attributes to chaff and deception. [VERIFIED] His data come from ships under 7,000 tons and omit layered modern defenses. [VERIFIED] Two internal conflicts in the Hughes material also carry forward: Hughes writes that force B in his worked example would need 42 ships before it had survivors, whereas re-solving his Equation 1 as printed gives a threshold of 25 ([Hughes 1995](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6c38a6c-d56e-4dab-b045-8284411f8672/content)) [AMBIGUOUS; no erratum found], and Equations 2 through 6 were unrecoverable from the OCR.

## In air combat, height is stored energy, first look and initiative, with no single price

Boyd and Christie's energy-maneuverability theory puts altitude and speed on one scale. Shaw defines specific energy Es = H + V^2/(2g) (with H in feet, V in feet per second), observes that "many combinations of speed and altitude will yield the same specific energy," and gives the rate of change as specific excess power Ps = V(T - D)/W ([Shaw 1985](https://falcon.blu3wolf.com/Docs/Fighter%20Combat-Tactics%20and%20Maneuvering.pdf)). [VERIFIED for definitions; the formula images were lost in extraction, so the standard form is REPORTED by the brief and Wikipedia] The original Boyd, Christie and Gibson report (APGC-TR-66-4) was not accessible, and sources disagree on whether the work dates to 1964 or 1966 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy%E2%80%93maneuverability_theory); [migflug](https://migflug.com/afterburner/john-boyd-energy-maneuverability-theory-explained/)). [AMBIGUOUS] On this scale the notes' ideal no-drag conversion shows a fighter at 400 knots (675 ft/s, kinetic specific energy about 7,075 ft) descending 10,000 ft reaches Es of about 17,075 ft, or about 621 knots, so 10,000 ft of height is worth about 221 knots on a 400-knot start or 475 knots from a standing start, less in practice because drag acts on the dive. [ESTIMATED] The notes' dive-versus-climb arithmetic, using an assumed Ps of 100 ft/s (not a sourced figure), has regaining 10,000 ft take about 100 s and spending it about 30 s at a 30-degree dive, a ratio near 3.4 to 1. [ESTIMATED] Shaw's sourced statement is qualitative: potential energy is limited, an angles fighter that pulls hard loses energy "and, since potential energy (altitude) is limited, this energy loss eventually will mean loss of speed," and an energy fighter should convert an advantage "usually without surrendering the entire energy margin" ([Shaw 1985](https://falcon.blu3wolf.com/Docs/Fighter%20Combat-Tactics%20and%20Maneuvering.pdf)). [VERIFIED] A height edge only pays when large enough: for an effective high-side or overhead gun pass, "the altitude advantage should be about equivalent to the minimum instantaneous turn radius of the energy fighter," so a 2,000 ft turn radius calls for about 2,000 ft. [VERIFIED] The defender's rule is "work below the bogey, make it come down to engage, and try to confine the fight to the horizontal plane," and a higher-energy opponent "can be either faster or slower than the defender." [VERIFIED] Altitude also lengthens missile reach: first-generation infrared missiles reached about 2.3 nm at high altitude but under 1 nm at low altitude ([CSBA](https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/190269/Air-to-Air-Report-.pdf)). [VERIFIED]

Classical rules treat height as a means. Mannock's mantra was "always above; seldom on the same level; never underneath," and his principle was that the enemy must be surprised and attacked at a disadvantage, if possible with superior numbers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mick_Mannock); CSBA quoting Bradbeer, [CSBA](https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/190269/Air-to-Air-Report-.pdf)). [REPORTED for the mantra; VERIFIED as CSBA's quotation of Mannock's principle] Malan's rule 4 is "Height gives You the initiative" and rule 8 is "When diving to attack always leave a proportion of your formation above to act as top guard"; the rule numbering differs between a search summary and the fetched list, and Boelcke's first-version Dicta contain no explicit word for height, only "secure advantages before attacking" and "keep the sun behind you" ([Wikipedia, Ten Rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Rules_for_Air_Fighting); [Wikipedia, Dicta Boelcke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dicta_Boelcke)). [REPORTED; versions AMBIGUOUS] CSBA summarizes the WWI-era advantages as greater altitude convertible to speed, approach from up-sun, approach from blind spots (behind and below) and opening fire at short range while the defender is surprised ([CSBA](https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/190269/Air-to-Air-Report-.pdf)). [VERIFIED] The notes found no primary source for "height, sun, surprise" as a fixed doctrinal triad and treat it as folklore shorthand. Boyd's generalization, that superiority comes from operating "inside our adversaries' time scales" ([New Conception, 1976](https://slightlyeastofnew.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/03/newconception.pdf)) [VERIFIED], is contested: Storr's critique, relayed by Richards, is that "there is no OODA loop," and Richards himself reports cases where faster tempo helps the slower side ([Richards 2018](https://slightlyeastofnew.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/boyds-real-ooda-loopx.pdf)). [REPORTED]

The "most victims never saw the attacker" claim has an uneven evidence base. CSBA states that a USAF analysis of 112 Vietnam air combat engagements in the 1970s found 80% of aircrew shot down were unaware of the impending attack, and that Barkhorn estimated 80 to 90% of his own victories were against unsuspecting targets; CSBA names no study for the 112 engagements ([CSBA](https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/190269/Air-to-Air-Report-.pdf)). [VERIFIED as CSBA's statement] A HistoryNet article attributes an 81% figure ("unaware of an attack or became aware too late") to the Air Force's Red Baron study, and a blog quoting the report gives rear-quadrant first-attack tallies of 37 to 2 for MiG-17s and 9 to 5 for MiG-21s, a median F-4 and F-8 acquisition distance of about 3 miles, and four correlating factors with altitude and range weaker than quadrant ([HistoryNet](https://historynet.com/great-kill-ratio-debate/); [blog](http://elementsofpower.blogspot.com/2014/02/air-to-air-combat-over-southeast-asia.html)). [REPORTED; quotes not checked against the report] Hubbard's 1944 "90%" is quoted on a forum, where a participant calls it "picked for effect," and the same thread reports a questionnaire of about 210 American Fighter Aces Association members finding roughly 20% of kills involved surprise ([forum](https://theaerodrome.com/forum/showthread.php?t=76427)). [REPORTED; AMBIGUOUS as evidence, since the survey could not be identified and sample differences (aces versus victims) may explain the gap] The four wordings ("unaware," "never saw," "did not realize until hit," "unaware or aware too late") are not interchangeable, and the only dataset-backed figure comes from a radar-vectored, rear-quadrant Vietnam environment that may not transfer.

Detection is where altitude has formal quantitative support. CSBA's visual-search model, built on Schallhorn et al., puts cumulative detection probability below 0.50 until a target is within 1.9 to 2.8 nm and uses 2 nm as the effective uncued visual radius; a WWII head-on detection range of about 1.5 nm against a 200 m gun range and a 140-knot closure rate gives a rear attacker about 35 s from detection to firing, 21 s for a Me-262 ([CSBA](https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/190269/Air-to-Air-Report-.pdf)). [VERIFIED] The notes' arithmetic reproduces 35.8 s. [ESTIMATED] Radar horizon is 1.23 x (sqrt(antenna height in feet) + sqrt(target height in feet)) nautical miles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_horizon)) [REPORTED], which the notes compute as about 426 nm for both at 30,000 ft, about 225 nm for 30,000 ft against 100 ft (matching CSBA's E-3 low-flyer figure, which suggests the horizon limited that figure), and about 24.6 nm for both at 100 ft. [ESTIMATED] CSBA also cites AWACS detections at about 225 nm against low flyers in 1991 and Gulf War data in which 16 of 33 fixed-wing engagements were beyond visual range ([CSBA](https://www.files.ethz.ch/isn/190269/Air-to-Air-Report-.pdf)). [VERIFIED] The modern mantra "First Look, First Shot, First Kill" appears in both CSBA and a 2008 RAND briefing ([RAND](https://www.aereo.jor.br/wp-content/uploads//2016/02/2008_RAND_Pacific_View_Air_Combat_Briefing.pdf)). [VERIFIED] Missile engagement zones depend on "relative altitudes, and geometry" and launch altitude and speed set the energy available to the missile ([Dantas et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.04474)). [REPORTED]

Doctrine for formations assigns altitude layers different jobs. Shaw says wingmen cover each other's blind zones, and that "large vertical separations between fighters can reduce defensive capability, since the lower aircraft may not be able to climb up quickly to the level of the wingman to render assistance without losing so much airspeed that it becomes ineffective," so higher-performance fighters afford larger altitude splits; larger splits give "better offensive potential" because enemies are "less likely to see both aircraft simultaneously" at the price of reduced defensive capability ([Shaw 1985](https://falcon.blu3wolf.com/Docs/Fighter%20Combat-Tactics%20and%20Maneuvering.pdf)). [VERIFIED] In escort geometry, "rear-element altitude is typically somewhat higher than that of the strike group to provide greater potential energy for quick reaction" ([Shaw 1985](https://falcon.blu3wolf.com/Docs/Fighter%20Combat-Tactics%20and%20Maneuvering.pdf)). [VERIFIED] The Thach weave is a lateral, not vertical, mutual-support geometry, effective because of the F4F's firepower and a wide line-abreast separation greater than the fighter's turn radius ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thach_Weave); Shaw). [VERIFIED for Shaw's account; history REPORTED] The notes found no formal model with an explicit altitude term (reward function, differential game or Lanchester variant), no source giving a single closed-form value of height, no source on five-layer doctrine, and no source quantifying how often a top-cover element changed outcomes.

## Conclusion

Read together, the five literatures suggest that "how many units can get shots off" is the right variable in form but that it enters differently in each model, and the difference matters more than the label. In Lanchester it is the number engaged and the targets each shooter can acquire per unit time, which is why a cap on engaged units and the shrinkage of a hidden side's target density each push the law toward linear; in Deitchman and its successors it is the acquisition term p(R) or mu; in Hughes it is hits delivered per salvo net of counterfire, with scouting multipliers that can zero out the other side's reply; in air combat it is the first look plus the energy to convert it into a firing position. Only the first two predict squared returns to numbers, and Hughes's salvo model deliberately does not: its returns are linear in unit counts and its instability comes from truncation and weak staying power, while Armstrong's multi-salvo results restore square-law behavior for aimed fire and approximate linearity for area fire.

The evidence also has a consistent asymmetry between theory and test. The mathematics of frontage caps, hidden-side advantage (bounded at 2x in kill rate under the standard normalization) and sequential arrival is internally consistent and in several places verified in primary sources, yet historical validation is weak (no basic law fits two-sided data well, and Hartley and Helmbold find square-law effects "largely masked"), the RTS prediction work is position-blind, the ambush and altitude values rest largely on judgment (Dupuy's surprise factors) or contested counts (the 80 to 90% unaware figures), and the notes found no source that combines layered height, visibility and frontage in one model. The open items the notes carry forward are Osipov's actual equations and exponent, Deitchman's body text and the Hughes 42-versus-25 discrepancy, the unresolved Schulte secondary figures, the Fox 13-to-14 versus 16 ship-equivalent gap, the Dupuy surprise-multiplier versions, the unverified Red Baron and 112-engagement provenance, and the absence of any published Lanchester-style analysis of chokes, high ground or vertical layers in real-time-strategy games.
