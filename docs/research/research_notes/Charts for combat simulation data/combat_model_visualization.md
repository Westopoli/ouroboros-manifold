## 1. Lanchester models: phase-plane / state-space plots, strength-versus-time curves, force-ratio versus outcome plots

### Takeaway
Textbook Lanchester presentations use three distinct pictures. (a) Two survivor-versus-time curves on one set of axes, used to show who is wiped out and with how many survivors. (b) A state-space plot with one side's strength on each axis and the parity line (a straight line for the square law, a separatrix) splitting the plane into "X wins" and "Y wins" regions, with trajectories as arrows or curves. (c) A parameter-plane map (axes are ratios of the inputs, not unit counts) where the shaded region is "who wins" and sub-regions show differences between law variants. I found explicit figure text for (a) and (c) and for a state-space plot in a three-way extension; I did not find a verbatim description of the classic two-side phase-plane figure with trajectories in the notes I could read.

### Cited Findings
- Survivors-versus-time curve (Washburn Figure 3, "A square law battle"). Per the extracted figure text: y-axis "survivors" (tick marks 0 to 1200), x-axis "time" (0 to 2), two curves labeled "x-type" and "y-type". The accompanying text gives the parameters x0 = 1000, y0 = 800, alpha = 0.8, beta = 0.9, and says the figure shows "y is eliminated entirely at time 1.5 in spite of the fact that beta > alpha". The figure comes from a spreadsheet sheet "SquareLaw" built "to permit easy variation of parameters to explore sensitivity to initial conditions and firing rates." Winner is the curve that stays above zero; survivors for the winner are read off the end of its curve (xf = 529 stated in text). The note that the side with the higher kill coefficient still loses is the main thing the reader is meant to see. — [Washburn](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf); VERIFIED (text and axis labels; the curve shapes are ESTIMATED from the equations).
- Fit-to-history time curve (Washburn Figure 2, "Lanchester model of the Iwo Jima battle"). Text says "the theoretical and actual graphs of x vs time are shown in Figure 2", with a second, upper curve for a different definition of x ("U.S. troops alive" instead of "not killed, wounded, or missing"), and "the fit in both cases is quite good". Used to show a model curve overlaid on historical data points. — [Washburn](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf); VERIFIED (text); layout of points versus curve is ESTIMATED.
- The "crossplot" (state space with time eliminated). Washburn derives dy/dx = alpha x / (beta y), giving the invariant beta(y^2 - y0^2) = alpha(x^2 - x0^2), and sets an exercise: "Construct a 'crossplot' of y as a function of x from Figure 3; that is, eliminate time and plot y as a function of x." This is the phase-plane trajectory built from the same battle as the time plot. — [Washburn](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf); VERIFIED.
- The winner rule is stated as a comparison of two numbers: "the outcome in a square law battle is determined by a comparison of beta*y0^2 with alpha*x0^2, these quantities are sometimes referred to as 'fighting strengths'", and the same notes explain the numerical dominance with a dilution argument (a new unit fires, and it dilutes enemy fire). Final survivors: if beta*y0^2 <= alpha*x0^2 then yf = 0 and xf = sqrt(x0^2 - (beta/alpha) y0^2). The parity case is beta*y0^2 = alpha*x0^2; this is the line a phase-plane plot draws. — [Washburn](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Parameter-plane winning-region map (MG Figure 2.1). Caption (verbatim in substance): "Winning combinations of (initial) numerical disadvantage ratio n0/m0 and fighting ability ratio alpha_m/alpha_n for Side 1 in the (n0/m0)-(alpha_m/alpha_n) plane. In the darker shaded region, Side 1 is eliminated. Above the dashed curve, Side 1 wins only by the linear law in the unshaded region marked L or by the square law in the unshaded region marked S; above this curve, Side 1 wins by either law. The lighter shading indicates where Side 1 wins either way, but there are more survivors according to the linear law." Axis labels on the page extract: vertical axis "alpha_m/alpha_n" with a tick at 4. A footnote says the figure is "a quadrant of the r-k plane" with r = n0/m0 and k = alpha_m/alpha_n, and gives the boundary curves analytically (win condition k > r^2 for the square law, k > r for the linear law; the lighter regions are bounded by a curve with an upper and a lower branch). Reader takeaway stated in text: if Side 1 is heavily outnumbered it should prefer positional or one-to-one fire (linear law) because it can only win there; if it is greatly favored numerically it should prefer concentrated fire (square law) because the required fighting-ability ratio drops from r to r^2. Used in a mathematics-of-war course to pick the mode of battle. — [MG notes](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf); VERIFIED (caption, footnote, surrounding text); shapes of the shading are ESTIMATED.
- Winning regions in the two-side state space and a one-dimensional slice (KCFGS Figure 2). Caption: "The figure depicts a typical example of model (5). Two solutions (dashed black curves), starting at the 1-simplex are plotted. For the solution starting in W1 side 1 is the winner and for the solution starting in W0 combatant 0 is the winner. The total extinction line (black solid line) separates the entire phase space I0 x I1. The total extinction point (black dot) splits the 1-simplex into the two winning regions W0 and W1." In words: a plane with side 0's strength on one axis and side 1's on the other; a straight solid line through the origin is the separatrix (the stable path); two dashed trajectories start on either side and run to opposite axes; a diagonal "1-simplex" (I0 + I1 = 1) crosses the separatrix at a dot that marks the break-even split. Text gives the break-even split as I0 = 1/(1+Gamma) with Gamma = sqrt(a0/a1) and says the characterization relies "on the geometric property that a line separates the plane." The paper's proof also states "the stable path separates the phase space into invariant regions." — [KCFGS](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/lanchester3dorl.pdf); VERIFIED (caption and text).
- Three-way extension (KCFGS Figures 1, 3, 4, 5, 6). Figure 3(a): "the winning regions Wi, i = 0, 1, 2 are plotted. These regions are separated by the total-extinction curves (black). Each region is divided into two subregions Wij, j != i, where j is the second survivor of stage one. These are separated by the iso-extinction curves (gray)." Figure 3(b) shows the same as surfaces in a three-axis phase space. The state space is restricted to a triangle (unit 2-simplex) so the plot is a 2-D triangle colored by who wins, which is the three-player analogue of the two-player line split. Figure 4 is a sensitivity analysis: panel (a) plots "the area (in relative size) for the three winning regions" against the value of attack-intensity parameter a01 over [0.01, 100], while panels (b) and (c) show the winning-region triangle at a01 = 0.1 and 100. The text also names region types: where one side always wins, where a side wins only if it allocates fire wisely, and where a side "can be 'king maker'." The abstract says solution paths are "calculated and visualized in appropriate phase portraits." Used to show how much of the starting-strength space each side owns, and how that area shifts when one rule parameter changes. — [KCFGS](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/lanchester3dorl.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Force-versus-time curves in a stochastic-combat teaching context are also used by Washburn: the notes later describe a transition diagram, "all transitions are either 'down' or 'left'," on an (m, n) grid of unit counts for the probabilistic version, with a probability p(m,n) in each cell, and note a billion-cell grid (20,000 x 50,000) would be needed for an Iwo Jima model, concluding that the deterministic equations are "usually adequate when the forces involved are large; it is problems with a small number of units that are most in need of a probabilistic treatment." — [Washburn](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf); VERIFIED (text); the grid diagram's look is ESTIMATED.
- Wikipedia's Lanchester article has one illustrative figure whose caption reads "Idealized simulation of two forces damaging each other neglecting all other circumstances than the 1) Size of army 2) Rate of damaging. The picture illustrates the principle of Lanchester's square law." The summarizer found no phase-plane or force-ratio graphs on that page. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanchester%27s_laws); REPORTED.
- MacKay's "Lanchester combat models" (University of York, May 2005; 8 pages) as extracted contains no figure captions or plot descriptions at all; it is equations only. — [MacKay, arXiv math/0606300](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0606300); VERIFIED (absence in extracted text; I did not view the page images).
- Kress's treatment of Lanchester in "Combat Modeling" (Washburn and Kress 2009) is cited by KCFGS but I could not read that book. — [KCFGS](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/lanchester3dorl.pdf); REPORTED that the book exists.

### Inferences
- ESTIMATED: The classic two-plot pairing is "time curves show one battle; the state-space plot shows all battles at once, with the parity line as the answer to who wins." Washburn's exercise to derive the crossplot from the time plot is the clearest textual link between the two.
- ESTIMATED: The parameter-plane map (MG Figure 2.1) is the Lanchester-literature form closest to "which rule setting makes quality beat numbers": its two axes are exactly a numbers ratio and a fighting-ability ratio, and the region boundary is the break-even.
- ESTIMATED: KCFGS Figure 4(a) (area of each side's winning region against one rule parameter) is a compact way to show "how a rule setting changes who owns the start-state space" with a single curve.

## 2. Data farming and agent-based combat simulation: chart forms for many runs over many factors

### Takeaway
The NPS/Project Albert authors say the goal of exploratory runs is to find "regions, ranges and thresholds where interesting things happen," and they use a small, repeated toolkit: regression trees (their stated favorite for communicating to decision makers), main-effect and relative-importance bar charts, interaction plots drawn as a grid of tiny sub-plots, contour plots of two factors, trellis plots (small multiples) conditioned on one factor, 3-D surface plots driven by an interactive tool with sliders, pairwise scatter-plot matrices of the design itself, and a factor-significance table with M/S/NS cells. They also warn that one-run visuals and a handful of interior sample points are dangerous.

### Cited Findings
- Stated purpose of exploration. "The goal of exploratory analysis is to gain a better understanding of the system, by identifying significant factors and interactions, as well as finding regions, ranges and thresholds where interesting things happen," contrasted with "predicting, optimizing, or tuning." The models, designs and analysis methods should "organize debates, efficiently uncover new insights, and effectively communicate the findings to decision makers." — [CLS04](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc04papers/020.pdf); VERIFIED.
- General visual guidance. "Visualization is also extremely helpful—and perhaps better suited for exploratory investigations. Box and whisker plots, bar plots, trellis plots or other small multiples (Tufte 2001), surface and contour graphs, and other graphical methods can provide the analyst with useful information that may not be easy to quantify." Also: "relying on visual results of one (or several) runs is dangerous." — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Regression tree, SL02 Figure 4 "Regression Tree Model of the Proportion of Blue Casualties from an Investigation of Guerilla Combat" (hilltop defense by Blue against Red guerrillas; 51,300 responses over 22 factors from nearly-orthogonal Latin hypercubes). Each internal node is a threshold split on one input ("Red stealth less than 111.5", "number of Red agents less than 24.5", "reconnaissance stealth less than 108.5"); the leaf shows the predicted proportion of Blue casualties; the text reads one root-to-leaf path aloud ("Blue takes very low casualties ... no matter what values the other parameters take"). Stated reason: "Regression trees have proven beneficial in understanding and communicating the results of thousands of runs over many factors. Regression trees are more human-readable and can be easier to understand than multiple regression models. Trees simply show the structure in the data." — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED (text; the drawn tree layout is ESTIMATED).
- Regression tree with traffic-light leaves, CLS04 Figure 2 "Regression Tree for Blue Casualties" (MANA study of a future-force company-level attack with communication factors; more than 50,000 runs, nearly a score of variables). Redrawn from the extracted figure text: root "All Data"; first split "Range > 75% of Battlespace" versus "Range < 75% of Battlespace" (the second branch ends in a red leaf); the first branch splits on "Latency < 1 min" versus "Latency > 1 min" (the latter a yellow leaf); the first splits again on "Sufficient Capacity" (green leaf) versus "Insufficient Capacity" (red leaf). Text: "These findings are summarized graphically in the regression tree of Figure 2. We have found these displays useful for succinctly presenting the results to decision-makers. The green, yellow, and red boxes at the bottom of the leaves correspond to favorable, intermediate, and unfavorable outcomes for Blue forces, respectively. The graph has been simplified from the statistical software output, which also provides the number of data points in each leaf (cluster), as well as their mean and standard deviation." So the briefing version drops counts, means and standard deviations and replaces the leaf number by a colored three-level label. — [CLS04](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc04papers/020.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Relative-importance bar chart, SL02 Figure 5, "Relative Importance of 22 Factors in an Investigation of Guerilla Combat": a bar chart over 22 factors computed with MART; "the relative importance of the variables ... can be displayed in a simple bar chart." The text then says the bar chart shows Red tactics are important and the trellis plot "provides more insight on why this is so." — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Trellis plot, SL02 Figure 7, "Trellis Plot of the Proportions of Red vs. Blue Casualties, Conditioned on the Number of Red Agents": a grid of small scatter panels, one per band of the number of Red agents, with proportion of Red casualties against proportion of Blue casualties in each. Reader takeaway from the text: in the upper-left panel (the band of 19 to 27 Red agents) "Red inflicts high Blue losses while suffering low casualties," so Red can negate Blue's advantage in firepower and number by infiltrating with that number. Used because a bar chart said "Red tactics matter" and the trellis showed why. — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED (text; which panel is which is from the text).
- Pairwise-projection scatter matrices of the design, SL02 Figures 2 and 3. Figure 2 "Pairwise Projections of Scaled Factor Levels for a Five-Factor Second-Order Frequency-Based Design" (81 design points; frequencies 1/81, 4/81, 10/81, 17/81, 29/81) and Figure 3 "... for a Five-Factor Latin Hypercube Design": each is a matrix of scatter panels, one per pair of factors, points uniformly scattered for the Latin hypercube. Text contrasts: a 3^(5-1) fractional factorial would show "only nine combinations ... one at each corner, one at the center of each side, and only one in the middle" per panel, a 9^5 factorial a regular 9 by 9 grid of 81 points. Purpose is to show how well the runs fill the space, not the results. — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Interaction plot as a grid of tiny sub-plots, CLS04 Figure 3 "Interaction Plot for the Interceptor Model" (Pythagoras study of unmanned surface vehicles; response is mean proportion of enemies killed, axis ticks -0.1 to 0.8). Extracted figure text shows a matrix with row and column factors USV speed (2 and 40 knots shown), range from the high-value unit (nm), camera range (nm), and simulation length (minutes). Caption text: "The tiny sub-plots depict the four significant interactions involving USV speed, combat radius, sensor range, and endurance. (Curves indicate quadratic effects.) For example, the top row shows that with a USV speed of 2 knots, increasing the sensor range has a slight negative effect on performance. In contrast, when the USV speed is 40 knots, then increasing the camera range is quite beneficial." — [CLS04](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc04papers/020.pdf); VERIFIED (text and axis ticks; panel arrangement is ESTIMATED from extracted text).
- Contour plot, CLS04 Figure 4 "Contour Plot for the Interceptor Model": USV speed against endurance, shading for detection proportion. Text: "The darker red areas to the upper right represent high detection proportions, while the left and lower portions of the plot correspond to low detection probabilities. If either USV speed or USV endurance are low, then so is the detection probability. Improving only one will not appreciably improve performance. To achieve reasonable detection probabilities, both speed and endurance must be sufficiently high. This is but one instance of a situation where 'more' is not necessarily 'better.'" The authors add that diminishing returns (more USVs help up to a point) "is important" for trade-offs between cost and numbers. — [CLS04](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc04papers/020.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Interactive surface-plot tool, SL02 Figure 6 "Screenshot Including Multiple Performance Measures from the Project Albert Visualization Toolkit": "The x and y axes can be set to any two of the factors varied during the search. Slider bars for all other factors allow the analyst to quickly scan through and see how the response surfaces change. One can add or delete response surfaces for multiple performance measures; the surfaces themselves can represent performance means, standard deviations, or quantiles. Note that plotting minima and maxima has proven useful for identifying unexpected behaviors." CLS04 adds that combining this tool with standard stats packages "and interactively watching many scenarios unfold, has proven an effective means of generating insights." Meyer and Johnson 2001 ("Visualization for data farming") is the cited tool reference. — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf), [CLS04](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc04papers/020.pdf); VERIFIED.
- "Region where Blue always wins," SL02 Figure 8 "Triangular Region Depicting Outcomes Always Favorable to Blue" (from a study of information-system latencies, 11 factors, 2002 runs from 22 Latin hypercubes of 91 points per factor, region found by focusing on variables a neural network flagged in Clementine). Text: "the Blue force can ensure a positive outcome if it can control two of its process latencies—regardless of the values of the nine other factors." Shape per caption: a triangle in a 2-factor plane. — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED (caption and text); the axes are not named in the text I read.
- Factor-significance table, CLS04 Table 1 "Model Comparison for Squad Size Study": rows are factors or factor pairs (squad size, number of squads, ARV armor thickness, ARV speed, ARV weapon max range, plus interactions such as "Squad Firing Rates & Squad Size", and "Scheme of Maneuver"), columns are three simulations (MANA, Pythagoras, JANUS), each cell one of M = Most Significant, S = Significant, NS = Not Significant, NA. Used to show that three models of different resolution agree on which factors matter. — [CLS04](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc04papers/020.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Line plot of an output against time for different movement rules, SL02 Figure 1 "Blue Losses vs. Time to Complete Mission for Various Penalty Functions Determining Movement" (adapted from Gill and Shi 2002), used to show that the same scenario run under different movement-control logic gives different results on two performance measures. — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED (caption and one sentence); curve shapes unknown.
- Statements on avoiding statistics-heavy explanation. "Gridded designs are straightforward, and probably the easiest to explain to someone unfamiliar with the concepts of DOE and statistical analysis," but they explode in size (a 10^9 factorial needs a billion runs per replication). Trees are described as "more human-readable" than regression. — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Caution about sparse sampling. "Since even millions of runs constitute a sparse sample in a high-dimensional space, we must collect our data intelligently"; a model with 100 two-level factors has about 10^30 combinations. Also: "simply generating large volumes of data can easily overwhelm most post-processing analytic tools." — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf), [CLS04](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc04papers/020.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Horne and Meyer (NPS) frame data farming as examining "whole landscapes of potential outcomes" and as "an aid to decision-makers"; "Data Farming focuses on a more complete landscape of possible system responses and progressions, rather than attempting to pinpoint an answer. This 'big picture' solution landscape is an invaluable aid to the decision maker." They list "Data Analysis and Visualization" as the sixth of six domains and say the volume of output required "visualization tools and methods commensurate with this tremendous amount of data", with visualization and rapid prototyping important from about 1999-2000. Their worked example is a social-network scenario with spatial, "inclination space", static-graph and dynamic-graph views and centrality time series, not a combat-outcome chart. — [Horne and Meyer, Data Farming and Defense Applications](https://nps.edu/documents/106696734/108129281/MODSIMPaper-HorneMeyer.pdf/d9189c42-db75-4479-8f98-78e5c00fada8); VERIFIED (extracted text).
- A 2015 WSC guideline paper on agent-based simulation studies summarizes Sanchez and Lucas as emphasizing "visual guides for gaining insight into the one-way relationships as well as internal interactions of various factors, such as regression trees, 3-dimensional surface plots, neural networks." — [Guidelines for design and analysis in agent-based simulation studies, WSC 2015](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc15papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED (that it says so); the claim about SL02 is REPORTED.
- MANA, Pythagoras, ISAAC/EINSTein are the platforms; CLS04 notes the Project Albert models are "by design, easy to set up, quick-running, and suitable for data-farming," typically seconds to minutes per run. — [CLS04](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc04papers/020.pdf); VERIFIED.

### Inferences

No source says "do not show a scatter plot," but none of the briefing statements name scatter plots either.
- ESTIMATED: SL02 Figure 2/3 (pairwise projections) is a design-coverage plot rather than a results plot; it is not recommended for decision makers anywhere I read.
- ESTIMATED: The M/S/NS table in CLS04 is a heat-map-table in the sense of one categorical shade or letter per cell; I did not see color information in the extracted text.

### Gaps

- I could not read Meyer and Johnson 2001 (visualization for data farming), Kleijnen et al. 2005 "A user's guide to the brave new world of designing simulation experiments", Lucas et al. 2015 "Changing the paradigm: simulation, now a method of first resort", or Ilachinski's "Artificial War" and EINSTein papers, so their chart recommendations are not in these notes.

## 3. Loss-exchange-ratio and force-exchange-ratio charts; casualty curves; winning probability versus force ratio; break-even curves

### Takeaway
The historical-data side (Dupuy Institute) uses tables of force ratio bands against outcome and failure rates, scatter plots of exchange ratio against force ratio that show a hyperbola-shaped cloud with no clear relationship, and a chart of attacker success percentage against force ratio from a Concepts Analysis Agency study. The simulation side reports exchange ratios as an outcome measure (loss exchange ratio; force exchange ratio) and shows non-monotone winner maps over initial force size (Dewar model). The sharpest example for a deterministic simulator is the Dewar-model figure, where the winner as a function of both starting sizes is not a clean step.

### Cited Findings
- Winner map over both initial force sizes (SL02 Figure 9, "Winning as a Function of Initial Force Strengths in the Deterministic Dewar Model"; Vinyard and Lucas 2001; billions of runs on a deterministic combat model of Dewar, Gillogly and Juncosa 1996). Text: "the x and y axes represent the initial size of the Red and Blue forces, respectively." The expectation was that raising one side's size while holding the other constant gives a single step from one winner to the other. "This is true in some instances. The horizontal line at a Blue initial force level B0 = 800 shows a single change from Blue winning to Red winning once the initial Red strength crosses a threshold. However, the line at B0 = 450 shows an oscillation of winners (as R0 increases) over an extended range." The picture was "determined by a gridded sample of 69,451 points." Warning: "extreme non-monotonicity might go unnoticed, even when it exists, if samples are taken at only a few interior points." Figure 10 ("A Maelstrom of Non-Monotonicity in Four Additional Performance Subspaces of the Dewar Model") shows four more subspaces; "of the nine subspaces investigated, five exhibit pervasive non-monotonicity, with it showing up in over 80% of the surfaces examined." Cause: "Palmore (1996) showed that the non-monotonicity is caused by the chaotic battle trace. These results caused quite a stir in the Defense Modeling and Simulation world." The figure is described as a two-factor plane (Red size on one axis, Blue size on the other) with the winner at each point; the layout is ESTIMATED from the text, which does not state colors. — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED (text).
- Original Dewar et al. reference: Dewar, J., J. Gillogly, M. Juncosa. 1996. "Non-monotonicity, chaos, and combat models." Military Operations Research 2(2): 37-49. I did not read it. — cited in [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED (that the reference exists as cited).
- Force ratio versus exchange ratio scatter plots. The Dupuy Institute post "Comparing Force Ratios to Casualty Exchange Ratios" presents eight scatter plots with force ratio (attackers/defenders) on x and exchange ratio (attacker casualties/defender casualties) on y, using 243 pre-1900 battles and 628 engagements from 1904-1991; data cluster in the lower-left with extreme outliers forming "a hyperbola", and even truncated at about 5-to-1 or 6-to-1 they show "no clear patterns." Conclusion quoted: "there does not appear to be a direct cause and effect between force ratios and exchange ratios." The author uses the plots to reject three doctrinal theories about casualty ratios. — [Dupuy Institute 2018-12-14](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2018/12/14/comparing-force-ratios-to-casualty-exchange-ratios/); REPORTED (read through a summarizing tool; counts and claims not independently checked).
- Force-ratio band tables. The Dupuy Institute presents a table with four columns: Force Ratio (a band such as "1.15 to 1.88-to-1"), Result (a verbal label such as "Attack usually succeeds", "Attacker advances", "Attack fails"), Percent Failure, and Number of cases. For 1944 European-theater division-level engagements: 0.55-1.01:1 attack fails, 100% failure, 5 cases; 1.15-1.88:1 "attack usually succeeds", 21% failure, 48 cases; 1.95-2.56:1 "attack usually succeeds", 10%, 21 cases; 2.71:1 and higher "attacker advances", 0%, 42 cases. The author's stated purpose for the table form is to show "what the norms are" at each force-ratio threshold; the same format is applied by theater (e.g. German-Soviet 1943 data; Pacific theater U.S. attacks needing a lower ratio). — [Dupuy Institute 2018-04-25](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2018/04/25/u-s-army-force-ratios/), [2020-12-26](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2020/12/26/more-combat-results-tables-from-war-by-numbers/); REPORTED (summarizer).
- Planning-ratios table (doctrine). U.S. Army planning-ratio table (ATP 5-0.2, July 2019) lists mission against friendly:enemy ratio (hasty defend 1:2.5, deliberate defend 1:3, hasty attack 2.5:1, deliberate attack 3:1, delay 1:6, counterattack 1:1, penetration 18:1). The Dupuy Institute argues the table gives the impression "you need to have three-to-one odds to attack" and that WWII attacks at 1.00-1.49:1 won 63% of the time. — [Dupuy Institute 2019-11-14](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2019/11/14/the-source-of-the-u-s-army-three-to-one-rule/); REPORTED.
- Outcome-by-ratio chart "CAA, Battle Outcome vs. Force Ratio". A chart in Dupuy's post is described as attacker force ratio on the x-axis against battle outcome success rate (percent) on the y-axis, from the U.S. Army Concepts Analysis Agency's 598 battles 1600-1973; reported values: attackers with 3-to-1 or better succeeded about 74% of the time; between 1.5-to-1 disadvantage and under 3-to-1 advantage they won 58-63%; only 106 of 598 cases (17.7%) reached 3-to-1. I did not see the chart; the summarizer's axis description is the only evidence. — [Dupuy Institute 2016-07-11](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2016/07/11/trevor-dupuy-and-the-3-1-rule/); AMBIGUOUS (chart form described second-hand; values REPORTED).
- Campaign-level comparison table. Starting-strength ratio for attacker victories 2.28-to-1 versus defender victories 1.56-to-1; casualty ratio 0.66-to-1 for winning attackers versus 1.08-to-1 for losing attackers; table organized by outcome category (attacker victory, defender victory, draw) with strengths, casualties, percent loss per day. — [Dupuy Institute 2024-04-23](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2024/04/23/analysis-for-force-ratios-using-the-campaign-data-base-cadb/); REPORTED.
- Loss-exchange ratio as an output measure in simulation: the squad-size study used "loss exchange ratio (LER) and time to mission completion" as its measures of effectiveness (CLS04). A separate study "constructed a metamodel of the force exchange ratio" as a function of 22 factors over 129 levels (SL02, on Cioppa 2002). — [CLS04](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc04papers/020.pdf), [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED. The text I read does not describe an LER chart form beyond the factor-significance table and the trees above.
- Break-even in two parameters in Lanchester form is MG Figure 2.1 (numbers ratio against fighting-ability ratio) and the KCFGS separatrix; see section 1.

### Inferences
- ESTIMATED: Dupuy-style force-ratio tables turn a continuous relationship into bands with a verbal label, a failure rate and a case count, so the reader can see both the pattern and how thin the evidence is in each band.
- ESTIMATED: A hyperbola-shaped scatter of exchange ratio against force ratio is what one would expect when a ratio is plotted against a ratio over wide ranges; the Dupuy post reads it as noise, and I did not see any source recommend a log scale, though none ruled it out.
- ESTIMATED: The Dewar figure is the closest published analogue to a deterministic toy simulator mapped over starting sizes: it shows that a coarse sweep of one factor can look like a clean threshold while a fine grid shows alternating winners.

### Gaps
- I did not find a published "winning probability versus force ratio" curve (smooth sigmoid) in the sources read; the Dupuy material is banded tables and a chart I could not see.
- Loss-exchange-ratio line charts against force size in simulation studies: none located.
- Casualty curves (cumulative casualties versus time) were not found in the sources read except the Washburn survivors-versus-time curve.

## 4. Wargaming and defense briefing conventions for non-specialist decision makers: stoplight matrices, "so what" charts, radar charts, heat-map tables

### Takeaway
Within the NPS data-farming papers the briefing form is the simplified regression tree with green/yellow/red leaf boxes and a plain-English reading; the general-literature criticisms of radar charts and red-amber-green matrices are well documented. I did not find a defense-specific style guide in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Green/yellow/red as outcome categories on a decision tree: the CLS04 tree leaves carry green, yellow and red boxes for "favorable, intermediate, and unfavorable outcomes for Blue forces", and the authors say these displays are "useful for succinctly presenting the results to decision-makers." — [CLS04](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc04papers/020.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Plain-English reading under each figure: every CLS04 and SL02 figure is followed by a sentence of the form "if A is low and B is low then outcome is poor; improving only one will not appreciably improve performance." Example: "This is but one instance of a situation where 'more' is not necessarily 'better.'" — [CLS04](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc04papers/020.pdf); VERIFIED.
- CLS04 states findings as short bullet statements next to the tree (for example: communication range is the factor the response is "most sensitive to"; a 25 percent degradation "had dramatic, negative consequences"). — [CLS04](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc04papers/020.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Radar (spider) chart criticisms as summarized from Wikipedia: axis order imposes artificial adjacency ("neighboring variables are unrelated, creating spurious connections"); "Area scales as the square of values, exaggerating the effect of large numbers" (values 2,2 occupy four times the area of 1,1); "poorly suited for making trade-off decisions when one chart is greater than another on some variables, but less on others"; "radial distances are hard to judge." — [Wikipedia: Radar chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_chart); REPORTED.
- Risk-matrix (red-amber-green) criticisms as summarized from Wikipedia: Tony Cox argues matrices "can correctly and unambiguously compare only a small fraction (e.g., less than 10%) of randomly selected pairs of hazards" and can give identical ratings to quantitatively different risks; Thomas, Bratvold and Bickel show "risk matrices produce arbitrary risk rankings" depending on bin size and scale direction. The page did not develop colour-perception critiques. — [Wikipedia: Risk matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_matrix); REPORTED.
- Small multiples: Tufte's argument as summarized, "Compared to what?"; they "visually enforce comparisons of changes, of the differences among objects, of the scope of alternatives"; identical scales and axes across panels. SL02 cites Tufte (2001, The Visual Display of Quantitative Information, 2nd ed.) for trellis plots. — [Wikipedia: Small multiple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_multiple), [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf); REPORTED (Wikipedia), VERIFIED (SL02 cites Tufte).
- Parallel coordinates reading rules as summarized: roughly parallel lines between two axes suggest a positive relationship, crossing X-shapes a negative one; each axis has at most two neighbors, so at most n-1 relationships show at once; axis order matters and clutter grows with many lines. — [Wikipedia: Parallel coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_coordinates); REPORTED.

### Inferences
- ESTIMATED: The one explicitly stated defence-analysis convention in my sources is "colored leaves on a tree, with per-leaf counts, means and standard deviations stripped out for the briefing version."
- ESTIMATED: Radar and parallel-coordinates forms were not used in any of the combat-simulation papers I read; the criticisms above come from general sources, not from defense analysts.

### Gaps
- No MORS, RAND or DTIC document on briefing conventions (stoplight matrices, "so what" annotated charts, heat-map tables) was retrieved; the web-search budget ended before these searches.
- No defense-specific criticism of spider charts was found.

## 5. Naval salvo model and stochastic combat model result presentations (Hughes, Armstrong): tables versus distributions

### Takeaway
I could not retrieve any Hughes or Armstrong primary text. The salvo model is a difference-equation model whose results are normally reported as ships lost per exchange, and Washburn's notes show the stochastic version as probabilities on a grid of survivor counts. I have no evidence on the table-versus-distribution question for the salvo literature.

### Cited Findings
- The salvo model is given as difference equations per exchange, delta A = -(beta B - y A) u subject to 0 <= -delta A <= A (and symmetrically for B), computing losses as offensive missiles minus interceptions times damage per hit; Hughes developed it at NPS (published 1995) and Armstrong extended it with stochastic, sequential-fire and historical-battle (e.g. Coral Sea) variants; the page shows no figures or numeric tables. — [Wikipedia: Salvo combat model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvo_combat_model); REPORTED.
- Washburn's treatment of the stochastic Lanchester model uses a transition diagram on an (m, n) grid, where all transitions are "down" or "left" and p(m,n) is the probability of reaching a state; the expected value of a measure of effectiveness is computed backward over the grid; the notes state that large battles make the grid enormous, so the deterministic equations are used for large forces. — [Washburn](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf); VERIFIED (text); diagram appearance is ESTIMATED.
- On sampling variability in simulation outputs: SL02 uses 100 replications per design point in several cited studies and shows output as a mean, standard deviation or quantile response surface in the interactive tool; the tool can plot "means, standard deviations, or quantiles", with minima and maxima "useful for identifying unexpected behaviors." CLS04 ran 50 replications per design point in the squad-size study. — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf), [CLS04](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc04papers/020.pdf); VERIFIED.

### Inferences
- ESTIMATED: Applying "show means, standard deviations or quantiles" gives a stochastic version of any map above (one surface per statistic).

### Gaps
- Hughes 1995 ("A salvo model of warships in missile combat used to evaluate their staying power", Naval Research Logistics) and Armstrong's papers were not read; no figure descriptions retrieved.
- No source found on tables versus full outcome distributions for salvo or stochastic combat results.

## 6. Guidance on presenting simulation results to a commander or designer without statistical training

### Takeaway
The statements I could verify are scattered and short: use a small decision tree rather than regression equations; keep gridded designs for people unfamiliar with DOE; present the landscape rather than a single number; read each plot aloud in words; do not rely on a few interior sample points or one run; beware non-monotone responses. There is no dedicated guidance document among the sources I read.

### Cited Findings
- Trees are "more human-readable and can be easier to understand than multiple regression models" and the briefing version strips counts, means and standard deviations. — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf), [CLS04](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc04papers/020.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Gridded designs are "the easiest to explain to someone unfamiliar with the concepts of DOE and statistical analysis." — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Show landscape, not a point answer: "a more complete landscape of possible system responses ... rather than attempting to pinpoint an answer." — [Horne and Meyer](https://nps.edu/documents/106696734/108129281/MODSIMPaper-HorneMeyer.pdf/d9189c42-db75-4479-8f98-78e5c00fada8); VERIFIED.
- Present thresholds and "more is not better" findings; diminishing returns are called out as knowledge that program managers need for trade-offs. — [CLS04](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc04papers/020.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Authors recommend conditioning on a key variable (trellis) when a summary chart gives a "what" but not a "why". — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Use minimum and maximum surfaces to catch unintended behavior or code problems, not only means. — [SL02](https://informs-sim.org/wsc02papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED.
- Warning on narrow factor ranges: "if a narrow range of change is imposed on an important factor, but a wide range on an unimportant factor, then the latter could appear be more important than the former (Happe 2005)." — [WSC 2015 guidelines paper](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc15papers/015.pdf); VERIFIED that the sentence is there; the Happe attribution is REPORTED.
- A 2026 arXiv survey on interpretable and explainable surrogate modeling for simulations exists (Palar et al., arXiv:2604.14240) with a title promising a section on explainable AI for decision-making; I only got a summarizer's high-level description and no usable quotes. — [arXiv:2604.14240](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.14240); AMBIGUOUS.

### Inferences
- ESTIMATED: The practice in these sources is to pair every chart with a one-sentence reading in plain language and a named decision implication (for example, how many USVs to buy).

### Gaps
- No source found that states a rule for how many panels, colors or factors a briefing chart may carry.
- No evidence from user studies of how non-statisticians read these chart forms.
- The "so what" annotated chart convention was not located in any source.
