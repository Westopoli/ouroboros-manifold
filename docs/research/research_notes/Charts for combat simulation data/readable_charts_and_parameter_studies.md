# Readable charts for non-specialists, and chart forms for comparisons and parameter studies

Scope note and method caveats (read first). The web-search tool hit its session cap partway through, so some sources could only be reached as abstracts, as Wikipedia summaries, or not at all. Each finding carries one confidence tag: VERIFIED (read in the source text or its abstract page this session), REPORTED (secondary or tertiary summary, for example Wikipedia or a tool-generated summary of a page), ESTIMATED (my inference), AMBIGUOUS (sources conflict or the claim is unclear). Where a finding is "VERIFIED (abstract only)", only the abstract was read, not the full paper. Items I could not source are listed under Gaps rather than asserted. Per the brief, nothing here designs the report.

The three charts that prompted this research are referred to only in the Inferences subsections, tagged ESTIMATED.

## 1. Graphical perception: Cleveland and McGill (1984), Heer and Bostock (2010), and what follows for bars, dots, lines and heat maps

### Takeaway
Lab and crowdsourced experiments agree that people judge position along a common scale most accurately, then length, with angle, area and colour/shading judged less accurately. Pie-style angle and bubble/treemap area encodings are worse than position or length. The practical reading is that anything the reader must compare should be placed on a shared axis, and that colour intensity is a weak carrier of exact quantity.

### Cited Findings
- Heer and Bostock replicated Cleveland and McGill's proportional-judgment experiment on Mechanical Turk. Seven judgment types: T1 to T3 position along a common scale, T4 and T5 length, T6 angle (pie chart), T7 circular area (bubble chart). Ten charts at 380 by 380 pixels, 50 subjects per chart, 70 trials. Task: identify the smaller of two marked values, then estimate "what percentage the smaller was of the larger". Accuracy measured as log2(|judged percent − true percent| + 1/8), the same measure Cleveland and McGill used. — [Heer & Bostock, CHI 2010, "Crowdsourcing Graphical Perception"](http://vis.stanford.edu/files/2010-MTurk-CHI.pdf) (also at [idl.uw.edu](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/crowdsourcing-graphical-perception)). VERIFIED (read full text)
- Result as stated: "The ranking of types by accuracy is consistent between the two experiments"; position encoding "still significantly outperformed length encoding", although length judgments were more accurate in the crowdsourced study than in the original. — Heer & Bostock 2010, same PDF. VERIFIED
- On angle and area: "psychophysical theory predicts area to perform worse than angle, and both to be significantly worse than position. Theory also suggests that angle should perform worse than length, but the results do not support this. Cleveland & McGill also did not find angle to perform worse than length." So the strict "length, then angle, then area" order is only partly supported for angle versus length. — Heer & Bostock 2010. VERIFIED
- Rectangular area (cartogram/treemap-style) judgments matched circular area judgments in accuracy. Squares (aspect ratio 1) were judged worst, which the authors read as consistent with viewers using side lengths as a proxy for area. Treemap context did not add interference versus isolated rectangles. Accuracy improved toward the extremes (true proportion near 0 or 100 percent). — Heer & Bostock 2010. VERIFIED
- Gridlines: adding gridlines improved accuracy; no significant difference between 10 and 20 gridlines or between 50 and 100; the authors suggest gridlines be separated by at least 8 pixels because dense gridlines impede tracing to labels. — Heer & Bostock 2010. VERIFIED
- Data-quality detail: verification question ("which is smaller") was answered wrongly in only 14 of 3,481 responses (0.4 percent), and a pilot without a qualification test produced over 10 percent unusable responses, i.e. even simple instructions need checking. — Heer & Bostock 2010. VERIFIED
- The Cleveland and McGill (JASA 1984) elementary-task ordering, as commonly quoted from most to least accurate: position along a common scale; position along identical non-aligned scales; length, direction, angle; area; volume and curvature; shading and colour saturation. I could not open the JASA paper (tandfonline and JSTOR blocked), so this ordering is reproduced from the literature's standard citation, not read this session. Heer & Bostock's text confirms only the position, length, angle and area parts. — [Cleveland & McGill 1984, JASA 79(387), doi:10.1080/01621459.1984.10478080](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1984.10478080). REPORTED (not read; part of it VERIFIED via Heer & Bostock as above)
- Few's summary of the same literature: area is perceived through a power law with exponent of about 0.8 (citing Kosslyn); "relative line length is perceived almost perfectly, provided that the lines are oriented the same way"; students asked to size a circle with 16 times the area of a small one answered from about 6 to 50; angle judgments are biased (acute angles underestimated, obtuse overestimated). Cleveland on pie charts: they "do not provide efficient detection of geometric objects that convey information about differences of values" (Elements of Graphing Data, p. 268). — [Few, "Save the Pies for Dessert"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/save_the_pies_for_dessert.pdf). VERIFIED (read; expert commentary quoting experiments, not new data)
- Franconeri, Padilla, Shah, Zacks and Hullman's review of research-backed guidelines for communicating to students, coworkers and the general public: the visual system quickly extracts global statistics, but "comparing between subsets of values is slow"; effective graphics "avoid taxing working memory, guide attention, and respect familiar conventions"; designers should tailor to the audience, especially viewers with low graphical literacy. — [Psychological Science in the Public Interest 2021, doi:10.1177/15291006211051956](https://doi.org/10.1177/15291006211051956) via [Europe PMC record](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/34907835). VERIFIED (abstract only)
- FT Visual Vocabulary groups chart types by task (deviation, correlation, ranking, distribution, change over time, part-to-whole, magnitude, spatial, flow). It describes a heatmap as a grid "mapping data values with an intensity color scale", i.e. colour intensity is the quantitative channel. — [FT Visual Vocabulary README, via GitHub](https://github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/blob/main/visual-vocabulary/README.md). REPORTED (page summarised by a tool; the summary listed nine categories though it called them seven)

### Inferences
- A chart that makes the reader compare two things by position on one shared scale (aligned dots, aligned bar ends, a line against a line on the same axis) sits at the top of the ranking. A chart that asks for exact values from colour intensity (heat map without numbers) or from area/angle sits at the bottom. ESTIMATED, from the ranking above.
- Dot plots versus bars: both are position or length on a common scale, so the perceptual-accuracy ranking does not separate them. Choosing between them rests on other grounds (baseline zero for bars, see section 2). ESTIMATED
- Cleveland and McGill's tasks are single judgments of marks, not whole-chart comprehension. Their ranking says nothing direct about a lay reader's ability to understand what a chart is about (see sections 2 and 3). ESTIMATED

### Gaps
- The original 1984 paper text and its full ordered list were not read.
- No source found that directly tests heat-map cell readability with versus without printed numbers.

## 2. Explanatory versus exploratory graphics, titles that state the finding, on-chart annotation, direct labels, plain-language labels

### Takeaway
Experimental evidence (Borkin et al., Ajani et al.) supports titles that state the message, highlighted data with annotations tied to it, and decluttering. Practitioner guidance (UK Government Analysis Function, Datawrapper) converges on the same rules: headline states the message, labels sit on the data, plain words, no dual axes. The evidence has limits: tests are mostly memory and description tasks, not "can a lay reader work out what this chart means", and a stated-finding title also steers interpretation (Kong et al.).

### Cited Findings
- Borkin et al. (2016), 393 visualizations, eye tracking of 33 participants plus thousands of free-text descriptions. Abstract: findings "quantitatively support many conventional qualitative design guidelines, including that (1) titles and supporting text should convey the message of a visualization, (2) if used appropriately, pictograms do not interfere with understanding and can improve recognition, and (3) redundancy helps effectively communicate the message"; "visualizations memorable 'at-a-glance' are also capable of effectively conveying the message". — [Borkin et al., "Beyond Memorability: Visualization Recognition and Recall", IEEE TVCG 2016](http://olivalab.mit.edu/Papers/07192646.pdf) (read full text). VERIFIED
- Same paper, details: average description quality 1.90 for visualizations with titles versus 1.30 without (p < 0.001), partly driven by scientific visualizations lacking titles; when shown, titles were fixated 59 percent of the time during encoding and were the element most likely to be described at recall; titles were fixated more and earlier when at the top than at the bottom. The authors observed that when a title included the main message (their example "66% of Americans feel Romney Performed Better Than Obama in Debates") more descriptions recalled the message than when the title was generic (example "Cities"). They define "message redundancy" as restating the main conclusion by explanatory annotations, labels, text and pictures. — Borkin et al. 2016. VERIFIED. Note: the title comparison is observational on real-world charts, so title presence is confounded with source type (their own caveat about scientific figures).
- Borkin et al. (2013), 2,070 single-panel visualizations from news, government, infographic and science sources: charts with human-recognisable objects, and visually distinctive or unusual chart types, were more memorable; effective titles and text annotations helped recall of the main point. — [Borkin et al., "What Makes a Visualization Memorable?", IEEE TVCG 2013](http://olivalab.mit.edu/Papers/Borkin_etal_MemorableVisualization_TVCG2013.pdf), reached only through search-result summaries. REPORTED
- Ajani, Lee, Xiong, Knaflic, Kemper and Franconeri tested the practitioner "declutter" and "focus" guidelines. Focus = headline describing the pattern, a unique colour on the relevant data, and written annotations connected to those values. Result: decluttering raised professionalism ratings; adding focus raised aesthetics and clarity ratings and produced "better memory for the highlighted pattern in the data". Authors' limit: "we do not know whether these results would generalize beyond our memory-based tasks". Participants were undergraduates. Cole Knaflic is a coauthor, so this is partly a test of her own guidelines. — [Ajani et al., "Declutter and Focus", IEEE TVCG 2022, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2021.3068337](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2021.3068337) via [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/33760737). VERIFIED (abstract only)
- Kong, Liu and Karahalios: the slant of a visualization title "influenced the perceived main message of a visualization, with viewers deriving opposing messages from the same visualization"; no significant effect on attitude change. Implication: a message title is powerful, so it must be accurate and neutral in framing. — [Kong et al., "Frames and Slants in Titles of Visualizations on Controversial Topics", CHI 2018, doi:10.1145/3173574.3174012](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3174012). VERIFIED (abstract only, via OpenAlex)
- Hullman and Diakopoulos: design choices at the levels of "the data, visual representation, textual annotations, and interactivity" prioritise particular interpretations in narrative visualizations (the "visualization rhetoric" framework). — [Hullman & Diakopoulos, IEEE TVCG 2011, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2011.255](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2011.255). VERIFIED (abstract only)
- Segel and Heer characterise narrative visualization genres by "the balance between the narrative flow intended by the author ... and story discovery on the part of the reader (often through interactive exploration)". This is the explanatory versus exploratory axis in the literature. — [Segel & Heer, IEEE TVCG 2010, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2010.179](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2010.179). VERIFIED (abstract only)
- UK Government Analysis Function, data-visualisation charts guidance: use two titles, a headline that communicates the main message and a subtitle specifying data, geography and time period; "use annotations to highlight key features, provide context and aid interpretation", concise and near the data; prefer direct labelling and avoid legends for line charts (label lines directly or at the end); maximum four lines in a line chart before readability drops; bar charts never break the zero axis, rank bars by value unless there is a natural order, gaps narrower than bars; "In general, we do not recommend using dual axis charts because: they can be easily misinterpreted" and "use separate charts instead"; "If you cannot write down the message your chart is giving in a few sentences, you should think again." — [Analysis Function, Data visualisation: charts](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-charts/). VERIFIED (expert guidance, not experiment)
- Datawrapper Academy, text in charts: explanatory charts "will likely be better with annotations in it"; "Place the words that explain your chart elements as close to those elements as possible"; remove the colour key and label categories directly; write in plain, conversational phrasing as if describing the chart to a friend; their plain-language example contrasts a technical description with "The darker a country is on this map, the more its citizens support democratic political systems." — [Datawrapper, "Text best practices in data visualizations"](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/text-in-data-visualizations/). VERIFIED (expert guidance)
- Few on dual-scaled axes: "I can't think of a single case when there isn't a better solution than a graph with a dual-scaled axis"; reason: two scales on one axis encourage magnitude comparisons between the two series, which are meaningless, and line crossings are read as "one set of values exceeded another". Few also notes that when two series share one scale, "magnitude comparisons are appropriate and the intersections of lines are meaningful". — [Few, "Dual-Scaled Axes in Graphs: Are They Ever the Best Solution?" 2008](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/dual-scaled_axes.pdf). VERIFIED (expert argument; Few himself says it is not the final word)
- Cairo (quoted by Pew): "We forget that we don't just understand graphics automatically. ... Graphics have grammar. They have vocabulary." — [Pew Research Center, "The art and science of the scatterplot", 2015](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2015/09/16/the-art-and-science-of-the-scatterplot/). VERIFIED

### Inferences
- The strongest direct experimental support for "title states the finding" is Borkin 2016 (observational, recall-based) plus Ajani 2022 (experimental, memory-based). No study found tests "lay reader correctly states what the chart means within N seconds" with and without a finding-title. ESTIMATED
- A title like "Ten units holding this choke beat thirteen attackers but lose to fourteen" has the form of Borkin's good title (message plus concrete numbers) and of the Analysis Function headline. ESTIMATED
- Plain-language and concrete-unit labelling is supported by practitioner guidance (Datawrapper, Analysis Function) and by Cairo's literacy point; I found no experiment isolating "ratio or index axis versus concrete-unit axis" for lay readers. ESTIMATED
- A signed share from -1 to 1 and a "multiple of headcount" axis are both derived indices; the guidance above (plain words, state it as a sentence) is the closest available evidence for avoiding them. ESTIMATED

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: Tufte (Visual Display of Quantitative Information; Envisioning Information beyond the secondary quote in section 5), Few's "Show Me the Numbers", Cairo's "The Functional Art/How Charts Lie", Knaflic's "Storytelling with Data" (blog pages returned 404), Schwabish's "Better Data Visualizations", Kosara's blog posts, the FT Chart Doctor columns. Their positions are widely known but I cannot cite what I did not read.
- Hullman's more specific annotation experiments (for example on annotated charts and takeaway recall) were not located in the time available.
- Heavy debate on whether memorability equals comprehension (Borkin 2013/2016 critics such as Munzner's "On the memorability debate" blog, Kosara's eagereyes post) was surfaced in search results but not read. AMBIGUOUS whether the 2016 claim "memorable is effective" generalises.

## 3. What lay readers misread: scatter plots, log scales, dual axes, ratio axes, heat maps, box plots, radar charts, small multiples, and chart-literacy studies

### Takeaway
Chart literacy is uneven and tied to schooling: bar and line charts are taught, scatter plots mostly are not, and one third of US and German adults had low graph literacy and low numeracy. Hard evidence exists for scatter plots (Pew), log scales (Romano et al.) and chart-type difficulty (VLAT family). For ratio axes, heat maps without numbers, box plots and small multiples I found no lay-reader experiments.

### Cited Findings
- Pew Research Center, September 2015 science-knowledge survey: 63 percent of US adults correctly read a scatter plot of sugar consumption versus tooth decay (item adapted from the OECD's PISA); 84 percent of postgraduates, 79 percent of college graduates and about 50 percent of those with high school or less answered correctly (four options, correct one: "the more sugar people eat, the more likely they are to get cavities"). Cairo: "The bar chart, the line chart, we are taught how to read those in school"; scatter plots are mainly met in economics, statistics or science courses. — [Pew, "The art and science of the scatterplot"](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2015/09/16/the-art-and-science-of-the-scatterplot/). VERIFIED. Note: a simple monotone-trend scatter; no diagonal reference or derived axes, so it is a lower bound on difficulty for more complex scatters. ESTIMATED
- Galesic and Garcia-Retamero: "even the simplest graphs are not understood by everyone"; nationally representative samples (Germany n=495, US n=492) completed about 9 of 13 graph-literacy items correctly; "approximately one third of the population in both countries had both low graph literacy and low numeracy skills". — [Galesic & Garcia-Retamero, "Graph literacy: a cross-cultural comparison", Medical Decision Making 2011, doi:10.1177/0272989X10373805](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X10373805) via [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/20671213). VERIFIED (abstract only)
- VLAT: 12 visualizations (line, bar, stacked bar, 100% stacked bar, pie, histogram, scatterplot, bubble, area, stacked area, choropleth, treemap), tried out on 191 test takers; item difficulty indices ranged from 0.15 to 1.0, mean 0.64, with 17 easy (above 0.85), 19 moderate and 18 hard (below 0.5) items among 54 tried-out items. (The paper's abstract says 53 final items; the tryout text says 54.) — [Lee, Kim & Kwon, "VLAT", IEEE TVCG 2017](https://www.bckwon.com/pdf/vlat.pdf). VERIFIED (read PDF text). The per-chart accuracy table was not extracted.
- Verma and Fan (2025 preprint), five visualization-literacy tests combined, N=503 US adults: item difficulty spanned the full range; reliably different by graph type (chi-square(12)=29.1, p=.004), hardest stacked bar charts (0.40 correct, 95% CI 0.28 to 0.51), easiest histograms (0.83, CI 0.79 to 0.91); but graph type and task type "only explained a modest amount of variation in performance across items", so the chart form alone does not predict whether readers will succeed; tree maps appeared in a single test so their estimate is confounded with that test. — [Verma & Fan, arXiv:2505.08031](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.08031) (read PDF text). VERIFIED (preprint, not peer reviewed as far as I could tell)
- Mini-VLAT is a 12-item short form of VLAT (omega 0.72) and correlates with aptitude for learning an unfamiliar visualization (a parallel coordinate plot test). — [Pandey & Ottley, Computer Graphics Forum 2023, doi:10.1111/cgf.14809](https://doi.org/10.1111/cgf.14809). VERIFIED (abstract only, via OpenAlex)
- Börner, Bueckle and Ginda: data-visualization literacy framework (reading and constructing visualizations) developed from teaching more than 8,500 students; no per-chart difficulty ranking in the abstract. — [Börner et al., PNAS 2019, doi:10.1073/pnas.1807180116](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1807180116). VERIFIED (abstract only)
- Log scales: showing COVID-19 deaths on a log scale rather than a linear scale led people to "a less accurate understanding of how the pandemic has developed", less accurate predictions and different policy preferences; the authors recommend linear scales "at least as a default option" for general-public communication. — [Romano, Sotis, Dominioni & Guidi, Health Economics 2020, doi:10.1002/hec.4143](https://doi.org/10.1002/hec.4143) via [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/32844495). VERIFIED (abstract only)
- Dual axes: Analysis Function and Few advise against them (see section 2). Experiment note: Isenberg et al. (2011) is titled "A study on dual-scale data charts" but studies charts that show two data resolutions (focus plus context) in one chart, not two y-axes with different units; it found "cut-out" charts with collocated context best and superimposed charts worst. It should not be cited as evidence on two-y-axis charts. — [Isenberg et al., IEEE TVCG 2011, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2011.160](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2011.160) via [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/22034368). VERIFIED (abstract only)
- Radar charts: criticised as "poorly suited for making trade-off decisions", radial distances "hard to judge", filled areas scale with the square of values, and adjacent axes imply spurious relations; effectiveness limited to small data sets. — [Wikipedia, Radar chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_chart) (cites Cleveland's hierarchy; I did not read the underlying sources). REPORTED
- Box plots: Williamson, Parker and Kendrick (1989) advocate box plots for readers of tables of data, an expert-advocacy piece, not a lay-reader test. — [Annals of Internal Medicine 1989, doi:10.7326/0003-4819-110-11-916](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-110-11-916) via [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/2719423). VERIFIED (abstract only)

### Inferences
- Because bar and line charts are taught and scatter plots mostly are not (Cairo via Pew), a report for a non-specialist plausibly needs scatter plots to be introduced with an explicit reading instruction in words; the evidence is Pew's 50 percent for lower-education respondents on even a simple scatter. ESTIMATED
- A chart type is not by itself a guarantee of lay readability (Verma and Fan found type explains little item variance); the question asked of the chart (the task) and the specific design matter. ESTIMATED
- The Romano et al. result is about log-scaled counts over time; a ratio or multiple axis ("enemy size as a multiple of this side's headcount") is a different encoding and has no direct lay-reader study that I found. ESTIMATED

### Gaps
- No lay-reader study found for: heat maps without printed numbers, box plots (misreading studies), small multiples, ratio/multiple axes, signed share (-1 to 1) axes, or diagonal reference lines in scatters.
- The VLAT's per-chart accuracy table (Table 2) was not extracted; and a search-summary claim about stacked area (0.46) and scatterplot (0.68) baselines came from an unnamed secondary snippet, so I have not used it. AMBIGUOUS/unsourced.

## 4. Chart forms for "A versus B" comparisons that readers find easy: dot plots, dumbbells, slope charts, bar with reference line, icon arrays, bullet graphs

### Takeaway
Evidence for easy A-versus-B reading is strongest for risk communication with icon arrays (several experiments, but with a conflicting randomised trial) and rests on perceptual-accuracy logic for bars and dots. Few's bullet graph is a documented design with a stated rationale but no lay-reader test found. For dumbbells, slope charts and reference-line bars I found only descriptions or no source.

### Cited Findings
- Icon arrays: in two experiments (older adults n=59; students n=112), icon arrays "increased accuracy of both low- and high-numeracy people, even when transparent numerical representations were used"; risks shown as icon arrays were seen as less serious than the same risks shown numerically; with 1,000 icons rather than 100, risks were perceived as more serious and risk reduction as larger. — [Galesic, Garcia-Retamero & Gigerenzer, "Using icon arrays to communicate medical risks: overcoming low numeracy", Health Psychology 2009, doi:10.1037/a0014474](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014474) via [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19290713). VERIFIED (abstract only)
- Okan et al.: icon arrays help across studies but low-graph-literacy people "experience only limited benefits"; explanatory labels in the array's regions improved understanding among less graph-literate participants, and a reflective question with feedback produced large gains for all. — [Okan, Garcia-Retamero, Cokely & Maldonado, J. Experimental Psychology: Applied 2015, doi:10.1037/xap0000045](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000045) via [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25938975). VERIFIED (abstract only)
- Conflicting evidence: a randomised trial of 120 male veterans found no difference in understanding at the immediate test between percentages, frequencies, and frequencies with icon arrays, and "accuracy was inferior with frequencies with icon arrays than percentages or frequencies" at the 20-minute recall test; conclusion "Icon arrays may impair short-term recall of CVR". — [Ruiz et al., Patient Education and Counseling 2013, doi:10.1016/j.pec.2013.06.026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pec.2013.06.026) via [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23916416). VERIFIED (abstract only). The two sets of findings are AMBIGUOUS in aggregate: icon arrays are not a guaranteed win, and the effect may depend on population, task, and whether labels are present.
- Bullet graph (Few): developed "to replace the meters and gauges that are often used on dashboards"; one featured measure shown as a bar, optional comparative marker (a target or the same measure a year ago), and two to five shaded ranges "that declare its qualitative state (for example, good, satisfactory, and poor)"; "Its linear design not only gives it a small footprint, but also supports more efficient reading than radial meters"; bars start at zero. — [Few, Bullet Graph Design Specification, Perceptual Edge, rev. 2013](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/misc/Bullet_Graph_Design_Spec.pdf). VERIFIED (design specification; no experimental test cited in the document)
- Bar guidance: never break the numerical axis from zero; rank bars by value unless there is a natural order. — [Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-charts/). VERIFIED
- FT Visual Vocabulary descriptions: dot plot "for displaying the change or range (minimum/maximum values) across multiple categories"; slope chart for how values or ranks change between categories or over time "particularly when data can be simplified into 2-3 points". — [FT Visual Vocabulary README](https://github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/blob/main/visual-vocabulary/README.md). REPORTED
- The Heer and Bostock position-versus-length result (section 1) is the perceptual rationale for placing both items on a common scale: dot and bar are both top-tier encodings. — Heer & Bostock 2010. VERIFIED

### Inferences
- A statement such as "ten units beat thirteen but lose to fourteen" is a count threshold. Icon arrays or rows of unit icons would show those counts literally, but the evidence for icon arrays comes from risk-of-event proportions in health settings (hundreds to thousands of icons), not from threshold crossings. ESTIMATED
- Bullet-graph-style shaded qualitative bands behind a bar are a documented convention for showing "this value falls in the good/satisfactory/poor zone". ESTIMATED
- The Galesic 1,000-versus-100 icon finding implies that the icon-array denominator changes how large the same proportion feels, so the chosen number of icons affects perceived seriousness. ESTIMATED

### Gaps
- No source retrieved on dumbbell / connected dot plots for lay readers (the Datawrapper dot-plot pages returned 404). No evidence found on bar-with-reference-line readability. Spiegelhalter (Art of Statistics; his own risk-communication papers) not retrieved.
- Slope charts: only a vocabulary description, no lay-reader study.

## 5. Chart forms for parameter studies and sensitivity analysis, including the region where all pass/fail conditions hold

### Takeaway
Tornado diagrams, one-at-a-time plots, parallel coordinates, ICE/partial dependence and Sobol bars are described in the literature mainly for analysts. I found no readability study for non-experts on any of them, and none on feasible-region or operating-envelope plots. Small multiples have an expert-endorsed rationale (Tufte) and one experiment (Robertson) showing they beat animation for accuracy.

### Cited Findings
- Tornado diagram: a bar chart with variables on the vertical axis sorted by effect size, largest at top, built by one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis (each variable moved to its low and high value with others at baseline); advantage as stated: with many variables, the top bars identify which ones matter. — [Wikipedia, Tornado diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tornado_diagram). REPORTED
- One-at-a-time (OAT) limits: "cannot detect the presence of interactions between input variables and is unsuitable for nonlinear models"; unexplored share of the input space grows superexponentially with the number of inputs. Sobol indices give the "proportion of variance explained by an input or group of inputs", first-order and total-effect, including interactions. The article also lists scatter plots of each input against the output as a visual method. — [Wikipedia, Sensitivity analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensitivity_analysis) (the primary text is Saltelli et al., which I did not reach). REPORTED
- ICE plots: refine partial dependence plots by graphing the relationship between predicted response and a feature "for individual observations", exposing heterogeneity and interactions that averaging hides; implemented in R package ICEbox. Presented to supervised-learning practitioners. — [Goldstein, Kapelner, Bleich & Pitkin, "Peeking Inside the Black Box", arXiv:1309.6392](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6392). VERIFIED (abstract only)
- Parallel coordinates: show at most n−1 neighbour relationships for n axes; axis order is "critical for finding features, and in typical data analysis many reorderings will need to be tried"; the stated audience is analysts of high-dimensional data. — [Wikipedia, Parallel coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_coordinates). REPORTED. Mini-VLAT's authors use a parallel coordinate plot as an "unfamiliar visualization" for testing learning ability, i.e. they treat it as unfamiliar to lay adults (Pandey & Ottley 2023, section 3). VERIFIED (abstract only; that is my reading of their use, not an explicit statement)
- Small multiples: Tufte, Envisioning Information (1990) p. 67: "At the heart of quantitative reasoning is a single question: Compared to what?" and "for a wide range of problems in data presentation, small multiples are the best design solution." — via [Wikipedia, Small multiple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_multiple). REPORTED
- Robertson et al. compared animation, overlaid traces and a small-multiples display for trend data: both static depictions were significantly faster than animation for analysis, and "the small multiples display is more accurate". — [Robertson, Fernandez, Fisher, Lee & Stasko, "Effectiveness of animation in trend visualization", IEEE TVCG 2008, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2008.125](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2008.125) via [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/18988980). VERIFIED (abstract only)
- Heat-map convention: grid cells coloured by intensity scale (FT Visual Vocabulary description, section 1). Colour intensity ranks near the bottom of Cleveland and McGill's accuracy order (REPORTED, section 1), and Franconeri et al. state that comparing subsets of values in a display is slow while global statistics are extracted quickly (section 1). — as cited. REPORTED / VERIFIED (abstract only)
- Shaded bands as "state" regions: Few's bullet graph uses two to five shaded ranges on the scale to declare qualitative state (section 4). VERIFIED

### Inferences
- A two-parameter grid coloured by pass/fail shows a region directly (a global pattern, which the visual system extracts quickly per Franconeri), whereas reading exact thresholds from colour is the weak use of colour. ESTIMATED, combining two cited statements.
- Feasible-region diagrams (linear programming style), engineering design-space and operating-envelope charts, and phase diagrams are standard in textbooks and papers aimed at readers trained in those fields; I found no evidence on how non-expert readers read them. ESTIMATED (absence of evidence)
- Because the OAT limitation is interaction blindness, a one-at-a-time sensitivity line chart can mislead if parameters interact; a two-parameter grid is the minimum form that shows an interaction. ESTIMATED

### Gaps
- No lay-reader study for tornado, contour, ICE, PDP, parallel-coordinate, Sobol-bar or feasible-region plots. No retrieval of Saltelli et al. (Global Sensitivity Analysis: The Primer; Sensitivity Analysis in Practice), or engineering design-space texts.
- Evidence on contour plots versus heat maps for non-experts not found.
- Whether heat maps with printed values in cells help lay readers: not found.

## 6. Time series of two competing quantities: direct-labelled lines, difference shading, mirrored areas, and animation

### Takeaway
The best-supported guidance is: two lines on one shared scale with direct labels (Analysis Function, Few), and prefer static views to animation for accuracy (Robertson). The general animation literature is mixed and mostly about learning mechanical systems. I found no sourced evidence on difference shading or mirrored-area charts.

### Cited Findings
- Analysis Function: line charts suit time series with multiple series; at most four lines; label lines directly or at the end instead of using a legend; avoid dual axes. — [Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-charts/). VERIFIED
- Few: two series on the same scale allow magnitude comparison and "the intersections of lines are meaningful" ("one set of values exceeded another"). — [Few 2008](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/dual-scaled_axes.pdf). VERIFIED
- Robertson et al. 2008: the animated trend display "is the fastest technique for presentation and participants find it enjoyable and exciting, [but] it does lead to many participant errors. Animation is the least effective form for analysis"; static overlaid traces and small multiples were both significantly faster than animation for analysis. — [Robertson et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2008.125) via [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/18988980). VERIFIED (abstract only)
- Hegarty, Kriz and Cate: in three experiments, animated diagrams of a mechanical system produced no better understanding than static diagrams; comprehension improved when students had to predict motion from static diagrams and when a verbal description of the dynamic processes was added. — [Hegarty, Kriz & Cate, Cognition and Instruction 2003, doi:10.1207/s1532690xci2104_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci2104_1). VERIFIED (abstract only; a mechanical-systems domain, not charts)
- de Koning et al.: visual cueing that focuses attention on one element of an animation improved comprehension and transfer, including for uncued information; animations "are often cognitively very demanding". — [de Koning, Tabbers, Rikers & Paas, Applied Cognitive Psychology 2007, doi:10.1002/acp.1346](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1346). VERIFIED (abstract only, via OpenAlex)
- Okan et al. found that dynamic icon-array features helped only in specific forms (sequential presentation, explanatory labels, reflective questions with feedback); explanatory labels helped less-graph-literate readers (section 4). VERIFIED (abstract only)

### Inferences
- If a replay is used, the cited findings favour a replay paired with a static summary and with cues or labels on the thing to attend to, with the static view carrying the quantitative claim. ESTIMATED, from Robertson, Hegarty et al., de Koning et al.
- For two competing quantities that must be compared by magnitude, a single shared scale is the correct condition; dual axes are the explicitly warned-against case. ESTIMATED, from Few.

### Gaps
- Tversky, Morrison and Betrancourt (2002), "Animation: can it facilitate?", International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 57(4): could not be retrieved (sciencedirect 403, ubc.ca 404), and the OpenAlex search returned unrelated records. I did not rely on memory for its claims. Its congruence and apprehension principles should be read directly before citing.
- No source retrieved on difference shading between two lines, mirrored area/"population pyramid over time", or stream-of-two charts.

## 7. Concrete examples, pictures of the situation and natural frequencies alongside abstract numbers

### Takeaway
Natural frequencies (counts out of a stated total, such as "10 out of 1,000") roughly doubled to tripled correct Bayesian reasoning in Gigerenzer and Hoffrage's experiments and replicated in managers. Pictograms and recognisable objects aid recognition and recall when related to the data (Borkin), and icon arrays help in many but not all risk-communication studies.

### Cited Findings
- Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995): frequency formats "elicited a substantially higher proportion of Bayesian algorithms than probability formats: 46% in the standard menu and 50% in the short menu. Probability formats, in contrast, elicited 16% and 28%", stable across 15 inference problems; argument: the "natural" information format for humans was frequencies from a series of events rather than probabilities or percentages. — [Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, "How to improve Bayesian reasoning without instruction: Frequency formats", Psychological Review 1995](https://library.mpib-berlin.mpg.de/ft/gg/GG_How_1995.pdf). VERIFIED (read PDF text)
- Replication with managers and management students on four management tasks: "The percentage of Bayesian responses was almost twice as high when information was presented in natural frequencies compared to a presentation in terms of percentages"; natural frequencies are "the tallies in a natural sample in which hit rate and false-alarm rate are not normalized with respect to base rates"; knowing Bayes' rule did not change performance. — [Hoffrage, Hafenbrädl & Bouquet, Frontiers in Psychology 2015, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00642](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00642) via [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26157397). VERIFIED (abstract only)
- Icon arrays from natural-frequency-style risk communication: Galesic et al. 2009, Okan et al. 2015 and Ruiz et al. 2013 (section 4). Mixed evidence.
- Borkin et al. 2016: pictograms "if used appropriately" do not interfere with understanding and can improve recognition; visualizations with the best descriptions were more likely to contain pictograms; but "some pictograms can hurt the understanding" when unrelated to the data or message. They also note prior psychology work that concrete, imageable words are easier to remember than abstract ones. — Borkin et al. 2016. VERIFIED. Borkin 2013 reported human-recognisable objects as the most dominant factor for memorability. REPORTED (via search summary)
- Datawrapper's plain-language example turns an abstract technical description into a reading instruction for the chart (section 2). VERIFIED

### Inferences
- The two example statements in the brief ("ten units holding this choke beat thirteen attackers but lose to fourteen"; "about twice as many units firing") are counts and a simple ratio stated in words; they are in the natural-frequency family (tallies, not normalised probabilities). The Gigerenzer-Hoffrage result is about Bayesian inference problems, not about reading win/lose thresholds, so the transfer is by analogy. ESTIMATED
- The pictogram findings are about memory and recognition of the chart, with an explicit warning that pictograms unrelated to the message can hurt. ESTIMATED

### Gaps
- No sourced study on "picture of the situation" (an annotated scene or diagram of the scenario) next to a chart. Not found.
- Spiegelhalter's guidance on expected-frequency statements ("out of 100 similar people") not retrieved.

## Cross-cutting observations for the report writer

- Evidence strength differs sharply by claim. Experiments: position/length accuracy (Heer and Bostock), natural frequencies (Gigerenzer and Hoffrage), log-scale misunderstanding (Romano), animation versus static (Robertson), declutter-and-focus (Ajani), title effect on recall (Borkin, observational). Expert guidance only: dual axes (Few, Analysis Function), direct labelling and annotation (Datawrapper, Analysis Function), bullet graphs (Few), small multiples (Tufte), tornado, OAT, Sobol.
- Absence of evidence is itself a finding for: lay readability of feasible-region and tornado plots, ratio/multiple axes, signed-share axes, heat maps without numbers, dumbbell charts, difference-shaded line pairs, mirrored area charts, box plots, and small multiples.
- Conflicts to carry forward: icon arrays (positive in Galesic 2009, negative on short-term recall in Ruiz 2013); animation (fastest and enjoyable for presentation in Robertson 2008, but error-prone); angle versus length ranking (theory predicts angle worse, Heer and Bostock and Cleveland and McGill found no such difference).
- Tool access limits: Cleveland and McGill 1984, Tversky et al. 2002, Knaflic, Schwabish, Tufte, Cairo, Kosara, Hullman's annotation experiments, Saltelli, Spiegelhalter and the VLAT per-chart table were not obtained in full.
