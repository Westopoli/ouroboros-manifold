## 1. What exactly did F. W. Lanchester state in "Aircraft in Warfare" (1916), and what did Osipov state independently (1915)? Equations.

### Takeaway
Lanchester derived his "N-square law" in Chapters V-VI (articles of 2 and 9 October 1914) from the premise that, under modern long-range aimed fire, loss rate on one side is proportional to the number of enemy units firing. He stated the law in words, never used the word "linear", and treated the unaimed area-fire case (which gives strength proportional to numbers) as a variation on the main hypothesis. Osipov published an analysis in the Russian journal Voenniy Sbornik in June-October 1915; sources agree it was independent of Lanchester but disagree on whether it centred on the square law or a 3/2 power.

Modern restatement of the two models (equations that later authors attach to Lanchester):
- Aimed-fire square law (Washburn eq. 2.2-2.4): dx/dt = -beta y, dy/dt = -alpha x. Dividing gives dy/dx = alpha x / (beta y), so beta (y^2 - y0^2) = alpha (x^2 - x0^2). Fighting strengths are beta y0^2 and alpha x0^2: "fighting strength is proportional to the lethality coefficient and to the square of the number of units." Washburn's worked case: x0 = 1000, y0 = 800, alpha = 0.8, beta = 0.9 gives beta y0^2 = 576,000 < alpha x0^2 = 800,000, so y is annihilated and x_f = sqrt(x0^2 - (beta/alpha) y0^2) = 529 survivors. — [Washburn 2000, section 2.2](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf). VERIFIED. Check: sqrt(1,000,000 - 1.125 x 640,000) = sqrt(280,000) = 529.2. ESTIMATED
- Unaimed-fire linear law (Washburn eq. 2.5-2.7): if each x unit fires at rate r, each shot kills all y units inside lethal area a, and y units are spread uniformly over area A, then dy/dt = -(r a / A) x y. With alpha = r a / A: dx/dt = -beta x y, dy/dt = -alpha x y, state equation alpha (x - x0) = beta (y - y0), fighting strengths alpha x0 and beta y0. "There is no 'dilution effect' for the linear law, since fire is unaimed." — same source, section 2.3. VERIFIED
- Why numbers enter squared: a new unit in a square-law battle (1) fires at the enemy and (2) dilutes the enemy's fire against units already engaged; better weapons give only effect (1), which is why the lethality coefficient enters fighting strength only to the first power. — same source, section 2.2. VERIFIED
- Mesterton-Gibbons gives time-explicit solutions: with dm/dt = -a_n n and dn/dt = -a_m m, state equation a_m (m0^2 - m^2) = a_n (n0^2 - n^2); time to annihilate, t_f = (1 / (2 sqrt(a_m a_n))) ln[(sqrt(a_m) m0 + sqrt(a_n) n0) / (sqrt(a_m) m0 - sqrt(a_n) n0)]; survivors m0 sqrt(1 - a_n n0^2 / (a_m m0^2)). Linear form (his 2.11): dm/dt = -a_n m n, dn/dt = -a_m m n, state equation a_m (m0 - m) = a_n (n0 - n), winner if a_m m0 > a_n n0, survivors m0 (1 - a_n n0 / (a_m m0)); in theory the loser never reaches zero in finite time. — [Mesterton-Gibbons 2016](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf). VERIFIED
- Karr (1983) interpretations as quoted there: the square law arises either when targets are individually destroyed but each surviving shooter finds them at a constant rate, or when targets are dispersed over an area that shrinks in proportion to their number; the second (linear) model arises when it gets harder to acquire a target as the opposing group shrinks, or when targets "redisperse between shots" in a fixed area. — same notes, footnotes 4 and 8. REPORTED (Karr's own paper not read)
- Modern literature uses two different models that both yield the linear state equation: Lanchester's unaimed/"positional" fire (dm/dt = -a_n m n) and a one-to-one contest model, dm/dt = -a_n min(m, n), dn/dt = -a_m min(m, n) (Franks and Partridge 1993), in which "excess members of the larger group do not participate until there is an opportunity to replace a member of their own army - because, e.g., the battlefield geometry does not allow simultaneous attacks of many against one." Both have the same state equation and the same winning condition a_m m0 > a_n n0. — [Mesterton-Gibbons 2016, eq. 2.23-2.24 and surrounding text](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf). VERIFIED
- Terminology caveat: "linear" refers to the linear relation between group fighting ability and group size, not to the ODEs; both models yielding the linear law involve nonlinear ODEs, while the square law involves linear ODEs. — same notes, footnote 11. VERIFIED

### Inferences
- Lanchester's own prose supports two readings that later literature separates: the square law as a statement about aimed, concentrable fire, and the linear law as the area-fire or "no direct value in concentration" case (section 30). The one-on-one / frontage-limited reading of the linear law is his "ancient conditions" paragraph (section 20) plus the Macaulay allusion, not a separate model with its own equation.
- Lanchester's section 20 phrase "so long as the fighting line was unbroken" is the nearest he comes to naming a frontage condition: the number actually fighting is equal on both sides because the line geometry equalises it.

### Gaps
- Osipov's original text and the Helmbold-Rehm translation were not accessible (DTIC and Wiley returned 403). His actual equations and the status of the 3/2 exponent rest on secondary sources that disagree.
- Not checked: whether Lanchester's 1916 book text differs from the 1914 Engineering articles for Chapters V-VI; the scan edition used is the book.

## 2. Under what stated assumptions does each law hold, and which sources say a constricted frontage or defile yields the linear law?

### Takeaway
The square law needs every unit able to engage any enemy unit and to be engaged by several (aimed, concentrable fire, no crowding); the linear law arises from unaimed area fire or from fixed one-to-one contacts. Lanchester himself tied the ancient-conditions case to a fighting line where equal numbers engage, and later work formalises this as a cap on engaged numbers: when both sides are capped at the same width the state equation becomes linear, and with partial caps the fight is a piecewise hybrid.

### Cited Findings

Stated assumptions:
- Square law: "individuals on either side can concentrate their attacks on their opponents"; per-capita death rate equals enemy-to-friendly ratio times enemy individual fighting ability. — [Mesterton-Gibbons 2016](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf). VERIFIED
- Atkinson, Kress and MacKay state the conditions for the aimed-fire equations (dB/dt = -rR, dR/dt = -bB): "all units on both sides are in action, aim their fire, know when they have incapacitated their targets, and can quickly acquire new ones." — [Atkinson, Kress and MacKay 2020](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/Atkinson_Kress_MacKay_2020.pdf), section 2. VERIFIED
- Deitchman (1962), as paraphrased: the square-law equations apply when each side is visible to the other and each individual can fire on any opposing individual; the linear (product-form) equations apply when each side is invisible to the other and fires into an area believed occupied. His ambush variant: dm/dt = -a_n m n (ambusher m is invisible, so its losses scale with both sizes) and dn/dt = -a_m m (ambushed n is visible), state equation 2 a_m (m0 - m) = a_n (n0^2 - n^2); the ambusher wins an all-out fight if a_m / a_n > n0^2 / (2 m0). — [Mesterton-Gibbons 2016, eq. 2.25-2.27](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf). VERIFIED (reading of Deitchman as paraphrased; Deitchman's paper not read). Epstein (1985, p. 13) calls the ambush variant "may well be the most plausible of all Lanchester variants" (as quoted there). REPORTED
- Mesterton-Gibbons's summary: "concentrated fire favors a square-law outcome, whereas positional fire and one-to-one combat favor a linear-law outcome." — same notes. VERIFIED
- MacKay: any model of the form dR/dt = -F_G, dG/dt = -F_R with F_G / F_R independent of R and G gives fighting strength proportional to numbers, not their square; Lanchester used constant F for "ancient" hand-to-hand combat and artillery bombardment into a known area (F_R = r R G / A_G) gives the same property. — [MacKay, arXiv math/0606300, section 3.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0606300). VERIFIED
- A game-AI paper (Stanescu et al.) states "Lanchester's Linear Law applies when one soldier can only fight one other soldier at a time. If one side has more soldiers some of them won't always be fighting as they wait for an opportunity to attack," and argues the square law "has nothing to do with range - what is really important is the rate of acquiring new targets", with range merely letting soldiers engage as fast as they can shoot. — [Stanescu, Barriga and Buro 2015](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12780). VERIFIED (it is their stated view, not a result of that paper)

Constricted frontage and defiles:
- Lanchester's ancient-conditions and Macaulay passages (section 20 and section 35) as quoted in question 1. VERIFIED. He does not write an equation for a constricted pass.
- Atkinson, Kress and MacKay model a defile directly: "Blue is forced to attack along a road or other defile so that only a fixed number Bmax can engage. ... Hence B - Bmax Blue forces initially start in reserve and are neither effective nor vulnerable to Red's fire." Each Blue casualty is replaced from reserve, so the Blue term b B is replaced by b Bmax. Their motivating case is the Battle of Ein-A-Tinna (1982) where terrain forced the Israeli tanks into a single column: unconstrained ratio 6 Syrian v 30 Israeli, effective ratio 6 v 1 (citing Gabriel 1984). — [Atkinson, Kress and MacKay 2020, section 1 and 3.2](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/Atkinson_Kress_MacKay_2020.pdf). VERIFIED (the Ein-A-Tinna facts are the paper's, citing Gabriel; Gabriel not read)
- Symmetric deployment caps (their section 4.1): with perfect targeting and caps Bmax on Blue and Rmax on Red (mu = Bmax / B0, nu = Rmax / R0), the engagement "begins as Lanchester's ancient warfare model", dB/dt = -r Rmax, dR/dt = -b Bmax, with state equation b Bmax (B0 - B) = r Rmax (R0 - R) (linear in numbers). When one side falls below its cap the fight becomes mixed (dB/dt = -r Rmax, dR/dt = -b B), and when both are below their caps it is pure square-law aimed fire. The parity (mutual annihilation) condition that results is: r R0^2 / (b B0^2) = (2 mu - mu^2) / (2 nu - nu^2). — same paper, eq. 22-26. VERIFIED (read); I checked eq. 26 numerically (see below). ESTIMATED check
- One-sided constraint (Red unconstrained, perfect targeting, so nu = 1): parity needs r R0^2 / (b B0^2) = mu (2 - mu) (eq. 26 with nu = 1). The paper's own section 3.2 version (Blue also lacks target-kill information) has mu (2 - mu) / 2. Example given there: mu = 1/2 gives mu(2 - mu) = 3/4, "its kill rate must improve by a factor of 4/3, or its numbers be increased by the square root of this"; the extreme case of one engaged unit at a time (mu = 1/B0) needs a compensating factor of about B0 / 2. — same paper, eq. 19 and the text after it. VERIFIED
- If the deployment-limited sides also cannot tell empty positions (foxholes) from occupied ones, so fire becomes unaimed once reserves run out, their eq. 27 gives the pure linear parity law r R0 Rmax = b B0 Bmax (their "Lanchester's ancient model"), i.e. r R0^2 / (b B0^2) = mu / nu. — same paper, section 4.2. VERIFIED
- Bongers and Torres (2021) propose a "bottleneck combat model" where one or both sides cannot use all forces at once for physical reasons; as reported in the abstract: when the restriction applies to both sides it "leads to Lanchester's linear law" for both aimed and unaimed fire, with quality and the size of the restriction mattering more and quantity less; applied to Thermopylae, they find the Persians would have been defeated had the bottleneck persisted. — [Operational Research 21: 2859-2877 (2021)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12351-019-00513-0). REPORTED (abstract only, via a search-result summary; the paper was behind a login redirect)
- Franks and Partridge (1993) put the frontage-like idea into a single formula: dm/dt = -a_n min(m, n), dn/dt = -a_m min(m, n), explicitly justified by "battlefield geometry" preventing many-on-one attacks; state equation linear. — [Mesterton-Gibbons 2016, eq. 2.23](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf). VERIFIED
- Epstein's third criticism (as paraphrased) is relevant to frontage: "no crowding or force-to-space constraint ever moderates the extent to which Group 2 can concentrate its force"; the instantaneous casualty-exchange ratio (dm/dn = a_n n / (a_m m) for the square law) grows without bound with force ratio, and for linear-law models it is the constant a_n / a_m, so "the absence of diminishing returns is even more striking" there. — [Mesterton-Gibbons 2016, eq. 2.28 and footnote 14](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf). VERIFIED (as paraphrase of Epstein 1985; Epstein not read directly)
- Helmbold's 1965 letter "A Modification of Lanchester's Equations" proposes scaling relative attrition capability by a function of the force ratio to represent inefficiency of scale when force sizes are grossly unequal. — [Operations Research 13(5): 857-859](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.13.5.857) (abstract via search result). REPORTED

My numerical check of a frontage-capped model (ESTIMATED; Python Euler integration, step 0.001, equal quality b = r = 1, both sides capped at the same engaged width W, casualties among the engaged are replaced from the rest of the force; Blue 1000 against Red 500, so Blue wins and the number is Blue survivors):
- No cap (pure square law): 865.7 survivors (analytic sqrt(1000^2 - 500^2) = 866.0).
- W = 200: 599.9. W = 100: 549.9. W = 50: 525.0. W = 10: 505.0.
- Pure linear law gives 1000 - 500 = 500. So the outcome slides continuously from the square-law answer toward the linear-law answer as W falls, and a single exponent does not describe it: it is piecewise (equal-engaged linear stage, then mixed stage, then aimed-fire stage) as in the Atkinson et al. construction. ESTIMATED
- I also checked Atkinson et al. eq. 26 by integrating dB/dt = -r min(R, Rmax), dR/dt = -b min(B, Bmax) with B0 = R0 = 100, Bmax = 40, Rmax = 100 (mu = 0.4, nu = 1). Predicted parity r/b = (2 mu - mu^2) (B0/R0)^2 = 0.64. At r/b = 0.64 the result is mutual annihilation within integration error (Blue -0.00001, Red +0.45); at 0.608 Blue wins with about 17.9 left; at 0.672 Red wins with about 21.8 left. ESTIMATED

### Inferences
- Across the sources the same structural idea recurs: a cap on engaged numbers per side turns the relevant strength measure from (quality x N^2) toward (quality x N x cap), i.e. linear in numbers. Reserves behind the cap contribute only by replacing casualties.
- The literature I found treats a capped frontage as a regime change (piecewise ODEs), not as a fractional exponent. Fractional exponents (next question) arise from curve-fitting aggregate data, not from a frontage derivation. I found no source that derives a specific fractional exponent from a frontage width.

### Gaps
- No source found that derives an effective exponent between 1 and 2 from a frontage or range argument. The "hybrid" results here are piecewise models (Atkinson et al.; Bongers and Torres) and my own simulation.
- Taylor (1983), "Lanchester Models of Warfare" (2 vols., ORSA) and Washburn and Kress (2009), "Combat Modeling", the textbooks that likely treat variable-coefficient and limited-width variants, were not accessible and their claims on frontage are not reported here.
- Bongers and Torres 2021 and Kress and Talmor (1999, Markov stochastic Lanchester models of the 3:1 rule, J. Operational Research Society 50: 733-744, [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/palgrave.jors.2600758)) were seen only as abstracts; Atkinson et al. say they extend the deployment constraint in Kress and Talmor.

## 3. What mixed or fractional-exponent models exist, and what exponents did empirical fits find?

### Takeaway
The standard generalisation is Bracken's two-exponent form, dB/dt = a R^p B^q (plus a tactical parameter d), whose state-equation exponent is 1 + p - q (1 = linear, 2 = square, below 1 toward the logarithmic law). Fits to Ardennes and Kursk returned wildly different (p, q) depending on method, so no stable "fractional exponent for real battles" emerged; the aggregate evidence is that individual battle fits are too weakly determined to pick a law.

### Cited Findings

Model forms:
- Generalised Lanchester model (Bracken 1995), as written in Lucas and Turkes: dB/dt = a (d or 1/d) R^p B^q and dR/dt = b (1/d or d) B^p R^q, with d a tactical parameter that scales the defender's attrition by d and the attacker's by 1/d, p the exponent on the attacking force and q on the defending force; five parameters (a, b, d, p, q). p - q = 0 (for example p = q = 1) gives the linear law, "Lanchester viewed this as ... 'ancient conditions'", state equation b (B(0) - B(t)) = a (R(0) - R(t)); p = 1, q = 0 (or p - q = 1) gives the square law with state equation b (B(0)^2 - B(t)^2) = a (R(0)^2 - R(t)^2); p = 0, q = 1 (q - p = 1) is the logarithmic law. — [Lucas and Turkes 2004, section 1](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bc44457c-0423-42cc-bd62-585149fbf95d/content). VERIFIED
- MacKay writes Bracken's model as dR/dt = -g R^q G^p, dG/dt = -r R^p G^q with conserved quantity g G^alpha - r R^alpha and alpha = 1 + p - q; he says aimed fire is p = 1, q = 0 (alpha = 2) and unaimed p = q = 1, and writes "alpha = 0" for the latter. — [MacKay, section 3.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0606300). VERIFIED (as written); his "alpha = 0" for unaimed fire is inconsistent with his own formula, which gives alpha = 1 + 1 - 1 = 1 (and with Lucas and Turkes's p - q = 0). ESTIMATED (my arithmetic; likely a typo in the arXiv note)
- Derivation check: with dB/dt = -a R^p B^q and dR/dt = -b B^p R^q, dB/dR = (a/b) R^(p-q) B^(q-p), so B^(p-q) dB is proportional to R^(p-q) dR, giving B^(1+p-q) proportional to R^(1+p-q). So the state exponent is n = 1 + p - q, and fighting strength is (quality) x N^n. ESTIMATED
- The game-AI generalisation (Stanescu et al.) uses dA/dt = -beta A^(2-n) B and dB/dt = -alpha B^(2-n) A with "attrition order" n: n = 1 is the linear law, n = 2 the square law, and the state solution is alpha (A^n - A0^n) = beta (B^n - B0^n); the exponent "applies to the size of the forces involved in combat, but not to the fighting effectiveness". VERIFIED. Equivalence to Bracken: p = 1, q = 2 - n gives 1 + p - q = n. ESTIMATED

## 4. What is the quantitative trade between unit quality and numbers under each law?

### Takeaway
Under the square law quality must rise as the square of a numerical disadvantage (2:1 outnumbered needs 4x quality; 3:1 needs 9x); under the linear law it must rise in proportion (2:1 needs 2x). For a generalised state exponent n the required factor is (numerical ratio)^n.

### Cited Findings
- Square law: "a win for Group 1 requires its relative fighting ability a_m / a_n to exceed not only its relative numerical disadvantage n0 / m0, but also the square of that number ... For example, defeating an adversary that is three times as numerous requires fighters to be at least nine times as effective." Linear law: relative fighting ability need only exceed the numerical disadvantage, n0 / m0. — [Mesterton-Gibbons 2016, eq. 2.10 and 2.19](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf). VERIFIED
- Lanchester's own form: fighting strength proportional to the square of numbers times individual fighting value, with the 16-to-1 machine-gun example giving 4-to-1 replacement. — [Lanchester 1916, section 26-29](https://archive.org/stream/aircraftinwarfa00lancgoog/aircraftinwarfa00lancgoog_djvu.txt). VERIFIED
- Fighting-ability trade as a count equivalence: 1,000 rifles (a = 1) against 200 machine guns with 25 times a rifle's firepower are exactly equal under the square law (a_m m0^2 = 1 x 1000^2 = 10^6; a_n n0^2 = 25 x 200^2 = 10^6); the naive expectation was that the 200 guns would win 5 to 1. — [Mesterton-Gibbons 2016 citing Bellany (2002)](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf). VERIFIED (arithmetic check ESTIMATED: 25 x 40,000 = 1,000,000)
- Why quality is only linear in the square law: "fighting group ability is proportional to the square of the size of the fighting group but is only linearly related to individual fighting ability. It is therefore more important to enter battle with a large army than with fighters of high prowess." — [Mesterton-Gibbons 2016](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf). VERIFIED
- Lucas and Turkes: "in the linear law, ability (as measured by the attrition parameters a and b) is as important as numbers" and some have speculated the linear law "may turn out to be the 'new modern conditions'" if a force can orchestrate one-on-one engagements and so trade quantity for quality. — [Lucas and Turkes 2004](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bc44457c-0423-42cc-bd62-585149fbf95d/content). VERIFIED
- Mesterton-Gibbons's comparison diagram: if Side 1 is heavily outnumbered, it can win at all only if the linear law applies (the square-law threshold (n0/m0)^2 is too high), while if Side 1 is greatly favoured numerically (n0/m0 well below 1) it should prefer concentrated fire, because the square-law threshold (n0/m0)^2 is much smaller than the linear threshold n0/m0; there are two regions where Side 1 wins under either law but has more survivors under the linear law. — same notes, Figure 2.1 and the paragraph before it. VERIFIED
- Quality-versus-numbers table (ESTIMATED; required quality factor = ratio^n, where ratio is numerical disadvantage, n = 2 square, n = 1 linear, n = 1.5 and 1.56 as exponents mentioned in sources, 0.70 as Hartley's implied value):

| Numerical disadvantage | Linear (n=1) | n=1.5 | n=1.56 | Square (n=2) | n=0.70 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.5 : 1 | 1.5 | 1.84 | 1.88 | 2.25 | 1.33 |
| 2 : 1 | 2 | 2.83 | 2.95 | 4 | 1.63 |

| 5 : 1 | 5 | 11.18 | 12.31 | 25 | 3.09 |

- Survivors of the stronger-fighting side under each law (Mesterton-Gibbons eq. 2.9 and 2.18, with a_m / a_n = k and n0 / m0 = r): square law survivors m0 sqrt(1 - r^2 / k); linear law survivors m0 (1 - r / k). Example from MacKay: red has twice as many units as green but green units are three times as effective: r R^2 - g G^2 = r (2 G0)^2 - 3 r G0^2 = r G0^2 > 0, so red (the larger force) wins under the square law. — [Mesterton-Gibbons 2016](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf); [MacKay 2005](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0606300). VERIFIED for the MacKay example and the source equations; the k, r rewriting is mine (m0 sqrt(1 - n0^2 a_n / (m0^2 a_m)) = m0 sqrt(1 - r^2 / k)). ESTIMATED
- Capped deployment adds a further penalty to the numbers side: with Blue limited to mu of its force and perfect targeting, parity needs r R0^2 / (b B0^2) = mu (2 - mu); at mu = 1/2 that is 3/4, so Blue's kill rate would need to rise by 4/3 or its numbers by sqrt(4/3). — [Atkinson, Kress and MacKay 2020](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/Atkinson_Kress_MacKay_2020.pdf). VERIFIED
- Splitting a force under the square law: 50,000 beats 40,000 then 30,000 in sequence but loses to the two combined (Lanchester); MacKay's N-fold division example has the smaller but more effective green force winning with about 60% of its strength left after defeating a red force split in two. — [Lanchester section 28](https://archive.org/stream/aircraftinwarfa00lancgoog/aircraftinwarfa00lancgoog_djvu.txt); [MacKay](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0606300). VERIFIED. Under the linear law splitting gives the same result as not splitting when the quality ratio is 1 (Stanescu et al.). VERIFIED
- Support (non-fighting) troops and unit definition: MacKay shows the choice of what counts as a "unit" does not change the outcome in the model if numbers scale by 1/N^2 and effectiveness by N, but weighting numbers by "fighting power" (e.g. 1,000 troops plus 20 tanks x 30 = 1,600 units) "cannot be correct" because the model's point is to distinguish numbers from effectiveness. — [MacKay, section 3.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0606300). VERIFIED

### Inferences
- The "2:1 outnumbered needs 4x quality" figure is exact only inside the homogeneous aimed-fire model and requires fully escalated fights to annihilation; with unequal exponents (fractional or piecewise frontage models) the factor lies between ratio^1 and ratio^2 only if the effective exponent does.

### Gaps
- No source found giving the quality factor for the piecewise frontage-capped model in closed form beyond eq. 19 and 26 of Atkinson et al.

## 5. What are the standard critiques of using Lanchester laws (Epstein, Lepingwell, Dupuy)?

### Takeaway
Critiques cluster in four groups: model assumptions (homogeneous, deterministic, fight to the death, no withdrawal or space-for-time, no diminishing returns); validation failure (no historical battle cleanly fit); misapplication of "unit" aggregation; and over-reliance by military analysts. Defenders keep the laws as heuristics.

### Cited Findings
- Epstein's three grounds (as summarised): (1) the models do not let an army reduce attrition by withdrawing and assume a fight to the death; (2) as a consequence there is no trading of space for time, because the battle duration formula (t_f) does not depend on territory given up; (3) the models lack diminishing marginal returns: for the square law the casualty-exchange ratio grows at a constant rate with force ratio, and "no crowding or force-to-space constraint" limits concentration. He also considers analysts' reliance on the equations "truly alarming", given "little historical or empirical evidence to support their use" (the latter phrase is Lepingwell 1987, p. 127). — [Mesterton-Gibbons 2016, section 2 and footnote 13](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf), summarising Epstein (1985, pp. 3-13) and quoting Lepingwell. VERIFIED (as paraphrase; Epstein 1985 and Lepingwell 1987 not read directly)
- Lepingwell (1987), "The Laws of Combat? Lanchester Reexamined," International Security 12(1): 89-134, calls Lanchester models "useful as a heuristic for thinking about combat interactions, albeit a heuristic with distinct limitations" (p. 127), as quoted. — [Lepingwell, DOI 10.2307/2538918](https://doi.org/10.2307/2538918) via [Mesterton-Gibbons 2016](https://www.math.fsu.edu/~mesterto/NewCourses/MAP5932/2016/Notes/lecture02.pdf). VERIFIED (quote as reproduced; MacKay also calls Lepingwell "an excellent introduction"). MacKay credits Lepingwell with pointing out that Lanchester's mixed-force rule (square of the sum of square roots) is wrong; the correct conserved quantity uses average effectiveness, r R^2 - g_ave G^2, with g_ave = (g1 G1 + g2 G2) / G when the enemy spreads fire in proportion to type. — [MacKay, section 3.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0606300). VERIFIED. My check: Lanchester's rule gives (sqrt(g1) G1 + sqrt(g2) G2)^2 = g1 G1^2 + g2 G2^2 + 2 sqrt(g1 g2) G1 G2, while MacKay's gives g1 G1^2 + g2 G2^2 + (g1 + g2) G1 G2, which is larger unless g1 = g2 (AM-GM: g1 + g2 >= 2 sqrt(g1 g2)). ESTIMATED
- Homer-Dixon (1987), "A Common Misapplication of the Lanchester Square Law", International Security 12: 135-139, argues against weighting units by fighting power before squaring. — cited in [MacKay's reference list](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0606300) and discussed in his section 3.4. VERIFIED (that it is the cited source for the controversy); content of Homer-Dixon's article not read
- Dupuy Institute position: Lanchester equations "have been weighed, they have been measured, and they have been found wanting"; nine independent tests with basically no result (see question 3). — [Dupuy Institute 2016](https://dupuyinstitute.org/2016/02/29/lanchester-equations-have-been-weighed/). REPORTED (interested party; I did not retrieve Dupuy's own books, "Numbers, Predictions and War" or "Understanding War").
- Assumption critiques from authors in favour of using the models: forces homogeneous; no movement, terrain, command or morale ("Range, terrain, movement, and all other factors that might influence the fight are either abstracted within the parameters or ignored entirely. Fights continue until the complete destruction of one force"). — [Stanescu et al. 2015](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12780). VERIFIED
- Atkinson, Kress and MacKay list three unrealistic implicit assumptions of the square law: no attrition effort wasted on dead targets (perfect target identification), each side can deploy all units at the outset, and the loser is totally annihilated (battles seldom run to annihilation; loss-tolerance thresholds). — [Atkinson, Kress and MacKay 2020, section 1](https://faculty.nps.edu/mkress/docs/Atkinson_Kress_MacKay_2020.pdf). VERIFIED
- Data critique: before-and-after data alone cannot validate any exponent because for any p, q one can find a and b that fit perfectly; only two-sided, time-phased data can discriminate, and few exist. — [Lucas and Turkes 2004, section 1](https://calhoun.nps.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bc44457c-0423-42cc-bd62-585149fbf95d/content). VERIFIED
- Determinism critique: under the square law with a probabilistic (Markov chain) reading, results "can be surprisingly variable, with sometimes one side winning and sometimes the other", and the histogram of net survivors is bimodal. Brian McCue argues the determinism is a neglected shortcoming and that the end state the square law predicts is "one of the least likely outcomes". — [Washburn 2000, section 3.1](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf) VERIFIED; McCue, [Beyond Lanchester, via Dupuy Institute blog blurb](https://dupuyinstitute.org/category/methodologies/lanchester-equations/) REPORTED (book blurb only)
- Lanchester's own caveats: the law "based on averages evidently does not hold rigidly when the numbers become small"; morale, leadership and other factors "cannot be accounted for in an equation"; in the open versus "dug in" the hypothesis fails; land operations have "a multitude of factors extraneous to the hypothesis whereby its operation may be suspended or masked". — [Lanchester 1916, section 21, 25, 35, 36](https://archive.org/stream/aircraftinwarfa00lancgoog/aircraftinwarfa00lancgoog_djvu.txt). VERIFIED

### Inferences
- The critiques that bear most on constricted frontage are Epstein's third (no force-to-space constraint) and Atkinson et al.'s second (all units deployable at the outset). Both say the unmodified square law has no mechanism for a frontage limit, which is what the capped models in question 2 add.

### Gaps
- Epstein (1985), "The Calculus of Conventional War" and Epstein (1997) were not read directly; Lepingwell's and Dupuy's full arguments were not read. The critique summary relies on Mesterton-Gibbons's summary and the Dupuy Institute blog.

## 6. Generalisations: Osipov, Peterson's logarithmic law, Helmbold's exponents, heterogeneous models, stochastic models

### Takeaway
The literature offers (a) exponent families (Bracken, Helmbold, Stanescu), (b) the logarithmic law (Peterson) as the p = 0, q = 1 end, (c) heterogeneous (multi-type) systems where the average-effectiveness form replaces Lanchester's square-root rule, (d) deployment-constrained and ambush variants, and (e) stochastic (Markov chain) versions in which small-force luck matters and the winner's outcome distribution is bimodal.

### Cited Findings
- Heterogeneous forces: with two Green types G1 and G2 of effectiveness g1, g2, and Red spreading fire in proportion to type (dG1/dt = -r R G1 / G, dG2/dt = -r R G2 / G, dR/dt = -g1 G1 - g2 G2), d/dt [r R^2 - (g1 G1 + g2 G2)(G1 + G2)] = 0, so the conserved quantity is r R^2 - g_ave G^2 with g_ave the number-weighted mean effectiveness. — [MacKay 2005, section 3.3, eq. 11-14](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0606300). VERIFIED. Washburn's notes also show multi-state-variable Lanchester systems (mine warfare, flak suppression, Battle of the Atlantic with Morse and Kimball equations) solved numerically, with a control-theoretic fire-allocation example; heterogeneous systems "require numerical solution but such solutions are not difficult". — [Washburn 2000, section 2.4](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf). VERIFIED
- Mixed aimed and unaimed fire is nonlinear: MacKay's asymmetric case, dR/dt = -g G R / R0 (Red's targets are easy to find while Red outnumbers) and dG/dt = -r G (Green's losses independent of Red numbers), gives Green exponential decay and R = R0 exp[(g G0 / (r R0)) (e^(-rt) - 1)]; he concludes that a technologically superior force in a target-rich environment "may be a linear- rather than a square-law battle". — [MacKay, section 3.7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0606300). VERIFIED
- Asymmetric ambush (Deitchman 1962) and deployment-constrained (Kress and Talmor 1999; Atkinson et al. 2020) variants: see question 2. VERIFIED (via the cited papers)
- Stochastic Lanchester: Washburn's treatment makes the state (m, n) a continuous-time Markov chain where, in time step delta, P[(m, n) -> (m-1, n)] = f(m, n) delta and P[(m, n) -> (m, n-1)] = g(m, n) delta, equivalently (embedded chain) the next kill is on side 1 with probability f / (f + g); for the square law the histogram of net survivors is bimodal. — [Washburn 2000, section 3](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf). VERIFIED
- Kress and Talmor (1999) use Markov stochastic Lanchester models to re-examine the 3:1 attacker rule, finding situations where a 3:1 ratio is reasonable and others where the ratio should be smaller or larger. — [J. Operational Research Society 50: 733-744](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/palgrave.jors.2600758). REPORTED (abstract only)
- Lanchester difference-equation version (MacKay eq. 8-9): R(n+1) = R(n) - g G(n), G(n+1) = G(n) - r R(n) gives r R^2 - g G^2 multiplied by (1 - r g) each step, so with r g much less than 1 the square-law quantity is approximately conserved over a battle of duration of order 1/r or 1/g. — [MacKay, section 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0606300). VERIFIED
- Time-varying and supply: Engel's Iwo Jima model adds a reinforcement rate r(t) to one equation; more generally "one can also investigate power laws other than the first and second, make the lethality coefficients functions of time, etc." — [Washburn 2000, section 2.1 and 2.4](https://faculty.nps.edu/awashburn/Files/Notes/Lanchester.pdf). VERIFIED
- A support-troops variant: if fighting effectiveness rises with the support ratio f = f0 ((N - P) / P)^kappa for P fighting units out of N total, fighting strength f P^2 is maximised at P = (1 - kappa/2) N (half the force in support when kappa = 1); the Ardennes Allies used a support ratio of about 0.8 against the Germans' 0.5. — [MacKay, section 3.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0606300). VERIFIED (MacKay's own illustrative model, not a result from the data)

### Inferences
- The Atkinson et al. paper and MacKay's asymmetric case both show that "which law applies" can change mid-battle (large initial ratios look linear, then square as the ratio falls, or the reverse as frontage reserves are exhausted), which is the formal sense in which sources call the outcome a hybrid.

### Gaps
- Helmbold's own generalisation (force-ratio-dependent attrition) and Taylor's variable-coefficient theory (Taylor 1983; Taylor and Parry 1975, [Operations Research 23(3): 522-533](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.23.3.522)) were seen only as titles and abstracts.
- No source retrieved on heterogeneous stochastic models beyond Washburn's general treatment.
- Cangiotti, Capolli and Sensi, "A Generalization of Lanchester's Model of Warfare" (arXiv 2112.06973), covers multi-army and multi-front extensions; I did not extract its results.
