Deep research task: terrain and force geometry in military doctrine, for use as design input to a real-time strategy game.

Goal. I am designing an RTS whose central promise is that a player who understands landscape, formation, vertical position (layers), the enemy's position and unit-type matchups can beat a much larger force. I want the real-world military record behind that promise: which principles of terrain, geometry and information let a smaller force beat a larger one, with concrete cases and numbers, and where the principle fails.

Game facts to translate into (use these terms in the "how a game could test it" part):
- Five vertical layers, stacked. A unit sits on one layer. Fire and sight cross layers only within a limited reach by unit type. In some eras every unit flies (free layers); in others units walk on ground with ramps and slopes, and a few units fly.
- Terrain: narrow passages (chokes), tall cover that blocks sight and fire, slopes. Rule already decided: high ground sees down, and the top of a slope is hard to climb to under fire.
- Sight: a "ping" reveals a layer and place; going silent hides. Finding the enemy first and firing first matters.
- What decides a fight: how many units on each side can get shots off, and for how long (the "firing count"), weighted by unit type. Equal forces in equal positions: the larger wins; the question is what position and geometry changes that.
- Five skills the game wants players to express: (1) landscape (fight where terrain limits how many enemies can fire), (2) formation (arrange units so more fire at once: concave arc, spread line, screen with long range behind), (3) layers (use height and sight on purpose), (4) enemy position (find first, fire first, bait, ambush, take the unwatched route), (5) unit-type matchup (put each type against the type it beats).
- Each play needs a counter (go around or over, outrange, ping behind cover, mass on one end of a line, refuse the fight). Some situations are unbeatable (full surround, closed trap).

Please cover, each with named historical cases, force sizes, casualty or outcome numbers, and sources:
1. Chokes and defiles: narrow-front defence against larger forces (Thermopylae and similar), and the cases where the narrow front failed (flanking path, being outflanked, siege by attrition).
2. High ground and reverse slope: what height actually gave (sight, range, charge momentum, cover) and what it cost; reverse-slope defence cases.
3. Concave and envelopment: double envelopment, crescent and horns (Cannae and later), the conditions needed (centre holds, enemy committed), and the standard counters.
4. Line versus column, frontage and the number of weapons that can bear: how frontage limits effective fighting strength; cases where a smaller force on a narrow front beat a larger one.
5. Defence in depth, screens and reserves: long-range or fragile troops behind a screen; reserves; the flank-guard problem.
6. Reconnaissance, surprise, ambush and bait: feigned retreat cases (Hastings, Mongol practice, Tannenberg-type), the numbers on how often it worked, and the counters (scouting ahead, refusing to pursue).
7. Combined arms and unit matchup: rock-paper-scissors in real doctrine (pike and cavalry, archers and cavalry, infantry and armour, anti-air), and the vulnerability of a specialist without protection.
8. Vertical and three-dimensional terrain: air power versus ground, naval and submarine depth as a layered-sight analogue, mountain and urban multi-level fighting; how sight and fire cross levels.
9. Force ratio: the empirical record on how large an advantage is needed to attack a prepared defence on good terrain (the 3-to-1 rule and its critics, Dupuy's terrain and posture multipliers, Lanchester square versus linear law and where each applies). Give the multipliers with their sources.
10. Counter-cases: where terrain advantage did not save the smaller force, and why.

For each principle give: the claim in one sentence; two or three cases with date, force sizes and result; the numbers; the source (author, title, year; flag secondary or contested sources); and one line on the conditions under which it holds. Prefer quantitative sources (Dupuy, Lanchester literature, Biddle's "Military Power", Clausewitz and Jomini on terrain, operations research studies, historical battle databases) over narrative. Say plainly where evidence is thin or disputed. Do not invent figures. Do not pad with generic strategy advice.

Output: a structured report with one section per topic above, a table of principle / best case / key number / source, and a closing ranked list of the principles most useful for making small-force-beats-large-force possible and learnable in a game.
