# Goal 2: bases of operations and objectives, options

Written 2026-10-02 for Westley. Surface level; standard and wild options per part. The flag is Q-159-11.

## Already decided

- Every side has a base of operations it must protect. It is not always a city. It receives resources from elsewhere, generates them on site, or both, depending on the simulation and the mission.
- Economic locations are spread over the map; the significant resources sit opposite the objective.
- Settlements can be grown into further bases. Staying home means being overrun. Production can be built forward.
- A base can span all five layers, and an attacker has to cross them.
- From 2026-09-22, said of a city: "If it dies, the battle is over, re-enforcements can't come and you can't replenish your forces (classic RTS)."

## What the bible has that this must fit

- **Ten mission archetypes** (E-10 S-073): defensive stand, siege breaking, multi-front defense, assault, reconquest, containment, interception, suppression, counterinsurgency, rear guard or evacuation.
- **Five outcomes, not two** (E-10 S-077): a mission is scored into total failure, partial failure, partial success, strong success, total success.
- **Reinforcement structures** (E-15 S-126, S-127): 2 to 8 garrisons per map, capturable, producing units at no cost; the starting force is a fifth of the full force. This was written before there was an economy.

---

## Part A. What happens when a base of operations falls

**Standard**

- **A1. The battle is over.** The mission ends at that moment and is scored. Classic, and what was said on 2026-09-22.
- **A2. Cut off.** Production, reinforcement and shipments stop. The fight goes on with what is on the field until the clock or the objective ends it, and the loss weighs on the score.
- **A3. Fall back.** If the side holds an expansion or a forward base, that becomes the base of operations, at a cost. With none, A1 or A2 applies. Expansions become insurance.

**Wild**

- **A4. Falls by layer.** A base on fitted height bands falls in stages. Each band lost takes something with it: the lower town takes workers and housing, the middle takes production, the top takes command. The base is gone only when the top band is taken. A plain base on one band falls at once.
- **A5. Occupied, not destroyed.** A base is captured, not razed. The side that takes it gets its production and its people, and the side that lost it can take it back while the clock runs. A base can change hands more than once, and the region follows whoever holds it at the end.
- **A6. The long retreat.** When a base falls, the mission becomes an evacuation on the spot: get the workers, the survivors and what can be carried to an exit. The score is what gets out. The game already has this archetype.

## Part B. What kind of base a mission gives

**Standard**

- **B1. By archetype.** A table: which side has which kind of base in each of the ten archetypes.

| Archetype | Player's base | Enemy's base |
|---|---|---|
| Defensive stand, multi-front defense | home city, generating on site | forward base, supplied from elsewhere |
| Siege breaking | relief camp outside, supplied; the city inside is the objective | siege camp |
| Assault, reconquest | forward base, supplied | the city or fortress being taken |
| Containment, interception | field camp on the enemy's path | column or staging camp |
| Suppression, counterinsurgency | garrison in a city | hidden cells among the settlements |
| Rear guard, evacuation | the place being left | pursuing forward base |

- **B2. By supply distance.** The simulation decides, not the archetype. A mission in a region the civilization owns gets a home base that generates on site. One in a neighbouring region gets a forward base with both. One deep in foreign ground gets an expedition camp that lives on shipments. The archetype sets only the objectives.

**Wild**

- **B3. A base that travels.** For the moving archetypes (interception, rear guard, containment) the base is a column: a baggage train, a carrier, a caravan. Set up, it produces and cannot move. Packed, it moves and produces nothing. Where and when to set up is the decision.
- **B4. The pair chooses a posture.** Before the mission the pair picks how to arrive: dug in (a large base, a small starting force), expedition (a small base, more shipments), or raid (no base, only a hidden cache; nothing to lose and nothing to build). This sits against E-10 S-079, "Orders, not menus", and would need to be reconciled with it.

## Part C. Base, objective and the rich resources

- **C1. Three corners, always.** Base, objective and the significant resources are three separate places on every map, with the resources on the far side from the objective.
- **C2. It depends on who is defending.** When the objective is your own base (defensive stand), the rich resources are far from home and working them means leaving the walls. When the objective is the enemy's base (assault), they are behind you or off to a flank, and guarding them splits the attack. When the objective moves (escort, evacuation), they lie away from its route.
- **C3 (wild). The rich resource is a second objective.** Holding the significant resource at the end counts in the score beside the mission objective, and the simulation reads it: the region comes out richer or poorer. "Pick one or fight for both" becomes a choice between two kinds of success.

## Part D. The reinforcement structures already in the bible

E-15's garrisons produce units at no cost. With an economy that makes units, they need a new meaning.

- **D1. Forward works, ready-made.** A garrison is a production building already standing out on the map. Whoever holds it produces there, paying from their economy. It is the fastest way to a forward base.
- **D2. They are the neutral settlements.** A garrison is a settlement of the neutral populace. Holding it gives what the settlement has: a trickle of a resource, local units, or a trading post. This gives the contested populace its payoff, and it is the commander who wins it.
- **D3. Two games.** With nobody in the economy seat the garrisons work as written today, and the economy rules stay in the background. In the paired mode they are replaced by D1 or D2.
- **D4 (wild). Strongpoints on the layers.** Garrisons are placed at the slopes between height bands. Holding one holds the way up or down, and gives production on that band. Capturing garrisons is how a force climbs a mountainside city.

---

## How the parts lean on each other

- A4 and D4 both need the fitted height bands, and go together.
- A5 needs the contested populace and suits D2.
- B2 follows directly from "depends on the simulation and specific mission".
- C3 changes how missions are scored and touches E-10 S-077.

## My lean (opinion, Westley's call)

A3 with A4, B2 with B3 for the moving archetypes, C2 with C3, D1 with D4.

- **A3 and A4** make expansions and height matter at the moment a base is lost, and keep "classic" as the result when there is nothing to fall back to.
- **B2** is the simulation deciding, as already agreed, and **B3** gives the moving missions a base that makes sense.
- **C3** turns the resource-or-objective choice into something the simulation remembers.
- **D1 and D4** keep the garrisons, give them a cost, and put them where the layers are fought over.
