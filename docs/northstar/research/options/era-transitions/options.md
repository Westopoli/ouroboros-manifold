# Era transitions: options

Three ways a campaign crosses from one age's pack into the next, each proposing what decides the crossing. All three keep the same rescale at the boundary — nobody arrives at late game, the leading civilization still leads — and the same fading of the earliest names into distant myth.

## Option A — The Long Horizon

The crossing is read off the galaxy itself. For several chapters before it happens, the chronicle names a civilization outgrowing its age: workshops past what the old methods explain, a leading power's reach past living memory. When that reach tops out for its age, the next time jump carries the galaxy across.

**Play vignette.** Your debrief ends and the chronicle panel slides in, the map turning through the years. The second line printed isn't a battle or a birth — it opens in the register the game reserves for its biggest news, naming an age that has run its course. A garrison emblem along a contested edge reforms mid-turn into a different silhouette, tier for tier. When the panel closes, the galaxy is unmistakably yours — same borders, same rival colors, same lineage name — dressed for an age you haven't played in yet.

**At the boundary.** The galaxy view keeps its ordinary animation; the chronicle's opening entry borrows the register of a faction's fall or founding — the same register that, for several chapters before, had already been naming strain and outgrowing, so you knew this one was coming. You still recognize your bloodline, rivals' colors, home region and neighbors; what changed is every faction's reach rescaled so the leader still leads but nobody starts postured for the endgame, and earlier commanders now appear only as sayings and place names, fewer each jump after.

**What decides the crossing:** the leading civilization's overall reach for its age is tracked the way the chronicle already tracks any civilizational measure; the jump that would push that reach past what the age can express is the one that crosses.

**The loop:** past the last owned age, the same measure keeps being read, and the jump that would have kept "growing" instead lands back in the first owned age, rescaled the way a forward crossing rescales. In a match, a peer without the next pack cannot receive the crossing; the jump keeps landing on owned ages until every peer's set covers where it lands, and the lobby's common-era note (R-143-03) is what the table sees meanwhile.

**Honors:** R-148-02, R-148-03 as rewritten, R-143-01 to 03, sacred principle 3, A-321.
**Strains:** a civilization-wide measure the player never manages directly, so the chronicle alone must make the coming crossing legible; a quiet civilization could in principle never outgrow its age.

**Prior art.** None for a trigger tied to a civilization's own reach outgrowing its age. Total War's different-eras-as-different-titles model is the nearest counter-model — VERIFIED, `docs/legacy/games/total_war_franchise.md` — but it never crosses inside one continuous world.

**Tradeoffs.** The crossing reads as earned, not scheduled, at the cost of a countdown with no visible number.

## Option B — The Appointed Turn

The crossing is counted, not read. Every campaign has an announced number of chapters belonging to its current age, stated up front in the same onboarding conversation that sets difficulty and mission preferences. When the appointed chapter's time jump runs, it is the crossing, on schedule.

**Play vignette.** Your ninth chapter in this age ends in a costly win, and the briefing before it already told you this might be the last of its kind. The mission plays like any other. Afterward the chronicle panel opens as always, but its first line simply says so: the age you played is over, in the voice that announces a fallen commander's successor. The map turns through its years with no added flourish. When the panel slides away, you're looking at a galaxy dressed for the next age, briefing waiting, count back to one.

**At the boundary.** The galaxy view runs its ordinary animation with no spectacle; the chronicle's first entry plainly states the age has ended, unsurprising because every loading screen and briefing since the start quietly counted down to it. You recognize your bloodline, rival colors, regions, and your commander if they survived — onboarding's promise kept; what changed is reach and standing rescaled the same way any crossing does, and the chronicle begins naming earlier commanders as history rather than living cast, trimmed further each later crossing.

**What decides the crossing:** the player sets, or the game proposes and the player confirms, an age length in chapters at campaign start; the chapter that completes that count is the one whose jump crosses.

**The loop:** past the last owned age, the count restarts at one and the next jump lands back in the first owned age — same galaxy, same ledger, rescaled again — stated exactly as the first count was. In a match, a peer missing the next pack means the appointed count can't be honored table-wide; the jump lands on whichever owned age is next in the shared count, and the lobby explains why, same as R-143-03 already promises.

**Honors:** R-148-02, R-148-03 as rewritten, R-143-01 to 03, R-063-02, sacred principle 3.
**Strains:** a legible, fully scheduled trigger — nothing about a mission's outcome moves the number, so principle 5's game-theoretic weight sits lightly here.

**Prior art.** CK3's cultural-innovation progress across one continuous run is a structural cousin — VERIFIED, `docs/legacy/games/crusader_kings_3.md` — but CK3 never crosses into a differently-skinned age; the resemblance is only "a counted, visible progression."

**Tradeoffs.** Total predictability, easy to plan around, at the cost of feeling scheduled rather than won or suffered.

## Option C — The Last Word (wild)

No measure and no count decides this crossing; the chronicle decides it by running out of things to say. Every age's stock of stories has a shape, and as a campaign plays inside one age, the chronicle leans on the same handful of framings more than once. The game already tracks, invisibly, how repetitive its own storytelling has become; when a jump would otherwise tell a story it has essentially told already, that jump becomes the crossing instead.

**Play vignette.** You've noticed it without naming it: your last three chronicles leaned on the same shape of line for your rivals' border fights and your family's news. This jump's panel opens as ever, but its last line — where an ordinary jump would close on a regional entry — instead reads like a book closing a chapter: commentary on the story having been told before, in a voice you haven't heard the chronicle use. The map keeps turning past where it would normally stop. When it settles, the galaxy carries the same names forward into a different age's dress.

**At the boundary.** The turning-years animation runs longer than an ordinary jump's; the closing entry is written as commentary on repetition, a register no ordinary jump uses — felt in hindsight more than seen coming, since the last few chronicles had started to rhyme with each other before the game ever said why. You recognize your bloodline, rivals, and map exactly as before; what changed is the rescale, landing the same as any crossing, and named figures from the closed age fading into distant myth — the age's own last word about itself was that it had nothing left to say.

**What decides the crossing:** the chronicle already scores and categorizes every entry it writes (E-09 S-066, S-067); this option reads that scoring across a run of jumps within one age, and when the freshest available entries keep landing in categories already spent, that jump crosses instead of printing more of the same.

**The loop:** past the last owned age, the next jump lands back in the first owned age with the same galaxy and ledger, and the chronicle's sense of what it has "already said" resets with it — the returning age tells its stories fresh again, now about names faded to myth. In a match, a peer without the next pack means the chronicle keeps closing out ages it has already told everything it can about, one after another, until it reaches an age every peer owns; the lobby's common-era note (R-143-03) is what the table sees meanwhile.

**Honors:** R-148-02, R-148-03 as rewritten, R-143-01 to 03, sacred principle 3, and the chronicle's purpose as the long memory that "reads like history and not a log," which this option ties the crossing itself to.
**Strains:** R-065-01's five-to-ten-entry budget is sized for one age's ordinary stock, not for a mechanism measuring that stock running low; a player who plays very differently from the authored fragment pools could exhaust an age's freshness far earlier or later than another, with no external sign why.

**Prior art.** None located; no shipped game ties a structural transition to its own narration detecting repetition in itself. Epoch Cycle's collapse-rebuild cycling is the nearest cyclical-structure precedent — REPORTED, `docs/legacy/games/epoch_cycle.md` — but its boundary is a collapse condition, not narration running dry, and its post-mortem (thin inter-cycle differentiation) is the failure mode this option avoids.

**Tradeoffs.** The crossing feels like a genuinely earned narrative beat, timed to nothing the player can grind, at the cost of a trigger with no visible number to check.

## Findings

- R-143-03 limits a match to eras every peer owns, but section 8's loop rule for a missing pack — "the jump keeps going... until every player has an era" — never states what happens if that peer never buys it. No option can honor a trigger with no described terminus; all three assume the jump settles on the common owned set R-143-03 already names, a reconciliation the brief does not write.
- E-09's fixed five-to-ten-entry chronicle budget (R-065-01) fits one jump inside one age. A jump crossing more than one boundary at once, the multi-peer catch-up case section 8 describes, has more history to close out than any option's boundary framing can hold without crowding the ordinary lineage and chain entries the budget already promises. No option resolves this in player-facing terms; it is a budget question the brief leaves open.
- Q-148-04 (how the loop treats tech, characters, and the chronicle on a second pass through the same age) stays open after section 8, which answers only the first loop — names fade further into myth. What a character already reduced to myth becomes on a second pass — fainter, gone, or refreshed — is not confirmed anywhere in the brief; each option assumed further fading.
