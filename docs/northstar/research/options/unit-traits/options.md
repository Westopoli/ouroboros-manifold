# Unit passive traits: options

Two ways a commander picks up cards that make a force theirs, on top of the 20 to 40 unit roster, in the Dead by Daylight register Westley named. Both keep every launch rule intact and differ only in when a card is chosen and who it belongs to.

## Option A — Standing Orders

Before a mission, the commander opens a board listing every unit type they can field and a shared library of trait cards, all available from the first chapter — nothing leveled up or unlocked by play. The player drags up to two cards onto each unit type they plan to bring; an untouched type fields with stats alone. The same board, filled from the same library and cap, builds the enemy's force. A card is a fixed, named thing — "Long Stride," "Bulwark," "Scorched Earth" — one line of text; a player who always gives Raiders the speed card and Vanguards the damage-eating card is playing a build, the way a Dead by Daylight main runs the same four perks match after match.

**Play vignette.** You open Standing Orders before the drop. Your Raiders already carry Long Stride from last chapter — you leave it. You slot Bulwark onto your Vanguards for the first time: survive one killing blow per fight at 1 HP, once. You load in. Forty seconds into the push, a Vanguard takes a Bombard hit that should have erased it; instead it staggers, flashes white, and holds at a sliver of health. You pull it back to a Warden.

A trait is a card, and it belongs to the unit type: every Raider you bring runs whatever cards you gave Raiders. Individualizing a force means building a small, repeatable set of type-to-card pairings — a signature, even though the commander carries no level. The enemy fields cards too, from the identical library under the identical cap, so a fight can turn on a card the player never touched.

A rule-changing card reads as a moment: Bulwark's stagger-and-flash is unmistakable, then it shows a spent icon for that fight. A flat-number card, Long Stride's plus-15-percent speed, is never called out — felt only as a Raider that keeps beating you to the flank. The portrait strip shows a small badge under any unit whose type carries an active, unspent rule-changer; pure-number cards carry no badge — learned by feel, same as a Warden's aura today.

Standing Orders honors both-sides-same-profiles outright. It strains "no persistent player level" by sidestepping DBD's model rather than reconciling with it: everything is open from mission one, so there is nothing to level, but the "grow into it" feeling DBD sells through its grind is absent — the pool a rookie sees is the pool a veteran sees. Opt-in complexity holds: a new player leaves every slot empty and plays the roster as documented today.

An abused, nerfed card shows the change on the same board next chapter — the line of text changes, or a bracketed number drops — felt the next time that unit type takes a hit it used to shrug off.

**Honors.** R-083-01 (zero to two traits per unit), R-080-01 (identical library and cap both sides), A-061 (no level, no earn-in), opt-in complexity (empty slots are free).
**Strains.** DBD's own earn-and-grow feeling — every card stands in the window from day one, so none is discovered late.

**Prior art.** Age of Empires 3's Home City card deck, a limited pre-built deck of cards that alter units and economy before a match — REPORTED, Age of Empires Series Wiki and AoE3 Heaven, cross-confirmed. Dead by Daylight's four-perk loadout from an open pool per character — REPORTED, Dead by Daylight Wiki; the wiki also describes an earn-and-teach economy this option does not carry over.

**Tradeoffs.** A stable, legible build carried and refined chapter to chapter, an enemy card read the instant it fires. The cost is sameness: two commanders who both favor Bulwark-Vanguards see the identical trick from each other every time.

## Option B — The Muster (wild)

There is no card board. Before the mission, the game deals both commanders a hand — the same number of cards each, from one shared pool — and lays each card onto one specific unit already in the roster, not a type. The draw leans, never locks, toward what that civilization has just lived through in the chronicle: an insurgency-scarred faction turns up "Ambush Reflexes" more, a famine-scarred one turns up "Rationed" more — but the enemy draws from the same pool at the same hand size, so the lean is flavor, never an edge. The player gets one short pass to swap two dealt cards between units; after that the hand is fixed for the mission and discarded when it ends.

**Play vignette.** The briefing hands you five cards, dealt onto five named units already in your force. One is your Raider, Voss — "Second Wind." You tap it: flee at double speed for three seconds instead of dying, once. You swap it onto a different Raider you trust more at the front, your one reshuffle, then load in. Late in the fight, that Raider takes what should be the killing hit and instead bolts past the line at a dead sprint, alive for three more seconds before it drops — long enough to feel like it earned them.

A trait is a card here too, but dealt, not shopped, and it belongs to one soldier, not a type — this Raider carries Second Wind, not every Raider you own. Individualizing a force means noticing the hand you keep getting dealt and leaning into it — a commander long at war starts to recognize its veterans' habits — rather than assembling a fixed loadout in a menu. The enemy is dealt the same way, from the same pool, at the same hand size.

A rule-changing card still reads as a moment — Second Wind's sprint-then-drop is as unmistakable as Bulwark's stagger — but legible only on that one unit's portrait, the only place its icon appears. A flat-number card is felt the same way as Option A: never announced, only noticed over a fight.

The Muster honors "no persistent player level" as hard as a rule can be honored: nothing survives past the mission it was dealt into, so there is no build to level or carry. It strains "individualized to playstyle" on purpose: a player cannot reliably reproduce a signature pairing chapter to chapter, only nudge one hand with one reshuffle, so the deliberate build-crafting the DBD citation describes is present only in miniature. Both-sides-same-profiles holds structurally, since neither side chooses. Opt-in complexity holds in its strongest form — a player who never opens the briefing screen still gets a dealt hand, auto-placed.

Abuse and nerfing show up as a card that turns up less often, or does less when it does; the player notices an old favorite going quiet, not a menu line changing.

**Honors.** A-061 (zero carryover, nothing to level), R-080-01 (identical pool and hand size), opt-in complexity (strongest form — no screen required).
**Strains.** Deliberate, repeatable build-crafting — the "playstyle" half of the citation — since the player steers one reshuffle, not the whole hand.

**Prior art.** Mechabellum's per-round card draft, upgrades dealt from a pool rather than shopped freely — REPORTED, TheGamer and Steam community threads, for the dealt-not-shopped shape only. Tying the deal's weighting to a persistent, per-faction simulated history, and binding a card to one named unit instead of a type — AMBIGUOUS, no shipped precedent located for either half combined.

**Tradeoffs.** A fight that always feels like this muster of soldiers, not a build optimized in a menu, and a story reason for why a card showed up. The cost is control: a player who wants a favorite pairing every mission cannot, and the one reshuffle is the only lever against a hand they dislike.

## Findings

Two brief constraints could not be fully honored, named here rather than guessed past.

Section 8 asks for both DBD-level customizability — many cards, individualized to playstyle — and, elsewhere, zero persistence under A-061. The two pull against each other: DBD's own customizability comes from an earn-and-level economy across played hours, exactly what A-061 forbids. Option A opens the whole pool from turn one, at the cost of the "grow into it" feeling; Option B discards everything every mission, at the cost of deliberate repeatable builds. Neither delivers DBD's shape whole; each keeps the part Westley's other fixed rules leave room for.

R-083-01 and R-083-02 still read, today, as if a trait is a fixed fact "stated in the profile," authored once per unit type at build time. Both options move the choice of which cards apply to a pre-mission moment instead — the shift Section 8's "cards" language calls for — so those rules will need rewriting to describe a chosen-per-mission card, whichever option is picked. That rewrite sits outside this pass's write access and is named here rather than made.
