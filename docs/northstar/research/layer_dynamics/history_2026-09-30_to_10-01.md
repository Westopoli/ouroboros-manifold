# Layer dynamics: the layer lab

## 1. Purpose and status

Opened 2026-09-30. Design phase for the research program in register `Q-008-02` and research queue `Q76`. It feeds `Q-008-03` (the cross-layer numbers), E-01 S-008, E-12 (fog and sensors) and E-14 S-125 (what hard-mode AI strives for). Nothing here is bible text. When the lab yields numbers they are folded into `algorithms.md` and the S-008 rules, and this document is then a record.

The question: how do units on different layers damage and see each other so that the engagement's conditions, not the raw numbers, decide who wins? The method: a fast standalone C simulator (the layer lab, `toys/layerlab/`) that carries the system's constraints, run in small engagements that vary everything an RTS varies, read after every run, tuned, and run again.

## 3. Fixed constraints

These come from the bible and the register; the lab does not vary them unless a run says so.

- Five layers, always; layer 1 is the bottom (Q-001-01, Q-001-02). A unit is on exactly one layer at every tick.
- Terrain blocks layers at places (E-01 S-001). A unit never occupies a blocked position and layer.
- Layer changes on the route: a unit takes the shortest route to its goal across every layer it may use, changing layer on the way where that is shorter (E-01 R-001-05, `Q-001-04` closed 2026-10-01). The lab keeps an off switch only because a control needs an off state. What the ruling leaves open is `Q-001-05` (distance or time, the layer a flying unit ends on, pending transfers and preferences); the lab takes the recommended reading there and can vary it.
- A transition is one transition of one duration regardless of distance (Q-002-01), by unit type (Q-004-01: Raider 1.0 s, Interceptor 1.2 s, Vanguard 2.5 s, Warden 2.0 s, Bombard 4.0 s, Bastion 5.0 s). The old layer may attack the unit until the halfway point, the new layer from the halfway point on; visibility follows the normal cross-layer sensor rules (Q-004-02). E-01 R-004-02 carries the same rule.
- Slopes (Q-004-03, Westley 2026-09-30: "Choice 2 set by the slope, no need for a switch."): a slope is a band of ground joining two layers, adjacent or not. On a slope the transition is the unit's position along it, not a timer: the time is slope length divided by unit speed, the halfway point is the slope's midline, and a unit may stop or turn back (E-01 R-001-06, R-004-06 to R-004-08). The timed rule above applies only to a transfer away from a slope. The lab builds this one way, with no timed-versus-slope switch. A unit on a slope counts as on the layer on its side of the midline (Q-004-04).
- Unit movement kinds (Q-004-04, Westley 2026-09-30): flying and swimming units change layer anywhere nothing blocks, by a timed transfer; walking units stand only where a layer has land, change layer only by walking a slope, and never rise in place (E-01 R-001-07).
- A transitioning unit may attack, in a timed transfer or on a slope, while it can see its target, and stops when the transition takes that sight away (E-01 R-004-02).
- `Q-004-05` is closed with the recommended readings (2026-10-01): movement kind is set per unit type in the era pack and every Settled Reach type flies; the map carries a ground flag per layer; a slope spanning several layers has a cross-over at each layer boundary; a walking unit ordered to a layer paths to a slope, or the order is rejected.
- Every transition has a cross-over point: halfway through a timed transfer, the midline of a slope. Before it the unit is on the layer it is leaving, for attacking, being attacked and sensing; after it, on the layer it is moving to (E-01 R-004-02, the invariants). It is a matter of location, not timing.
- A unit never fires at a target it cannot see (`Q-008-04`, E-01 R-008-04). There is no blind fire.
- Vision across layers shrinks with distance: production uses factor 0.50 one layer away, 0.20 two layers away, none beyond, by absolute offset in either direction (`csrc/fog.c:585`); vision is symmetric by layer distance to start (`Q-005-02`, closed 2026-10-01), and asymmetric vision stays a candidate only if the top layer gets a counter; vantage nodes and interference zones modify it; cover blocks sight by height and never changes damage or movement (E-01 S-007).
- Production combat is same-layer only (Q-008-01). Every cross-layer rule in the lab is a hypothesis under test, not a fact about the game.
- Layer damage is weighted much more heavily than abilities and passives (Q-083-02, hub principle 4): an ability never outranks position in the lab either.
- Determinism: same seed and command stream give a byte-identical trace.

The goal is to create a game with significant strategic depth. We get to this goal by tuning all the variables we laid out such that that sentence becomes a reality." He added that the definition may take as long as it needs: "However long we need to clearly define that, and bring it down to specifications and think it through thoroughly, I'm ok with that, as I said this is the most important/distinctive part of the game".

### 4.1 Five skills

| Skill | What the skilled player does | What the naive player does | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1 Landscape | Fights where terrain limits how many enemies can fire: a choke, the far side of a slope's midline, behind tall cover | Fights wherever the armies meet | "understanding of the landscape" |
| S2 Formation | Arranges units so more of them fire at once: a concave, a screen | Moves as one blob | "unit formation" |
| S3 Layers | Uses the three layer actions on purpose: pings the layer and place that matters before committing; keeps the striking force silent and lets a decoy emit; puts each unit type on the layer from which its reach covers the enemy and the enemy's does not | Stays on one layer, never pings, leaves everything emitting | "layer awareness/intentionality"; the three actions are Westley's decision of 2026-10-01 (`Q-005-03`) |
| S4 Enemy position | Finds the enemy with pings, so it shoots first and engages from where the enemy cannot answer; and sets traps, engaging on purpose with a lesser force to draw the enemy into a surround | Has no scouting; meets the enemy when it bumps into it | "the other players current position"; and, 2026-10-01: "S4 not only shoots first, but induces traps, shoots on purpose with a lesser force to bring the enemies force into a trap (surround or somethign)." |
| S5 Unit-type matchup | Arranges its own unit types against the enemy types they beat | Sends every type at whatever is nearest | 2026-10-01: "S2 is not just about more units firing at once, it's also about the rock-paper-scissors, arranging their own rocks agains the enemies scissors, their own paper against the enemies rocks. Yes expand this to an S5, because it's bigger than just a subset of S2." |

The first prototype sets each side's arrangement once, by script. A reactive version, where one side or both re-form during the fight to keep their rocks on the enemy's scissors, is wanted after the prototype if it succeeds (Westley, 2026-10-01); it is not in this build.

Ruled by Westley, 2026-10-01:
- Skill is represented by scripted plays: for each skill a skilled play and a naive play.
- Every skill together beats a much larger force. First answer: about 2x. Asked about the band arithmetic (advantages multiply: five skills at 1.3x each combine to 3.7x) he answered "I like C" (singles of about 30 percent, full mastery worth 3x or more), added "but this is abstract and not very functional", and later confirmed 30 percent and 3x as rough sizes only.
- Big upsets need combinations; each skill alone gives a modest edge.
- With identical units, formation, vision and layer organisation the larger force wins. His words: "Hypothetically this situation would *never* happen in the real game, given there are 5 different layers, different vision, the landscape will be autogenerated and not symmetrical." So it is a control, not a scenario.

### 4.1a Skilled plays and their counters (accepted by Westley in outline, 2026-10-01: "your list looks good", "Everything else looks good")

The naive play, the same for every skill: a line abreast on one layer, walking straight at the enemy and shooting the nearest target; unit types mixed evenly along the line; no ping, everything emitting; the same units and cost as the skilled side would have at equal size. It does stop at its own range to shoot.

A counter is what the larger side does to pull the skilled play's edge back toward even. "Later" marks plays and counters that need orders to change during the fight (rung K4).

| Skill | Skilled play | Counter |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | Hold a choke: stand just behind a narrow passage so few enemies can fire at once | Go around or over it on another layer; or stand outside and outrange it |
| S1 | Hold the top of a slope, far enough past the midline that climbers cannot reach | Take another way up, or fly; or shell the top from below with a type that fires upward |
| S1 | Fight from behind tall cover | Get sight first: ping behind the cover, or move to a layer above its height |
| S2 | Concave around the point where the enemy arrives | Stop at the edge of range; or throw the whole force at one horn of the arc |
| S2 | Spread to full width so every unit is in range together | Mass the whole force on one end of the line, so only the few defenders at that end can fire, and work along it |
| S2 | Long-range units behind a screen of tough ones | Reach the back line from another layer; or send fast units around the screen |
| S3 | Ping the layer and place that matters before committing | Move after being pinged so the picture is stale (later); or show a decoy to be pinged |
| S3 | A silent striking force with an emitting decoy | Ping the likely approaches; do not chase the emitter (later) |
| S3 | Place each type where its reach covers the enemy and the enemy's does not | Change layer to leave their reach and enter yours; bring the type that fires upward |
| S4 | Find the enemy with pings and fire first | Go silent so less is found; relocate after the alert (later) |
| S4 | Take the route or layer the enemy is not watching | Watch more routes with pings; hold a reserve in the middle |
| S4 | Bait: a small group engages and falls back, the main force closes a surround (later) | Do not chase, or chase with a small detachment only (later) |
| S4 | Ambush: wait unseen and hold fire until the enemy is inside the concave (later) | Ping ahead before advancing; send a vanguard first (later) |
| S5 | Line each type up against the type it beats | Mix types evenly so there is no clean matchup; re-shuffle to mirror (later; the reactive idea) |
| S5 | Each type shoots the type it beats first | Keep the targeted type behind a screen |
| S5 | Keep the vulnerable type away from its counter | Reach it from another layer, or flank it |

The roll-up counter, as shown to Westley when he asked for a picture of it:

```
D  D  D  D  D  D  D  D  D  D     defender: one wide line of 10

                        A  A  A
                        A  A  A  attacker: everything at the right end
                        A  A
```

Only the defenders at that end are in range; the attacker destroys them with its whole force, then works along the line.

Almost every counter is another skill, which is hub principle 5 (counters have counters). The ping is the way to see far; the seer is dropped, or stays only at a very high cost (Westley, 2026-10-01: "the pinging should replace the seer, or make the seer really expensive"; `Q-005-04`).

Settled 2026-10-01, verbatim: "Yes refusing a fight counts as a counter, a skilled player only takes a battle that they can win, skilled players make it so that the opponent doesn't have a choice. - must have a counter only on most, some situations are unbeatable (like a full surround or a trap where the door gets closed). 1: ending ahead by a margin 2: headcount" So: refusing the fight is a counter, and the mark of skill is leaving the opponent no choice; a play needs a counter on most maps, not all, and a closed trap or a full surround is allowed to have none. In the lab the larger side is made to attack, so refusing the fight cannot be played as a counter there; C6 is checked for the other counters, and how refusal is to be checked is open.

### 4.2 The measure

- **Margin.** One signed number per fight: the share of side A's starting strength left, minus side B's. It replaces win rate, which is a probability in a system with no dice (pass 4: combat is deterministic once units are in contact).
- **Break-even ratio, R\*.** For one rule set, one situation and one pair of plays (a skilled side against a naive side): the enemy force, as a multiple of the skilled side's force, at which the fight is even. R\* = 1.3 means the play is worth 30 percent more army. The multiple is by headcount (Westley, 2026-10-01). So that a head on one side is worth a head on the other, both sides field the same mix of unit types in the same proportions; cost (the Q-074-01 power rating) is reported beside it, not used for the multiple.
- **Beating.** The skilled side beats a force when it ends ahead by a margin (Westley, 2026-10-01: "ending ahead by a margin"). The size of the margin that counts is a draft (§4.3).
- **Premium.** Real maps are not symmetric, so a map can favour one start. R0 is the break-even of naive against naive on that map; a skill's premium is R\* / R0. The premium is reported beside the firing advantage (§4.3).
- **Skill profile.** For one rule set: the premium of every combination of the five skills (5 singles, 10 pairs, 10 triples, 5 fours, the full set), plus controls and counter-plays.
- **An honest R\*.** A stalemate is not a win, and the larger, naive side always has to attack, or a side that never moves never loses. "Naive" is a ladder: a blob, a line abreast, and a line that holds still; the last isolates the hold-against-advance effect, which is real and cuts both ways in the run log (in pass 1 a holding line was out-shot by a line converging on it; in a later check a standing side beat packets walking in). Conditions are judged against the line abreast.

### 4.3 The conditions (restated around units firing; the restatement confirmed by Westley 2026-10-01: "yes it matches, proceed")

Westley, 2026-10-01, on fixed pass marks per skill, verbatim: "Q4: Remember, it depends on the number of units that can get shots, not a basic ratio. We need to capture and track the number of units that are able to get shots off on each side, and compare the about of time and number for both. That will indicate the quality of the choke, not just that they should do *1.3x* better because it's not as simple as that."

So a skill has no fixed worth. A play is worth what it does to the firing count in that situation: a wide choke is worth little, a narrow one a great deal. The lab therefore tracks, for every fight and each side, how many units are able to fire and for how long (unit-seconds of fire, and the count through time), and judges a rule set on two things: whether skill wins the firing count, and whether the result follows it.

- **Firing advantage** of a play in a situation: the skilled side's unit-seconds of fire per head, against the naive side's, and the same comparison window by window through the fight. This is the "quality of the choke" in his words, and the quality of any other position.
- **R\*** stays the measure of the result (§4.2): the headcount multiple at which the fight is even.

A rule set passes when all of these hold across the sampled maps:

| # | Condition | What is checked |
|---|---|---|
| C0 | Control | identical forces, identical play, mirrored map: equal firing counts and R\* = 1.0 |
| C1 | Skill wins the firing count | each skilled play, where the map offers it, gives its side more units firing for longer than the naive side gets. For unit-type matchup (S5) the count is weighted by type: a unit firing at the type it beats counts for more (§6.1; Westley, 2026-09-30: "not just unit values but their type as well") |
| C2 | The result follows the firing count | R\* rises and falls with the firing advantage (type-weighted), situation by situation: a position that lets three times as many units fire holds about three times the force. A rule set where some other number (a layer damage multiplier, health, raw range) decides the fight against the firing count fails. The unit-type matrix is not such a number; it is the fifth skill |
| C3 | Skills stack | a combination of plays gives a larger firing advantage, and a larger R\*, than each of its parts |
| C4 | No dominant skill | no single skill produces by itself the advantage the full set does |
| C5 | No dead skill | removing any one skill from the full set lowers the firing advantage and R\* noticeably |
| C6 | Skilled plays have answers (hub principle 5) | on most maps the counter-play takes the firing advantage back; refusing the fight counts; a sprung trap or a full surround may have none |
| C7 | The ceiling is high | in the best situations, with every skill used, the skilled side beats a much larger force (about three times its number is a rough size, not a pass mark) |
| C8 | Robust | C1 to C6 hold in a large majority of sampled situations (draft: 80 percent) |
| C9 | Fights stay readable | fight length stays inside E-11 S-085's window while knobs move |

Rough sizes for orientation, not pass marks: a single skill in an ordinary situation is worth about 30 percent more army; the full set three times or more. Draft numbers still to confirm: the 80 percent in C8, and the winning margin (a few percent of starting strength). Still open: how refusing the fight is checked in a lab where the larger side must attack; and whether abilities ever join the bar.

### 4.4 Maps

Westley, 2026-10-01, verbatim: "Q3: C, space doesn't have ramps but it does have very free layers that can be taken advantage of." The test set has two parts, reported separately. Space maps (the Settled Reach: every unit flies, no ramps) are the ones a rule set must pass: open space, obstacles to go around or over, lanes between obstacles, cover fields, and above all the five free layers, which are the terrain of a space fight. Land maps (ramps, walking units, ground that exists only on some layers) are measured and reported but do not block.

## 6. What the data must show ("lines crossing", made concrete)

"Lines crossing" was Westley's first name for the thing to find. Stated as a win probability crossing 0.5 it does not fit a system with no dice, and it never said what a good result looks like. In the terms of §4:

- A **line** is the edge, in the space of rule knobs, of where one condition of §4.3 holds: on one side skill wins the firing count and the result follows it, on the other it does not.
- The **crossing** is the region where every condition C0 to C9 holds at once.
- **Depth** is that region being non-empty and not a knife-edge: a small change to one knob should not throw a rule set out.

What the lab delivers from its data: (1) the rule sets that pass; (2) for each knob, which skills' firing advantage and break-even ratio it moves and by how much; (3) the rule sets that nearly pass and the condition each misses; (4) for every rule set, the plot of result against firing advantage, with the fights that fall off the line listed, since those are where a number other than position decided.

The first result is the **baseline profile**: the skill profile of today's default rules, under the base table and the three lever tables of §6.3. It says, before anything is tuned, whether each skilled play wins the firing count and whether results follow it. What the runs so far suggest (§8): the layer skill has no edge under vision alone; a concave is worth less than one unit; blindness is worth about one unit and a seer helps only above a sight threshold; for ten attackers against ten defenders the ramp goes from a win to a wipe-out between slope lengths 150 and 200, and the crossing moves with attacker number and defender distance.

Controls. Negative: identical forces and plays on a mirrored flat map give R\* = 1.0 within one unit; a detector that reads an advantage there is measuring the harness. Positive: a planted advantage of known direction (the unseen high side of pass 1) gives R\* well above 1.

### 6.1 The firing-share hypothesis (2026-09-30, first check in §8)

Westley, 2026-09-30, verbatim, on pricing each advantage as a fixed multiple of numbers:

> holding a choke depends on the choke breadth, the ratio of how many units are able to get shots on both teams. It's not as simple as a 1.5x. If team A is holding the choke (at advantage), and as 3x as many units that *can* shoot, then they can take on 3x the force as long as they stay at that choke. It's about the number of units that can get off shots (which depends on several factors, range, choke breadth, etc.)

> just having a height advantage doesn't mean much, it's the vision that comes with the layer difference that is the advantage. 4 should be folded into 5. But for vision as well, it's not as simple as just a 1.25x, just like the choke it's the ratio of units that can get shots off vs the ones that can't compared to the same numbers for the opposite team. This is leading us to the core of the game: the differentiator is **can your unit get shots off**.

> Maybe we should measure "units firing" as a part of the simulation, it's appearing that most of my responses are leading to the "units firing" response with the labels being somewhat dependent on that reality. Maybe we could have the simulation constantly compare the number of units firing for both sides, and use that to weight which side "should" have won, and if they didn't than there's a number that's off. We should test this theory.

Also from the same message, as scope for the lab:

- Height is not an advantage of its own; the vision that comes with the layer difference is. Height and vision are one advantage.
- Abilities are out of this calculation for now: "we're tuning base unit properties, abilities are another dimension that's too hard to map out at this time." Scenario X7 and pattern P3 wait.
- Catching the enemy mid-transition depends on army sizes ("the larger army can just walk up the ramp and kill the smaller army"); it is a tool for setting up traps, surrounds, and unit-type matchups, and is "much more effective when used in combination with" the unit-type counter. Unit type is measured, "not just unit values but their type as well."
- A hit from behind or the flank is "useless without a surround, vision advantage, or unit placement/count advantage."

**The hypothesis.** Who wins an engagement is decided by how many units on each side can get shots off, and for how long. Chokes, vision, surrounds, slopes, transitions and facing matter only through what they do to that count. Unit type is the one other factor: it weights each shot. If a side with the firing advantage loses, some other number has overridden position, and that number is off.

**Why it is plausible.** It is the Lanchester square law: with aimed fire, a side's strength goes as per-unit firepower times the square of the units firing. The first damage sweep (`toys/layerlab/results/log.md`, B's fire-up damage multiplier d against B's count, A fixed at 10) already fits it. The law predicts B breaks even at 10 / sqrt(d) units:

| B units | 12 | 14 | 16 | 18 | 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| d at the measured crossing | 0.747 | 0.550 | 0.444 | 0.350 | 0.250 |
| d the square law predicts (100 / B²) | 0.694 | 0.510 | 0.391 | 0.309 | 0.250 |

**The measurement the lab needs.** Per tick and per side, the number of living units that can fire: a target in range, seen, not blocked, the unit allowed to attack. The result today has total shots and unanswered-fire ticks per side, not this count. Add it to the result (per-tick series in the trace, and the sum over the fight).

**Two predictions, both ignorant of unit stats on purpose.** A prediction built from damage dealt against health always agrees with the outcome, because that is how the fight is computed; it tests nothing. So:

1. Count-only. For each side, firing fraction f = (sum over ticks of units that can fire) / (sum over ticks of units alive); predicted strength = f × (starting count)². The side with the larger strength is the predicted winner. This is a first form; the check below may show a better one.
2. Type-weighted. The same, with each unit that can fire counted as its category multiplier against its target's category (the Strike, Line, Siege matrix) instead of as 1.

Both are computed from the fight after it ends, so they diagnose a run; they do not forecast it.

**Reading a disagreement.**

| Count-only | Type-weighted | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| right | right | position decided it |
| wrong | right | unit type decided it; acceptable, that is the rock-paper-scissors |
| wrong | wrong | another number (a layer damage or range multiplier, health, damage per shot) overrode position: the number that is off |

**The check, on runs that already exist.** X0 (mirror), X2, X3, X4, and the damage sweep. Expected: both predictions match the winner in X2 and X3 (B never fires), are near a toss-up in X0 and X4, and the count-only prediction fails in the sweep's low-d cells, where B has more units firing and still loses because each shot is weak. If that is what comes out, the metric separates "position decided" from "a number decided" and the hypothesis has a working instrument. If the count-only prediction is wrong where no stat differs, the form of the prediction is wrong, not the game.

**What it changes if it holds.** Expected winners stop being hand labels per scenario: the principle generates the label, and Westley's hand labels become spot checks on it. The collisions he had begun labelling are paused until the check is in; his answers so far all turned on which side gets shots off and for how long. It also bears on which lever carries the layer advantage: under this principle the advantage comes from who can fire (vision, reach, terrain), not from a damage bonus for height. That last point is an inference from his words, not a ruling.

Design points for the build:

- R\* by scan, not bisection. Margin is not always monotonic in enemy count, and one fluke win at a high count would inflate a "largest force beaten". - Two force sizes per cell, since R\* at a choke depends on absolute numbers.

### Pass 2, 2026-10-01: the range lever, and a finding about target selection

**Superseded by pass 3 (same day).** The inversion described here, and the 98-to-2 size of the focus-fire effect, came mostly from units overlapping in the old movement, not from the nearest-target rule. With hard bodies the longer-range side wins (pass 3). Kept as the record of what the old build showed and how it was diagnosed.

Scenarios `x5_range_advantage.toml` (both sides attack-move) and `x5_range_advantage_hold.toml` (B holds). Same layers or not, the result was the same: **the side with the longer range loses.** Both attack-moving, 200 seeds: A shooting down at 1.1x range wins 11.5 percent (1.0x: 43.5; 1.3x: 9; 2.0x and up: 50). Same layer, no layer rule involved, A range 220 against B 200: A wins 10 percent. With B holding, A wins 200 of 200, and a static start with A in reach and B not has A winning 40 of 40, so the range rule itself is right.

Investigation (with evidence, not guesses): at equal total HP and equal units firing for the first 200 ticks after contact, A is ahead or level and still loses 88 percent. With no approach at all, the inversion survives when the fronts start 195 apart (A reaches many of B's units, B reaches few: A wins 19.5 percent) and vanishes at 150 apart (everyone reaches everyone: 47.5 against a 46.5 baseline). The candidate-set sizes at contact are 4.6 targets per A unit against 2.1 per B unit at 195, and 6.7 against 5.7 at 150. Changing only the target rule decides it: when A focuses the most damaged unit instead of the nearest, A wins 100 percent (and 98.5 percent when both sides focus: the longer range wins, as it should). At equal ranges, one side focusing and the other taking the nearest gives 98 to 2.

Reading: a side with a wide choice of nearest targets spreads its fire and kills slowly; a side with a narrow choice puts all of it on the same few units and kills them first. That is a real consequence of the stated rules (SPEC 5.4, nearest by default), not a coding fault, and it is not visible to the firing-share count, because both sides have the same number of units firing. Two consequences: (1) any lever result with an unequal reach is confounded by who concentrates fire; (2) focus fire is itself a very large lever (98 to 2), which fits the skill-ceiling aim and means "which target a unit picks" is a dimension of the map, not a detail. X2 to X4 and the damage and vision sweeps used equal ranges and are not affected.

Transfer class T1 for the direction (concentration beats spread), T2 for every number.

Simple arithmetic for the ideal case (attackers held to c firing and refilled from behind, D defenders all firing): the defenders beat about D(D+1)/(2c), which is 34 for 16 defenders against four abreast. Nothing here came near it. Why:

1. **The lane's walls block movement but not sight**, so attackers shoot defenders through the rock. E-01 S-007 has obstacles blocking sight by height; the lab's presets do not. Worked around in these scripts with cover discs along the walls.
2. **A unit ordered to hold never steps forward, and a unit allowed to chase runs to the enemy wherever it is.** Attackers stop at full reach from the nearest defender, which is the tip of any arc; the rest of the arc is out of reach and stays there. Defenders allowed to chase ran through the corridor and gave up the choke. There is no stance between the two: step up to engage, but stay near the post.
3. **Reach is four unit-widths.** With reach 200 and spacing 50, only a few units can stand within reach of one spot. That limits a choke and a concave alike; at best 7 of 16 defenders fired. Reach against body size is a knob the lab has not swept.

A held mouth also produced stand-offs (attackers stop in the corridor, nobody in reach), which is the refusal Westley counts as a counter.
