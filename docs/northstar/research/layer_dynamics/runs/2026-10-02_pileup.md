# 2026-10-02: the pile-up (A154), where it comes from and who has it

Instrument: worktree `Pileup` (branch `push/pp-pileup` = `push/pp-a-land2`, new movement ON, `OM_TP_PURSUE_BY_STEERING` = 1), the production C library and the Python reference in `simulation/`. Scratch copies of the C (iteration cap, arrival radius, pair-check rule) and scripts live outside the repo; nothing in `csrc/`, `tests/` or `scripts/skilllab/` was touched. Every C number is a fresh process, one case each.

The scene: `tests/csim/test_E13_S101_movement_that_reads.py`, 20 Raiders in a 4 x 5 block (35 wu apart) given one `om_command_group_move` to (1000, 70), 200 ticks. Rule R-101-01: no two units closer than 30 wu (Westley, A148: tolerance 29.9).

## Short answer

- It is neither a shared destination, nor the 3-iteration cap, nor the group slots. The 20 units each have their own slot (35 wu apart); raising the cap to 10 or 30 does not fix it.
- It is the collision pass's rule for who checks whom. A unit that has stopped (arrived) is only ever checked for overlap by a unit with a **lower id that is still moving**. A moving unit with a **higher id** that walks into a stopped lower-id unit is never pushed out. It walks straight through. The arrival radius (22.5 wu) is the trigger because it is what stops units early, off their slot, in each other's way.
- The Python does exactly this. The C copies it: with the map size set to the Python's (4000 x 4000) the two give the same position for all 20 units on all 200 ticks, to 1e-12.
- Letting every unit start its own pair checks removes it (worst pair goes from 4.98 to 27.4 wu in both). Adding more iterations on top gets 29.1 wu in both.

What happens (Python detour state read at each tick):

1. Unit 14 first sidesteps east (a "detour": when a neighbour blocks the 40 wu probe ahead, the unit picks a free angle and keeps it for 60 wu without looking again).
2. Tick 134: unit 13 (next to its slot, about to stop) blocks the straight line down to slot, so unit 14 picks a new detour, heading west-north-west `(-0.96, -0.30)`, for 60 wu (8 ticks, 134 to 141).
3. Tick 136-137: unit 9, which has been walking east, comes within 22.5 wu of its slot and **arrives**: it stops at (973.6, 158.7), 20.7 wu off its slot. It is now stopped and directly in unit 14's committed detour line.
4. Ticks 137-141: unit 14 walks the detour straight through unit 9 (7.2 wu per tick, 30 down to 4.98, out the far side at x 967.5). Nothing pushes it, because of the pair-check rule below.
5. Tick 142: detour over, unit 14 turns back to its slot and walks through unit 9 a second time, clear at tick 146.

```
  y
  ^                     u13 stopped (1008.9,125.1)
  |      u9 STOPPED       .
  |      (973.6,158.7)
  |        O<----- u14 walks west through it, tick 137..141 (detour, 60 wu)
  |           O     u14 at 996 -> 967 -> and back east at 142..146
  +----------------------------------------> x    slot u14 = (1017.5,140)
```

Why it is not pushed out (Python `_resolve_collisions`, C `om_move_collision_once`/`om_move_collide_pair`): only a unit that moved this tick starts pair checks, and a unit only checks partners with a **higher** id. Unit 9 did not move (stopped) and has the lower id, so it never starts the pair; unit 14 moved but only looks at ids above 14. The pair (9, 14) is checked by nobody. This is the same in the C (`ms->moved[]`, partners "above" the unit).

Without the canvas (the real test scene, no canvas set in C) the C baseline is the same 4.978 @140 (9,14); the C with `OM_TP_PURSUE_BY_STEERING` = 0 on the same scene never gets under 35.0 (units are deflected differently and stop later).

So of the four causes asked about: shared destination, no (a different, smaller problem); arrival, yes as the trigger (it stops units off their slot, in each other's way); 3-iteration cap, no; group slots, only in that 35 wu slots are closer than 2 x 22.5 wu of arrival tolerance (45 wu), so two units each stopping short of its own slot can land on top of the next; spreading slots to 70 wu removes it. The decisive defect is the pair-check rule.

## 2. Does the Python's own test suite show it?

Only two scripts in `simulation/` move many units and could see it. Neither asserts separation (the `verify_separation` call prints OK/WARN and never fails).

| `test_checkpoint_6b.py` `test_group_move()` | 10 mixed units, group move, 100 ticks | 33.2 @83 (1,2) | separation fine; its own "Overall" prints FAIL for cohesion/speed (max spread 550 wu), unrelated to separation |
| `test_formation_baseline.py` `run()` | 10 Raiders/Interceptors plain move to one point, 300 ticks (the 10 idle line units start overlapped by their spawn jitter, ignored) | 30.0 @164 (7,9) | OK (shared destination, units arrive late and one at a time) |

No other Python test (`test_magic_box`, `test_obstacle_navigation`, `test_checkpoint_6f`, `test_checkpoint_formation_perf`) checks separation. The pile-up is therefore **not shown by the Python's own tests**; it needs units to arrive (stop) while others are still walking in from a higher id, which none of these scenes does.

## For Westley

The C is a faithful copy of a Python defect, which is why the pile-up was "matched". The fix is one rule in the collision pass (each unit starts its own pair checks, or the check is symmetric), in both. It is not the iteration count and not the arrival radius, though more iterations shave the last 2 wu. Whether to fix it, and whether the Python stays the reference where it breaks R-101-01 (A148 already widened to 29.9 for a 29.92 residual), is yours to decide.
