# Territory / influence visuals around many points: survey for the galaxy view

Date 2026-10-02. Research only. Context: `docs/northstar/plans/2026-10-02-galaxy-perf-audit.md` (brute-force raymarch, 15 to 60x over a 2 ms budget on an RTX 3060; up to 400 systems, 15 factions, orbit camera that can sit inside the bubbles; territory changes rarely).

Labels: PRIMARY = developer's own words (dev blog, forum post by the dev, paper by the authors, vendor article co-written with the studio). THIRD-PARTY = player, modder, tutorial author, forum commenter. REASONING = my own inference, not sourced; treat as a hypothesis to test. Search was a web search plus page fetches; several pages (Wikipedia-class summaries, search snippets) were thin, and I say so where it matters.

## Headline finding

Nobody I could find ships true 3D metaball territory bubbles with merged surfaces on a busy map. The relevant evidence is:
1. Every shipped game I could verify draws borders/ranges as a 2D field (distance-field texture, or a flat area on a map plane), baked or incrementally updated when ownership changes, and sampled cheaply per pixel. Paradox (Imperator) and Supreme Commander are the two well-documented cases.
2. The real 3D prior art for "Gaussian blobs from many points, merged surface" is molecular visualisation (VMD QuickSurf): density on a grid, then marching cubes, recomputed only when atoms move. That is the same shape as audit recommendations 1 and 2.
3. Elite Dangerous, Sword of the Stars, Homeworld show translucent 3D spheres or per-system discs; I found nothing public on how they merge or draw them.

## 2. Ground strategy games and the 2D cousins

Paradox, Imperator: Rome (the best-documented border pipeline). PRIMARY (Intel developer article about the studio's own optimisation work): https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/optimized-gradient-border-rendering-in-imperator-rome.html
- Borders are an unsigned distance field generated on the GPU with the Jump Flooding Algorithm. Steps: a pre-pass mask for coastal borders; an init step that detects edges and downsamples by 4, storing x/y offsets; five JFA passes at offsets 16, 8, 4, 2, 1; a final pass converting offset vectors to normalised distance. Format R8G8_UNORM during JFA. The lookup texture is 8192 x 4096; the final field is smaller.
- The map shader then only samples one distance value per pixel to get a gradient around borders.
- Cost on change: originally the whole field was regenerated when any of three colour values changed, giving a GPU spike almost every in-game day. They changed it to redraw only quads covering the changed provinces, which cut the spikes to roughly one per month. The remaining bottleneck named was many samples from a very large texture.
- Lesson for us: territory changes rarely, so generation cost matters little; keep the per-frame lookup to one fetch; update only the dirty region.

Supreme Commander and Planetary Annihilation range rings. THIRD-PARTY (an engineer, "bgolus", who says he worked on similar tech, in a Unity forum thread; not a studio publication, treat as credible but unverified): "The way it was done in Supreme Commander was 2d distance fields, one texture for each range, at a relatively low resolution. You render circles with a consistent falloff with a max blend mode." For Planetary Annihilation: done in screen space, projecting rings onto the depth map as a full-screen post process rather than re-rendering geometry. https://discussions.unity.com/t/rts-range-rings-on-map/610499
- Max blend mode is the union trick: the union of circles equals the max of their per-pixel fields, so overlaps merge for free; the outline comes from thresholding the field.

Factorio turret ranges (merged range circles, PRIMARY: developer posila in the Friday Facts #281 comments, https://forums.factorio.com/viewtopic.php?start=40&t=64806 and https://www.factorio.com/blog/post/fff-281). Ranges draw as a single solid shape. First idea: stencil so each pixel is written once; not fast enough. Second: pass the turret list to a pixel shader that tests each pixel against all turrets; fast when the whole screen is in range, very slow for pixels outside any range (iterates the whole list). Final: a pre-pass drawing geometry into a smaller buffer, stencil-marking only the uncertain (edge) pixels, and running the exact per-turret shader only on those. This is the same failure our shader has (loop over all points per pixel, worst on pixels that miss) and the same cure (cheap coarse pass, exact work on a thin band).

Sanctuary: Shattered Sun (PRIMARY, small studio dev blog), https://www.sanctuaryshatteredsun.com/post/range-rings-to-rule-them-all : rings as one composite area per range type, circles as distance-field cones (cone height equals radius so the depth buffer does the union via depth test), Unity DrawMeshInstanced plus a custom pass. Reports 22 thousand circles on the largest map with no noticeable performance drop. This is a neat depth-buffer union: instanced cones in one draw, depth test keeps the nearest, so overlaps merge without a loop. It is 2D-ground-plane; in 3D the cone becomes a cone-of-revolution volume and the union is a depth-only trick, relevant to section 4.

Civilization V/VI, Total War, Northgard, Anno, Cities: Skylines, StarCraft II. I found no developer technique source for any of them. Civ V border discussions are THIRD-PARTY Unity/shader forum threads (marching-squares mesh plus outline shader, or a tile sheet, or SDF-drawn lines on a mesh); one blog covers SDF border lines on a strategy map: https://newarteest.wordpress.com/2019/11/30/border-lines-on-a-strategy-map/ . Total War: only an AMD GPUOpen terrain article (not borders). Say plainly: nothing reliable found for these.

## 3. Other industries

VMD QuickSurf (molecular surfaces). PRIMARY: Krone, Stone, Ertl, Schulten, "Fast Visualization of Gaussian Density Surfaces for Molecular Dynamics and Particle System Trajectories", EuroVis Short Papers 2012 (page 403 to my fetcher; abstract and Stone's slides read instead). Slides: https://www.ks.uiuc.edu/Research/vmd/publications/acs2013_stone.pdf
- Algorithm as stated in the slides: build a spatial acceleration grid with cells sized to the cutoff radius of the exponential kernel; compute a 3D density map where each lattice point sums contributions from particles in the 3x3x3 neighbouring cells; compute a volumetric colour map by summing particle colours weighted by their density contribution; extract an isosurface at a user isovalue with GPU marching cubes. Linear in particle count, "scales to millions of particles".
- The colour-weighted-by-density step is exactly our "which faction owns this voxel" channel.
- Timings: I could not find the numbers. The abstract and slides give "interactive frame rates for molecular dynamics simulations up to about a million atoms" and a 1.5x to 2x speedup plus halved GPU memory from a later memory optimisation, but no ms-per-frame table was retrievable. Do not quote a figure from this report.

Screen-space fluid rendering (van der Laan, Green, Sainz, I3D 2009). PRIMARY: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220792184_Screen_space_fluid_rendering_with_curvature_flow . Render particles as spheres to a depth buffer, smooth the depth (curvature flow), separately accumulate a thickness buffer for absorption, blur, shade. Real time, quality/speed tunable by blur size. It is the closest published cousin of "sphere impostors, blur, threshold".

Force fields and soft intersections. THIRD-PARTY tutorials: Cyanilux Forcefield Shader Breakdown (fresnel rim, scene depth difference for an intersection glow, single-sided rendering; notes that fancier distortion versions cannot easily draw both faces): https://www.cyanilux.com/tutorials/forcefield-shader-breakdown/ ; Wolfire soft particles: http://blog.wolfire.com/2010/04/Soft-Particles ; Game Developer, Softening polygon intersections in Blightbound: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/softening-polygon-intersections-in-blightbound . Gives a glow where a transparent shape meets scene depth; per-shape cost, but it needs the depth texture, which Godot Forward+ exposes.

Volume rendering in Godot. THIRD-PARTY: Godot docs recommend low 3D texture sizes (64^3 or lower) for fog volumes and say cost is dominated by screen coverage rather than texture size; an Asset Library volumetric demo and a Medium tutorial show 3D-texture raymarching in Godot 4. https://docs.godotengine.org/en/latest/tutorials/3d/volumetric_fog.html
Fog-of-war textures: the standard approach is a 2D low-res texture sampled by a shader (not sourced here; no specific reference gathered).

## 4. Cheap tricks for a merged-bubbles impression

All entries in this section are REASONING plus the sources named; I did not find a shipped game that documents using them for 3D territory.

a) Depth-tested sphere impostors, soft intersections, alpha or additive. Each system draws a sphere or billboard; where two spheres of the same faction overlap, additive makes the overlap brighter, which reads as "two bubbles" not one merged region. Look: overlapping balloons rather than a merged blob. Camera inside: works if back faces render (otherwise the near plane clips the sphere and the effect pops). Cost: 400 spheres of large screen area is overdraw-bound; roughly proportional to total covered pixels times overlap count. Merging: none, unless a depth-prepass picks one layer.

b) Stencil/depth union with one outline. Draw all of a faction's spheres to a mask (stencil or a depth-only layer), then draw one flat tint where the mask is set, and an outline from an edge detect on the mask. Merges perfectly in screen space for the silhouette but not the surface (no real intersection shape between spheres, just a flat region). Factorio found stencil alone too slow for many circles in a pass-per-pixel-write sense and combined it with a coarse pre-pass. Camera inside: near-plane clipping of the spheres leaves holes unless back faces or a full-screen quad is used; flat fill looks like fog, not a surface.

c) Thickness accumulation then threshold (screen-space metaballs). Render each sphere's front faces additive-negative and back faces additive-positive (or analytic chord length in a fragment shader) into a half-float buffer so the sum is path length through spheres; or render a smooth radial falloff per sphere additively (a splat) and threshold the sum, which is the classic 2D metaball blur-and-threshold in screen space. Per faction one channel: 15 factions need 4 RGBA targets (MRT) or a pass per faction at reduced resolution. Look: soft merged blobs with smooth necks, no true 3D surface normals unless you derive them from the buffer gradient (fluid-rendering style), contour is consistent. Camera inside: the front-face half is clipped, so thickness is underestimated near the camera; the analytic chord version handles it (clamp the ray start to the near plane). Cost: overdraw of 400 large splats at half resolution (cheap if the splat is a fixed screen-space quad per system), plus one blur and one composite. Scales with total covered pixels, not with systems times pixels. Closest published relative: van der Laan et al.

d) Rim-only fresnel spheres whose interiors cancel. Each sphere shows only edges; interiors transparent. Looks like glass bubbles with intersecting rings; the contour of a union is not drawn (each sphere's own rim remains inside the union). Cheapest but does not read as merged territory. Camera inside: rim effect inverts and needs two-sided handling (Cyanilux notes this).

e) Baked volume (audit rec 1) and baked mesh (audit rec 2). Not "cheap tricks" but the documented prior art is QuickSurf (density grid, colour-weighted ownership, marching cubes) and Imperator-style baked fields (distance field texture, dirty-region rebake). Camera inside: a volume raymarch handles it natively (start the ray at the camera); a mesh handles it if back faces render and sorting is handled (one layer via depth prepass).

## 6. Shortlist (REASONING, not a decision)

1. Baked 3D field, one-pass raymarch of a single volume (audit rec 1). Best evidence trail: the density-grid-plus-colour-weighting design is literally QuickSurf's front half, Imperator shows that rebaking only the dirty region makes change cost negligible when changes are rare, and Godot docs back small 3D textures with cost dominated by screen coverage. It keeps today's look, handles the camera inside natively, and removes both the per-point loop and the 15-fold box overdraw. Residual risk: the raymarch fragment cost over the whole covered screen at 1080p; mitigate with a half-resolution viewport or fewer steps; this must be measured.
2. Baked iso-surface mesh from the same grid (audit rec 2). QuickSurf is the proof that marching cubes on a Gaussian density grid is fast and interactive at scales far above 400 points. It fits a low-poly direction and drops the raymarch entirely. Risks are the faceted look versus smooth metaballs (a design call), transparency ordering for stacked or nested faction surfaces and a camera inside (needs back faces and probably a depth prepass), and the seam glow, which has to come from a per-vertex baked border value or a depth-intersection pass (forcefield technique).
3. Screen-space thickness/splat accumulation at reduced resolution. Choose only if the bake route is rejected or the visuals need a soft volumetric look; its cost is independent of point count after splatting, but it has the least precedent for territory, the camera-inside handling is awkward, and 15 owners need multiple render targets. Per-pixel candidate culling (audit rec 4) is the lower-risk fallback to keep in the pocket.

Techniques 1 and 2 share the bake, so the bake can be built once and both rendering paths prototyped against it.
