- **Buy-once base game.** Owning the base game grants the **starting (era 1) civilization
  of every branch** in the branch system.
- **Paid Steam DLC expansions.** Additional eras within each branch ship as one-time-purchase

## Expansion Packs and Monetization

This section defines how the game makes money. The era-agnostic architecture (Sacred Principle #3) is not just an engineering nicety — it is the commercial engine of the entire project. The game is a **skeleton**. Expansion packs are the **skin**. The simulation, mission generator, event chains, combat system, and game loop are era-neutral machinery. A theme pack plugs into that machinery and reskins every surface the player sees: unit names, art, prose, music, chronicle templates, map aesthetics, cultural flavor. The skeleton ships once. The skins ship forever.

### Time Period Expansion Packs

Each expansion pack is a complete time period that the player can select at the start of a new game or cycle. The base game ships with the **sci-fi theme** — Asimov-inspired, set 10,000+ years in the future. This is the right starting era for both design (Foundation inspiration is the core thematic identity) and marketing (the target audience of strategy gamers on Steam over-indexes on sci-fi; Stellaris, Endless Space, FTL, Homeworld are the competitive neighborhood where an indie can differentiate). Sci-fi tech tiers are also easier to design than historical eras because there's no accuracy constraint — the progression is invented and baked into the era at design time. Expansion packs add additional eras — each with its own AI-generated library content (naming conventions, chronicle templates, mission briefing prose, unit flavor, visual style, tech tier definitions). Historical eras (Roman, WW2, Medieval, etc.) are expansion pack content.

**Example time periods** (illustrative, not exhaustive): Medieval, Caveman, Roman, Greek, Imperial China, Ark-style dinosaur world, WW2, Age of Sail, Aztec, Viking, Feudal Japan, Cyberpunk, Post-Apocalyptic — and many more. The era-agnostic engine means any historical or fictional setting that maps onto the simulation's abstractions (regions, military strength, economic output, factional tension, population, missions, faction agents) is a valid expansion pack. The list of possible eras is effectively unbounded.

### Isolated Time Period Chapters

Some expansion packs may feature an **isolated time period** — a chapter arc that takes place entirely within a single era (e.g., an expansion where every chapter is Roman, or every chapter is far-future). When a game uses an isolated time period, chapters are shorter than the base game's multi-era generational arcs. The simulation teleports forward when it reaches a configured threshold (a stability collapse, an extinction-level event, or an era-specific trigger), or a major event causes the cycle to restart from the beginning. The specific mechanics for time period isolation — threshold triggers, chapter length scaling, restart conditions — are to be determined during design of the first non-sci-fi expansion.

### Rotational AI-Generated Expansion (Post-Launch)

A single rotating expansion slot where AI generates an entire themed content pack on a weekly cadence. These packs draw inspiration from popular fiction universes and genre tropes — the spirit of Star Wars, Terminator, Alien, Dune, and similar — translated through the game's era-agnostic engine into playable theme packs. This is not a near-term feature. **This is a "after we finish the game" idea.** It is listed here so the architecture accounts for it — the library pipeline and theme pack interface should not make this impossible — but no design or implementation work should target it until the base game and first paid expansions ship.

### Monetization Model

**Free version:** The player gets the **Roman era** as the permanent free tier. The Roman era is a complete, genuine game experience — full simulation, all mission archetypes, the complete unit roster (all units restricted to ground-layer combat per the era's unit-type layer restrictions). The Roman era demonstrates the simulation depth and generational storytelling but does not showcase the 2.5D vertical layer system, which is a premium feature visible only in multi-layer eras.

> **RE-OPEN NOTE (v3.00 proposed):** Roman's role is being redesigned. Under v3.00 Roman is still the free tier but is **deliberately scoped down** (reduced faction count, reduced mission variety, no progression systems) to differentiate from the paid experience. Separately, Roman is also an **on-track era for paid players** and is no longer a tack-on — paid players can earn Roman mastery and advance tier progression in Roman on equal footing with other work eras. See "Progression Track Redesign (v3.00 Proposed)" → component 4 below.

**New player trial period.** On first download, the player receives a **trial key** granting access to both the **1980s era** (3–5 missions) and the **Settled Reach sci-fi era** (3–5 missions). The trial missions are fully featured — same simulation, same AI, same 2.5D layer combat — not a degraded preview. The trial exists to demonstrate the layer system and premium era quality. The 1980s era (submarines on layers 4–5, surface ships/land on layer 3, aircraft on layers 1–2) is the clearest possible showcase of why layers matter. The Settled Reach era shows the full sci-fi experience. After the trial missions are consumed, both eras lock and the player returns to the free Roman tier unless they subscribe or purchase era packs.

> **RE-OPEN NOTE (v3.00 proposed):** The trial period mechanism is being replaced by a **multi-stage tutorial** (approximately 15 minutes, sub-objectives across 3 hallmark eras including Settled Reach and 1980s) plus a permanent **inspection mode** for all eras. The trial counter mechanic goes away; the tutorial is skippable and replayable once per hallmark era. Conversion pressure comes from the emotional sequence of *tasted the best → returned to limited Roman → can only look at the eras they tasted → wants them back*, which is stronger than a mission-count trial. See "Progression Track Redesign (v3.00 Proposed)" → components 8 and 9 below.

**Trial expiration.** The trial counter is per-mission, not time-based. Starting a mission in a trial era decrements the counter. If the player is mid-mission when the counter reaches zero, they complete that mission — no mid-game lockout. The lock activates at the next mission selection screen.

**Subscription ($3–5/month):** Full access to the complete game including the base sci-fi theme (Settled Reach) and all available time period expansion packs. Cancel anytime. This is the primary revenue model. Marketing material on the Steam page highlights subscription content — the rotating library, the expanding era catalog, the living game that grows over time. The subscription funds ongoing AI content generation and new expansion development.

> **RE-OPEN NOTE (v3.00 proposed):** The subscription no longer grants flat access to the full catalog. It grants immediate access to a **5-era starter bundle** plus the ability to grind progression toward non-starter eras on the progression track. Starter bundle selection and grind pace tuning are open. See "Progression Track Redesign (v3.00 Proposed)" → components 5 and 6 below.

**Cancellation behavior.** When a subscription lapses, the player loses access to all eras except the free Roman tier and any eras they have **purchased outright** as one-time buys. Eras accessed only through the subscription (including eras with grind progress — see "Era Mastery Grind" below) become unavailable until the player resubscribes or purchases them individually. Grind progress toward era mastery is preserved on the server — resubscribing restores the player's progress exactly where they left off. The subscription is the access layer. One-time purchases are the ownership layer. These are cleanly separated: subscribing grants temporary access to everything, purchasing grants permanent access to specific eras.

> **RE-OPEN NOTE (v3.00 RESOLVED Session 4):** The cancellation behavior is restructured under the **conditional ownership model**. Eras in the player's library fall into three categories: **Purchased** (permanent playable ownership regardless of subscription), **Earned** (eras grinded past the mastery retention threshold while subscribed — permanent playable ownership, survives cancellation), and **Rented** (the 5-era starter bundle — revoked on cancellation, mastery progress preserved server-side for resubscription). See "Progression Track Redesign (v3.00)" → component 3 below.

**Expansion packs (one-time purchase):** Individual time period packs sold as standalone purchases for players who want a specific era without subscribing. Purchased eras are permanent additions to the player's library regardless of subscription status. This is the secondary revenue channel.

> **RE-OPEN NOTE (v3.00 RESOLVED Session 4):** One-time purchases remain permanent playable ownership regardless of subscription state — confirmed under the conditional ownership model. Purchased eras are in the "Purchased" category and are always playable. See "Progression Track Redesign (v3.00)" → component 3 below.

> **Governing principle:** The era catalog is a **progression track**, not a flat library. The game sells *aspiration plus access*, not just access. The player's relationship to the catalog is organized around working toward specific desirable eras over months of play, with hallmark eras sprinkled throughout the track as visible payoffs rather than a single distant endpoint.

**Core components of the redesign:**

**1. Branching progression structure with multi-peak curves per branch (rewritten Session 6, supersedes Session 3 flat-track model).** The era catalog is organized as a **branching tech tree**, not a single linear progression. At launch there are **2 branches × 3 eras each + Roman as a standalone free-tier era outside the branch structure = 7 launch eras total**. Each branch extends to 5+ eras post-launch, and additional branches are added as post-launch expansions (Branch 3 — ancient history — is the first planned post-launch branch).

**Launch branch catalog (Session 6, work eras resolved April 22, 2026):**

- **Branch 1 — Sci-fi / Future.** 3 eras at launch. **Settled Reach** is the Branch 1 hallmark at position 3. Branch 1 is the high-tempo, high-awareness, rapid-recovery branch (see "Branch System" subsection below for the full mechanical specification). Work eras: **Planetary Colonization** (position 1, entry) — surface warfare on colonized worlds and stations (Mars, orbital stations, asteroid belt, Earth-orbit facilities), single-layer, ground-bound engagement, rare environmental events, squad/taskforce scale (20–60 units, Branch 1 DNA). **Interplanetary** (position 2, bridge) — solar-system-spanning combat across all planets, partial layer access (atmosphere-capable vs. space-only ships), asymmetric engagement matrix (orbital vs. surface), occasional environmental events (solar flares, debris fields), squad/taskforce scale (20–60 units, Branch 1 DNA). Scope ramp: stations/colonies → solar system → galaxy.
- **Branch 2 — Modern / Recent history.** 3 eras at launch. **The 1980s** is the Branch 2 hallmark at position 3. Branch 2 is the medium-tempo, partial-sensor, industrial-replacement branch. Work eras: **Colonial / Independence Era** (position 1, entry) — AoE3-register large-formation ground combat (~1750–1820), line infantry, cavalry, artillery, single-layer with terrain-elevation, ground-bound engagement, rare environmental events, large force scale (company-to-legion range, 200–600 units — deliberately above Branch 2's company/battlegroup default to deliver the AoE3 massed-battle feel of ranked formations clashing). **World War II** (position 2, bridge) — ground-dominant combined arms (~1939–1945), partial layer access (paratroopers, amphibious, close air support), mostly-reciprocal engagement with asymmetric air-to-ground exceptions, occasional environmental events, company/battlegroup scale (100–300 units, Branch 2 DNA). Chronological ramp: 1750s → 1940s → 1980s.
- **Roman (standalone free tier).** Outside the branching structure. Permanent free-to-play onboarding experience. Zero-position relative to all branches — not part of Branch 1, not part of Branch 2, and not a placeholder for a future Branch 3 ancient-history slot. Roman's mechanical signature sits closest to the future Branch 3 (slow tempo, heavy fog, slow reinforcement) but is tuned independently as the onboarding experience.
- **Branch 3 — Ancient history (post-launch).** Deferred to first post-launch expansion. Populated with non-Roman ancient civilizations.

**Multi-peak curve within each branch.** Each branch's internal ordering is linear (no sub-splits — `Q-mon8.4` resolution) and contains one hallmark plus two work eras at launch. Hallmarks are premium, high-production, aspirational. Work eras are fully realized quality content with lower production ceiling. The player sees the full branch upfront on branch entry (WoWS preview pattern — inspection available on any branch era regardless of current progress; commitment driver).

**Hallmark placement: position 3 (end of track). RESOLVED April 22, 2026.** The hallmark sits at the end of each branch's linear track: Work Era 1 → Work Era 2 → Hallmark. The hallmark is the aspirational apex the player works toward. This is the classic tech-tree payoff structure (WoWS Tier X, Fortnite battle pass final tier). The tutorial provides an early taste of the hallmark; the track delivers the earned payoff. Work eras are not filler — they are mechanically distinct, strategically deep experiences in their own right.

**Graduated mechanical ramp within each branch. RESOLVED April 22, 2026.** The two work eras per branch take *graduated* positions on the hallmark-exclusive axes (see `era_mechanical_axes.md` for the full axis framework). Position 1 (entry) is most constrained on hallmark-exclusive axes. Position 2 (bridge) is partially open. Position 3 (hallmark) is fully open. Each step adds mechanical freedom — the player's journey through a branch is a deliberate escalation of the branch's unique identity, not a lateral move between interchangeable eras. This interacts with the access model: position 1 is subscription-gated, position 2 is grindable to permanent ownership, position 3 is premium-purchased.

**Launch hallmark count: 2 (Settled Reach, 1980s).** Supersedes the v3.00 Session 3 "3–4 hallmark eras for 20-era launch catalog" working number — the 2-branch launch structure yields exactly 2 launch hallmarks. Post-launch hallmark cadence: each new branch ships with its own hallmark, and each existing branch gains additional hallmarks as it extends to 5 eras. Reference models for per-branch progression cadence: Destiny 2 subclass element mastery, World of Warships national tech tree lines, Stellaris ascension paths. Across all of these the pattern is consistent: **hallmarks are the thing the player remembers, work eras are the thing the player does**. Both are necessary; they serve different purposes.

**Branch mechanical identity.** Each branch has a distinct mechanical signature across three axes (tempo, information warfare, attrition/economy). See the "Branch System" subsection below for the full specification. Branch identity is not cosmetic — playing Branch 1 feels mechanically different from playing Branch 2 in ways the player notices in the first mission. This is the "my branch vs. yours" retention identity that Session 5 research surfaced as critical to successful branching systems (WoWS, WoT, Stellaris, Destiny, PoE). Branch identity is expressed entirely through per-era config parameters consumed by an era-agnostic engine — compatible with Sacred Principle #3.

**Hallmark selection for the 2 launch branches is RESOLVED** as Settled Reach (Branch 1) and 1980s (Branch 2), because these are the two eras with captured visual identity, captured material registers, and the bible's flagship 2.5D layer system showcase status. **Work era selection is RESOLVED (April 22, 2026):** Branch 1 work eras are Planetary Colonization (position 1) and Interplanetary (position 2). Branch 2 work eras are Colonial / Independence Era (position 1) and World War II (position 2). See launch branch catalog above for full descriptions. Branch 3 hallmark selection is a post-launch question.

**2. Production budget is deliberately unequal across the catalog.** Hallmark eras receive 1.5–2× the production budget of work eras: the most distinctive Q2e frame variant material, more elaborate unit art, unusual mission archetypes, possibly era-specific mechanics that don't exist elsewhere, the most striking visual identity in the card template. Work eras are real content and are not filler, but their production ceiling is lower and their role is to maintain engagement between hallmarks. This is a capex allocation principle — evenly distributing production budget across all 20 eras would produce 20 mediocre eras instead of a mix the player actually values.

**3. Conditional ownership model (RESOLVED, Session 4; updated April 16, 2026).** The relationship between paid channels and playable eras follows a **conditional ownership** model with three categories of eras in the player's library:

- **Purchased eras** — One-time buy on the external website. Permanent playable ownership regardless of subscription state. Includes hallmark eras and any era tagged as premium in the catalog.
- **Earned (retained) eras** — Eras the player grinded past the retention threshold (see below) while subscribed. Permanent playable ownership. Survives subscription cancellation.
- **Entry eras** — Era 1 in each branch. Accessible only while subscribed. Revoked on cancellation (library flag cleared, era returns to inspection-only). Mastery progress is preserved server-side and resumes on resubscription.

> **Update note (April 16, 2026):** The "Rented eras" category (previously the 5-era starter bundle) is replaced by "Entry eras" (Era 1 in each branch, subscription-gated). The starter bundle concept is retired — subscribing grants entry-era access in all branches, not a fixed bundle. See component 5 for the full resolution.

**The retention threshold.** An era is retained permanently when the player accumulates enough mastery currency in that era — the same currency that drives the SC2-border mastery UI elaboration system (component 10). Target: approximately **15 hours of play at baseline**, with small bonuses from in-mission performance (unit retention, objectives completed). The exact currency formula is a tuning-stage decision deferred to implementation; the 15-hour figure is the design-level target the tuning must hit. Reference model: Apex Legends character unlock currency — passive accumulation through play, with performance nudging the rate.

**Entry eras are eligible for retention.** A subscriber who grinds an entry era past the 15-hour threshold retains that era permanently on cancellation — the same rule as any earned era. The retention mechanic applies uniformly to any era the player puts real time into, regardless of how they initially accessed it.

**Subscription price: $2/month.** Deliberately low — below the psychological threshold where strategy players organize outrage (Paradox's $4.99/month DLC subscriptions triggered hostile forum reaction; $2 sits in "coffee-money" territory where the subscription is emotionally treated as a tip rather than a recurring bill). The $2 figure is a working target for launch, not locked permanently, and may be revisited post-launch based on actual retention and conversion data.

**Business logic.** The model solves three problems simultaneously: (a) Paradox-style "own nothing, pay forever" hostility is defused because the player always has a path to keep content on the way out — grind it, retain it, cancel, play it forever; (b) the "subscribe and dump" extraction case is blocked because the starter bundle is explicitly rented and revocable; (c) subscription retention is still strong because the progression track, grind-earning path, and UI elaboration rewards all require an active subscription to engage with, not just the rented starter content.

**Reference models:** This is a hybrid of WoW's tiered ownership model, Paradox's optional subscription, and Apex Legends' earned-currency unlock pattern. It is not a direct copy of any of them.

**Design constraint on clarity.** Every purchase surface — Steam page, website checkout, in-game store, subscription signup, subscription cancellation flow — must state with absolute clarity which eras in the player's library are **Purchased**, **Earned**, or **Rented**, and what happens to each category on cancellation. The failure mode is a player canceling and being surprised by which eras disappear. The fix is aggressive labeling and a cancellation confirmation screen that shows exactly what will be lost before the player confirms.

**Steam exposure note.** Because all paid transactions flow through the external website rather than Steam, the Steam DLC refund window (14 days / 2 hours) does not apply to this model. There is no Steam DLC purchase to refund. Steam reviews remain a risk surface but the automatic refund-cascade failure mode does not exist. This is a meaningful constraint relaxation compared to a hypothetical Steam-transacted version of the same model.

Under v3.00, Roman is **also an on-track era for paid players** — it is not a second-class era and its on-track role is equal to other work eras. Paid players can earn Roman mastery, advance tier progression in Roman, and receive the full Roman experience that the free tier doesn't expose. This resolves the role conflict Roman was under v2.x (simultaneously "the free demo" and "an era on the same catalog as everything else").

**6. Grind pace is deliberately retention-first.** Grind progression toward earning non-starter eras is tuned so that **continuous subscription is strictly better than intermittent subscription**. The bible's existing v2.05 pacing target (2–4 months of active subscription play per era mastery) already implies this, but the redesign names it explicitly as a retention-tuning philosophy. A casual player (~10 hours/month) takes 6+ months per era, at which point continuous subscription is clearly mathematically preferred. This is deliberate: the grind exists to create subscription retention, not to reward maximum player-friendliness. Future pacing adjustments should be evaluated against this principle before being made — speeding up the grind "to be nicer to players" risks accidentally killing the subscription economy.

**7. Entry fee structure — RESOLVED: no entry fee (Session 4).** Free-tier Roman players go directly to the optional $2/month subscription with no intermediate paywall. Rationale: at a $2/month subscription price, the candidate entry-fee paths (trial month discount, lifetime track-access purchase, hybrid) all add friction and complexity without meaningfully improving conversion. The cleanest pitch is "play Roman free, subscribe for $2 to unlock everything else." Simplicity wins in pricing, especially at low price points where the friction of an additional transaction costs more than the transaction could possibly be worth.

**8. Multi-stage tutorial using hallmark eras.** The onboarding experience is restructured as a **multi-stage tutorial spanning multiple hallmark eras**, replacing the v2.x trial period mechanism. The tutorial structure:

- **Target length:** Approximately 15 minutes total. Not the 60–90 minutes of a traditional multi-mission tutorial — this is a tight onboarding experience that uses **sub-objectives of missions across different eras** rather than full missions. The player is dropped into a mission in Settled Reach for a specific tactical moment (not the full mission), then jumped to a mission moment in 1980s, then a third hallmark era moment. Each stage teaches one aspect of what makes the game special (layer combat in space, layer combat with realistic unit-type restrictions, a third hallmark's distinctive mechanic).
- **Skip option:** The player can skip the tutorial entirely if they choose.
- **Replay limit:** The tutorial can be replayed once per hallmark era to prevent repeat-tutorial grinding as a way to extract the full hallmark experience without paying. After the replay is consumed for a given era, that era is inspection-only until acquired through normal channels.
- **Intent:** Every player who installs the game experiences the hero feature (layer combat in the most distinctive eras) within 15 minutes. The tutorial is simultaneously the onboarding, the marketing moment, and the conversion hook. The player's mental model of the game after tutorial is not "Roman RTS" — it's "a game whose range spans far-future space combat, Cold War multi-layer warfare, and [third hallmark]." That mental model is what sells the subscription.

**Why sub-objectives instead of full missions:** A 15-minute target requires tight pacing. Full missions in hallmark eras would take 15–30 minutes each and the tutorial would balloon past 60 minutes. Sub-objectives let the tutorial showcase the *moment* of each era (the layer tactical decision, the distinctive mechanic, the visual identity) without committing to a full mission arc. This also sidesteps the "tutorial becomes the full game experience" problem — the player gets a taste, not a meal.

**After tutorial:** All hallmark eras lock to **inspection-only mode** (see component 9). The player returns to Roman free play. The conversion sequence is: played the best eras → returned to limited Roman → can now only *look* at the eras they played → wants them back. This is an emotional funnel (taste → loss → longing) that's more powerful than either a trial period (play → timer expires → no emotional peak) or a traditional demo (play the demo → same flatness before and after).

**9. Inspection mode as a first-class system.** Players can **inspect any era in the catalog** at any time, regardless of ownership or subscription status — free players included. Inspection mode is a read-only browse experience showing:
- Unit roster for that era with art, stats, abilities, tactical role descriptions
- Map samples or example region art for that era
- Mission archetype samples and example briefings
- Era-specific mechanics descriptions
- Cultural/flavor content from the era's chronicle templates
- The era's full visual identity (card treatment, logo variant, accent color, UI theming)

**What inspection mode does NOT allow:** launching missions, starting a game, earning progression, or any interaction that counts as "playing" the era.

**Reference model:** World of Warships ship inspection — the game lets players examine premium ships they don't own, rotate them, read their stats, see their module options. This creates sustained longing across multiple visits rather than a one-time trial satisfaction. Warframe operates similarly with its warframe previews. For a strategy game specifically, inspection is a better free-preview tool than brief playtime because the emotional effect is *longing* (which drives purchases) rather than *satisfaction* (which doesn't).

**Inspection mode is also a marketing surface.** Screenshots of inspection mode for hallmark eras become social content — players share what they're working toward. The inspection view of a hallmark era should be visually impressive in its own right, not just a stat readout.

**10. Mastery UI elaboration (SC2 border model).** Era mastery no longer rewards only cosmetic badges — it rewards **progressive elaboration of the era's in-game UI itself**. A player new to an era sees the base UI treatment for that era (standard HUD, standard mission briefing, standard chronicle layout). A player grinding mastery sees subtle upgrades — mastery progress indicators, rank prefixes on chronicle entries, small era-specific flourishes on the HUD. A player at high mastery sees significantly elaborated UI — custom borders in the era's accent color, bespoke animated frames on mission briefings, premium chronicle scroll treatment, a prestige badge on the era's library card. A player who has completed mastery sees the full premium UI — the entire in-era interface is rendered in a more elaborate version of itself.

**Additive, not replacement.** The mastered UI is the base UI with more layered on top. The bones are the same; the decoration is elaborated. This preserves visual identity consistency across screenshots from different players and prevents game-wide fragmentation of the visual language.

**Hallmark eras get more elaborate mastery UI than work eras.** A mastered hallmark era is significantly more visually impressive than a mastered work era. This reinforces the hallmark distinction at the reward layer and gives players a second reason to prioritize hallmark eras on the track (first reason: the era itself is more desirable; second reason: the mastery UI is more impressive).

**Reference model:** StarCraft 2 ladder borders — Masters and Grandmasters received visually distinct, larger, more imposing UI borders that persisted across their entire SC2 UI. The SC2 border system did three things simultaneously: visible personal reward, social signaling to other players, and game-wide UI change based on accomplishment. For Ouroboros Manifold (single-player), the social signaling axis mostly doesn't apply, but the other two do — and the third (UI changes based on accomplishment) is the most powerful and most underused piece of the SC2 system. Mastery UI elaboration adopts points 1 and 3.

**Inspection-mode implications:** When a free player inspects an era, they see the **base** UI treatment. When they see shared content (screenshots, clips, Steam page art) of a mastered version, they see the **elaborated** UI. The visual difference between "new to this era" and "mastered this era" is itself a marketing moment — the player inspecting an era sees what they could earn, visibly different from what they currently have.

**Production cost note:** Creating base and mastered UI variants is additional work per era. At launch, only hallmark eras need to ship with mastered UI variants; work eras can ship with base-only UI and receive mastered variants in post-launch updates. This keeps the launch production budget manageable while preserving the mastery UI concept for the eras that most need it.

**11. Conversion strategy — tutorial taste + ambient inspection (RESOLVED April 22, 2026).** The primary free-to-paid conversion strategy uses the bible's existing tutorial and inspection mode systems, executed as designed. No single conversion gate moment; no FOMO rotation; no aggressive upsell. The emotional engine is **loss aversion (taste then restrict)** combined with **sustained longing (ambient inspection)**. The tutorial gives the player a genuine taste of premium hallmark eras (Settled Reach, 1980s), then returns them to limited Roman. Inspection mode is always accessible from every screen, providing constant gentle exposure to eras the player doesn't own. Mission debrief screens show contrast ("what this mission would look like in [era]") as timed emotional nudges at gameplay peaks. This is a Pattern 1 + Pattern 2 hybrid: taste-then-restrict provides the initial conversion pressure, ambient browsing provides the sustained longing that accumulates over multiple sessions. No new systems are needed beyond what components 8 (tutorial) and 9 (inspection mode) already specify. Reference models: World of Warships ship inspection (sustained longing), Genshin Impact banner system (theatrical presentation of aspirational content), Warframe market previews (look but can't play).

**12. Era Library navigation — "Manifold Planes" spatial metaphor (RESOLVED April 22, 2026).** The Era Library (the in-game storefront where players browse, inspect, and purchase eras) uses a **stacked translucent planes** navigation metaphor. Each branch is rendered as a translucent geometric plane floating in dark space, viewed at a slight perspective angle. Planes are stacked with parallax depth — the visual reads as "parallel worlds layered through a higher dimension," which is literally what eras are in the game and what the title "Manifold" references.

**Layout:** Each plane contains its branch's eras as glowing nodes arranged left-to-right in progression order. Hallmark eras are oversized, multi-ring glowing nodes that visually dominate the plane. Work eras are smaller, dimmer nodes. Locked eras are translucent/dim. Roman floats at the origin point below all planes — the zero-dimension anchor, connected to all planes by faint dashed lines. Each plane is tinted with its branch's accent color (Branch 1 = cool blue/sci-fi, Branch 2 = warm gold/modern). Future branches appear as dashed-outline placeholder planes.

**Interaction:** Click a plane → it sweeps forward (camera tween) and flattens to fill the screen, revealing that branch's eras with full card art. The other planes fade back but remain visible as context. Click an era node → zoom into full-screen inspection mode (component 9). The player can navigate back to the full manifold view at any time.

**Aesthetic requirement:** The Era Library must feel like a sibling screen to the main menu — dark void background, gold accent palette, GPU particle systems, serif typography, the sense of peering into something vast and ancient. The main menu uses an atom-model galaxy; the Era Library uses stacked manifold planes. Both live in the same visual universe but are distinct metaphors (galaxy = your civilization's world, manifold = the meta-catalog of all possible worlds).

**Scaling:** New post-launch branches add new planes to the manifold stack. The spatial metaphor scales naturally — more planes = a deeper manifold. At 20+ eras across 4-5 branches, the manifold reads as "a rich multiverse" rather than "a cluttered list."

**Production path:** Translucent planes are shader work in Godot. Era nodes reuse existing GPU particle and glow systems. The "bring plane forward" interaction is a camera tween. Era card art within a focused plane uses the Q3 era card template assets (2D Canva exports). No 3D modeling required.

**Reference models:** No direct precedent — the manifold planes concept is unique to this game's title and theme. Indirect influences: Destiny 2 Director (spatial arrangement of content destinations), Genshin Impact banners (theatrical per-item presentation on zoom-in), the game's own main menu galaxy (dark-space aesthetic with orbital particle rings).

**13. Era card template (RESOLVED April 22, 2026).** The reusable visual unit representing each era across all surfaces (Manifold Planes nodes, Steam page, social media, Era Library). Eight sub-decisions resolved:

**Card format:** Portrait 2:3 aspect ratio. Matches Steam Library Capsule (600×900). Trading-card feel reinforces era-as-collectible identity.

**Hero visual — per-era glyph.** Each era gets a unique **compound-mark glyph** — a bold geometric core shape surrounded by a fine detail halo of era-specific notations. The core carries the era's material rendering (travertine for Roman, canvas for 1980s, etched titanium for Settled Reach); the halo gets a lighter treatment (etched, faded, ghosted). Two-scale readability: core reads alone at small sizes, halo detail appears at full card size. Production via Recraft V4 with Inkscape cleanup on halo detail as needed.

**Glyph subject — civilization's sacred object.** Each glyph depicts the object the civilization worshiped through its works. Roman = the arch (permanence through engineering). 1980s = the satellite dish / radar array (information dominance). Settled Reach = the orbital ring / Lagrange point marker (precision). Material rendering carries instant era recognition; shape carries deeper thematic meaning. Post-launch eras follow the same principle.

**Card layout — glyph-plus-context.** Glyph occupies the upper half. Lower half: era name (Newsreader serif), branch name and position, ownership status badge, mastery progress bar, one-line tagline. Era logo variant at top edge as header mark.

**Hover interaction — radial peek + side-sliding data.** On hover: symbol lifts with parallax shift; 3–4 asset thumbnails (units, structures, environments) radiate outward from center. Structured era data slides in from the sides. Full inspection remains behind click-through.

**Mastery card elaboration (SC2 border model).** Mastery progression visibly transforms the card's border and visual treatment. The difference between a base-tier card and a max-mastery card should feel like SC2 Gold vs. Grandmaster borders — earned, aspirational, a status symbol. Extends component 10's mastery UI elaboration to the era card itself.

**Hallmark vs. work era differentiation — frame + glyph complexity.** Two simultaneous differentiators: (1) hallmark cards get a gold brand-color frame treatment (thin gold rule or glow); work-era cards get no frame or a subtle accent-color hairline; (2) hallmark glyphs get a richer compound mark with more detailed halo and additional satellite elements; work-era glyphs are cleaner, simpler. Both distinctions are visible at any size and on any surface.

**Card data slots:** `branch_id`, `branch_position`, `era_category` (Purchased/Earned/Rented/Inspection-only), `mastery_tier` (drives frame elaboration), `tutorial_flag`, `is_hallmark` (drives gold frame + rich glyph), `era_glyph_asset`, `era_tagline`.

**14. Steam page presentation strategy (RESOLVED April 24, 2026).** The Steam store page is the single most important external marketing surface. Unlike Paradox (one Steam page per DLC) or Total War (one Steam page per era), Ouroboros Manifold gets **one page to sell seven eras, two branches, a free tier, and a progression system.** This constraint shapes every decision below.

**Governing principle: sell the system, not any single era.** The Steam page sells the *meta-identity* — one game that contains multitudes, the skeleton/skin concept made visible. Individual era marketing (era cards, era detail pages, per-era social posts) lives inside the game's Era Library and on the external website. The Steam page sells the *reason to download*.

**Capsule art direction — master shield or composite glyph presentation.** The capsule must not commit to any single era's visual identity. Leading with Settled Reach makes it look like a Stellaris competitor; leading with WWII makes it look like a Hearts of Iron competitor; leading with Roman leads with the free tier. Instead, the capsule features either: (a) the **master shield** (era-neutral brand identity, mythic/civilizational register, gold on dark background), or (b) a **composite of era glyphs** arranged around the master shield (arch + satellite dish + orbital ring, showing content variety without committing to one era). In both cases the OUROBOROS MANIFOLD wordmark must be legible at 184×69px (smallest Steam capsule). The gold brand color (#c9a84c) provides high contrast against Steam's dark UI. Production via Recraft V4 render of shield/glyphs → Canva composite → export at all Steam capsule sizes (462×174, 920×430, 1232×706, 748×896).

**Short description (~200 characters) — multi-era concept in one sentence.** The short description must communicate "one game, many eras" immediately. Target register: "Guide civilizations across time — from Roman legions to Cold War submarines to galaxy-spanning fleets. One engine. Every era a different game." The sentence structure is: [emotional hook — what the player does across history] + [proof of variety — three era snapshots] + [differentiator — the skeleton/skin concept]. The three-era snapshot should span the full range (ancient → modern → future) to prevent any single-genre read.

**Screenshot order — alternating eras, unified by the layer system.** The first four screenshots carry the most conversion weight (shown on hover preview in Steam lists). They must communicate "this game contains multiple visually distinct worlds" and "a meta-layer connects them." Recommended order:

- **Screenshot 1:** Tactical combat in one era (Settled Reach or 1980s — a hallmark era showing the layer system at full expression). The layer system is the hero mechanic; show it first.
- **Screenshot 2:** Tactical combat in a *visually different* era. The hard visual cut between screenshots 1 and 2 is itself the pitch — the same HUD, the same UI, completely different world. This is the "one engine, many eras" proof delivered visually.
- **Screenshot 3:** The chronicle scroll or galaxy view — the generational meta-layer. This communicates "this is not just an RTS; there is a civilizational arc above the battles." Differentiator from Stellaris (which has no chronicle) and from Total War (which has no generational persistence).
- **Screenshot 4:** The Manifold Planes era library or a branch progression view. This communicates "there is a catalog of worlds to explore and a progression system to navigate." Shows the player what they're buying into, not just what they're playing right now.
- **Screenshots 5–8+:** Deeper gameplay moments — mission briefing, different terrain types, unit variety, environmental events, a chronicle entry printing. Each screenshot should show HUD and UI to help the player understand the interaction model.

**The layer system as the unifying visual thread.** The 2.5D vertical layer system is present in every era and is the single most visually distinctive mechanic. Every era expresses it differently: Roman has terrain-elevation pseudo-layers, WWII has paratroopers and close air support crossing layers, 1980s has submarines/surface/air as strict layer locks, Settled Reach has free traversal across space layers. Showing the layer system in action across multiple eras simultaneously communicates both mechanical depth (this game has a unique tactical dimension) and era variety (the same mechanic looks completely different in each setting) without the presentation feeling unfocused. The layer system is the visual thread that says "these wildly different eras are all part of one coherent experience" — it prevents the "three games duct-taped together" misread.

**Preventing the unfocused read.** The danger of showing multiple eras on one page is that browsers think "this is three different games" rather than "this is one elegant system." Three elements prevent this: (1) the **master shield and gold palette** appear on every marketing surface as the brand constant; (2) the **HUD and UI chrome** are visually consistent across all eras (same layout, same interaction model, era accent color changes but structure doesn't); (3) the **chronicle scroll and galaxy view** are era-agnostic screens that appear between tactical missions in every era, providing visual continuity across era cuts. In screenshots and trailer, the HUD consistency across era cuts is the subliminal signal that this is one game, not a collection.

**Trailer structure — era-cut montage (~60 seconds).** The trailer is the highest-impact marketing asset and the best vehicle for the "one engine, many eras" pitch. Structure:

- **Seconds 0–5:** Hook — a single dramatic tactical moment (a fleet engagement in Settled Reach, or a submarine ambush in 1980s). Must work without sound (Steam auto-plays muted). The layer system should be visible in this opening shot.
- **Seconds 5–15:** Hard cut to a completely different era. Roman legion formation advancing on a ridge. Same HUD frame, completely different world. The cut *is* the pitch — the viewer's reaction to "wait, that's the same game?" is the conversion moment.
- **Seconds 15–30:** Rapid montage across all eras — WWII armor column, Interplanetary orbital bombardment, Colonial line infantry — each clip 2–3 seconds, showing the layer system in each era. The montage communicates catalog breadth and mechanical variety simultaneously.
- **Seconds 30–45:** Pull out to the meta-layer — galaxy view with faction territories, chronicle scroll with entries printing, the generational time-jump moment. This is where the trailer communicates "these battles are part of something larger."
- **Seconds 45–55:** The Manifold Planes era library or a branch progression visual. Show the catalog structure — "here is what you can explore." Hallmark eras glow with gold frames.
- **Seconds 55–60:** Master shield logo resolve. OUROBOROS MANIFOLD wordmark. Tagline. "Free to play. Infinite eras."

The trailer must work entirely without sound because Steam auto-plays muted. Visual storytelling, not narration. If music is added, it should be era-neutral (orchestral, not themed to any specific era).

**Branch progression infographic — static map, not price list.** The "About this game" section should include a single **infographic-style image** that shows the progression structure as a spatial map: Roman at the origin as the free anchor, two branch lines extending outward with their three eras each, hallmark eras at the apex with gold frames. This communicates "here's where you start, here's where you can go" as a journey, not a pricing table. The infographic doubles as a social media asset. Production: Canva template using era glyphs, gold/accent color coding, branch labels. No gameplay screenshots in this image — it's structural, not experiential.

**"About this game" section structure:**

- **Above the fold (first visible content):** Punchy 2–3 sentence pitch selling the Foundation feeling. No repeated capsule art. No logo lockup. Something new that advances the pitch beyond the capsule.
- **First image below pitch:** The branch progression infographic (map, not price list).
- **Feature highlights:** Short bullet points — era-agnostic engine, 7 launch eras across 2 branches, 2.5D layer combat, generational chronicle, free Roman tier. Each bullet is one sentence. No walls of text.
- **Second image set:** 2–3 screenshots not used in the header carousel, showing deeper gameplay moments (chronicle scroll, mission briefing, mastery UI elaboration).
- **Below the fold:** Longer description of the generational loop, the skeleton/skin concept, the branch system. This is for the reader who's already hooked and wants detail.

**Tag strategy.** Priority tags (highest weight): Strategy, RTS, Sci-fi, Historical, Singleplayer, Story Rich, Atmospheric, Indie. Secondary tags: Sandbox, Replay Value, Grand Strategy, Procedural Generation, Turn-Based (for the simulation layer). "Story Rich" and "Atmospheric" are high-priority because they differentiate from pure-mechanics strategy games and attract the Foundation-feeling audience. Avoid tags that lock perception to a single era (don't use "Space" as a top tag — it excludes history-interested browsers).

**What does NOT go on the Steam page:** Individual era pricing. Subscription details. Purchase flow mechanics. The Steam page sells the *reason to download the free version*. All monetization happens on the external website after the player has tasted Roman and the tutorial. The Steam page's job is conversion to download, not conversion to purchase.

**Reference models for this strategy:** Fortnite (master brand + seasonal variant presentation on a single page), Stellaris (atmospheric sci-fi positioning with "Story Rich" tagging), Total War franchise page (chronological sweep communicating era variety), Supergiant/Hades (confident, clean indie page that trusts visuals over text), Deep Rock Galactic (progression system communicated through visual elaboration, not pricing charts).

**16. In-game era merchandising — inspection-as-storefront with contrast moments and persistent acquisition banner (RESOLVED April 24, 2026).** When a player focuses on an era they don't own in the Era Library, the sell screen is **inspection mode itself** (component 9). No separate store page UI — the inspection content (unit roster, map samples, era mechanics, chronicle samples, visual identity) does the selling. Three additions convert inspection from passive browsing into active conversion:

**Persistent acquisition banner.** A compact, visually striking banner is anchored at the bottom (or top) of the inspection screen whenever the player is viewing an era they don't own. The banner is always visible — it doesn't scroll with content. It shows: era name, ownership status, and a single action button. The button label adapts to the era's access model: "Subscribe to Access" (entry era), "Earn This Era" with mastery progress bar (grindable era), or "Purchase — $X" (hallmark/premium). The button opens the external website in the system browser.

**Banner visual treatment — SC2 Masters/Grandmasters register.** The banner is not a flat UI bar — it's a **prestige-register element** styled after StarCraft 2's ranked division banners. Metallic sheen, subtle animated glow, the era's accent color as the dominant tone with gold highlights. The banner should feel like something the player *wants* to interact with, not something they want to dismiss. It occupies minimal vertical space (~60–80px) but commands attention through material quality, not size. The visual register communicates "this is premium content worth having," not "this is an ad." For hallmark eras, the banner uses gold as the dominant color with the era accent as secondary — reinforcing the hallmark's premium status. For work eras, the era accent color dominates with gold as highlight.

**Contrast moments.** At 2–3 key scroll points within the inspection flow, the content shifts from pure showcase to **split comparison**: "Here's what this mission looks like in Roman [your current era] vs. in this era." The contrast is the emotional conversion engine — the player sees their current limited experience next to the richer version. Contrast points target the moments where the era difference is most visible: (1) a tactical combat moment showing the layer system (Roman's single-layer ground combat vs. 1980s submarines + surface + air), (2) a mission briefing comparison (Roman's limited archetype set vs. the full 10-archetype variety), (3) a chronicle entry comparison (Roman's T2-locked visual treatment vs. a mastered hallmark era's elaborated chronicle). Contrast content is authored per era — start with one contrast per era at launch, add more post-launch.

**Interaction flow:** Player clicks era node in Manifold Planes → inspection screen opens with full component 9 content + persistent acquisition banner at bottom + contrast moments embedded at scroll points → player clicks acquisition button → system browser opens to the external website's era-specific purchase/subscribe page → player completes transaction on website → game receives entitlement update → era unlocks.

**What this is NOT:** This is not an aggressive upsell screen. No countdown timers. No "limited time" pressure. No modal popup interrupting gameplay. The inspection content creates longing; the contrast moments sharpen it; the acquisition banner is always available but never obstructive. The conversion philosophy is the same as component 11: loss aversion (taste then restrict) + sustained longing (ambient inspection), not FOMO.

**Reference models:** World of Warships ship inspection (persistent "Research" / "Purchase" button alongside full ship preview), SC2 ranked division banners (visual prestige register for the acquisition banner treatment), Warframe market previews (inspection with clear purchase path), Genshin Impact banner presentation (theatrical, premium-feeling acquisition surfaces that make spending feel like an event rather than a transaction).

### Branch System (Session 6)

The era catalog is organized into **branches** — mechanically distinct groups of eras that share a common tactical signature. Branches are a **monetization and progression catalog concept**, not an engine concept. The engine does not know what a branch is; each era carries its own config parameter set, and eras happen to cluster into branches because their parameter sets share a signature.

**Launch branches.** 2 at launch, 3 eras each, one hallmark per branch. Plus Roman standalone (free-tier, outside all branches). Branch 3 (ancient history, non-Roman) deferred to first post-launch expansion. Each branch extends toward 5+ eras over the long tail.

**Three axes of branch mechanical identity.** Each branch has a distinct signature stance across three axes simultaneously:

- **Axis A — Tempo.** Engagement duration, movement speed, action density per unit time.
- **Axis B — Information warfare.** Fog density, sensor range, cross-layer sight, scout-dependency vs. sensor-dominance.
- **Axis D — Attrition / economy.** Reinforcement rate, production structure density, replacement pace, force preservation value.

(Axes were considered individually during Session 6 design work; A, B, and D were retained as the hybrid. Axes labeled C and anything beyond D were considered and not selected. The A/B/D labels are preserved here for traceability.)

**Branch signatures (launch + post-launch):**

| Branch | Tempo (A) | Information (B) | Attrition (D) | Player feel |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — Sci-fi / future** | Fast. Short decisive engagements (30–45s brawls), rapid maneuver, high burst damage. | Near-total awareness. Sensor dominance, free cross-layer sight, counter-stealth is the active cost. | Small quantities, rapid recovery. Fewer units, fast replacement. Force preservation matters less; plan quality matters more. | *Plan the strike, execute fast, reconstitute and go again.* |
| **2 — Modern / recent history** | Medium. Engagements in the 1–2min range, combined-arms coordination time. | Partial sensor asymmetry. Radar, intel, reconnaissance all partial; some things seen, some things hidden; ECM and scouts matter. | Industrial replacement. Moderate quantities, moderate replacement pace. Doctrine and logistics decide campaigns. | *Combined arms, manage information, outproduce or outmaneuver.* |
| **3 — Ancient history (post-launch)** | Slow. Longer engagements (2min+), attritional grind. | Heavy fog, scout-dependent. Reconnaissance is king; ambush is a real threat; sensor systems largely absent. | Slow reinforcement, casualties hurt, force preservation matters. | *Scout carefully, preserve your force, position decides the fight.* |

**The 2.5D vertical layer system exists across every branch and every era, including Roman — but layer access, cross-layer engagement, and environmental threat vary per era along the graduated mechanical ramp.** The engine's layer infrastructure is universal: every era can express layers, detection across layers, and engagement across layers. What differs per era is *how much* of that infrastructure is enabled. Within each branch, hallmark eras at position 3 enable the fullest expression (Settled Reach: free layer traversal, fully reciprocal engagement; 1980s: strict layer lock, asymmetric engagement matrix). Work eras at positions 1 and 2 enable progressively less — position 1 eras (Planetary Colonization, Colonial/Independence) are single-layer with ground-bound engagement; position 2 eras (Interplanetary, WWII) introduce partial layer access and asymmetric engagement. This graduated ramp is the structural mechanism that makes hallmark eras feel mechanically aspirational, not just thematically premium. The layer system is always *present* in every era; it is not always *fully exercised*. See `era_mechanical_axes.md` for the full 8-axis framework defining which axes are Branch DNA (shared by all eras in a branch) vs. hallmark-exclusive (reserved for the branch's apex era).

**Roman's branch position.** Roman is outside the branch structure (`branch_id = null` or `"free_tier"`). As an ancient-history civilization, Roman's mechanical signature sits closest to Branch 3 (slow tempo, heavy fog, slow reinforcement), but Roman is tuned independently as the onboarding experience. Roman's specific stance is resolved under the Roman free-tier scope design question, not here. Working assumption: Branch-3-adjacent tempo and attrition with possibly reduced information-warfare depth to keep the free tier simpler to onboard.

**Symmetric ruleset within an era.** Both player and enemy forces play under the same branch rules within a given era. Branch is a property of the era's tactical ruleset, not a player-only restriction.

**Cross-branch faction interaction: out of scope for launch.** When the player is in an era, they fight factions from that same era. Single-era mission scope keeps balancing tractable and eliminates the sub-question of "whose branch rules apply when factions from different branches interact." Cross-era antagonist systems are a post-launch question.

**Branch identity vs. difficulty orthogonality.** Branch is "which game" (tactical capability signature). Difficulty is "how hard the game is" (AI competence, resource pressure). These are orthogonal axes. The bible principle "difficulty never changes enemy strength" still holds within a branch — difficulty modulates AI behavior and economy pressure, not the underlying capability set. Branches must not be read as difficulty tiers, and difficulty settings must not be read as branch-lite variation.

**Sacred Principle #3 compatibility — load-bearing implementation constraint.** Branch mechanical differentiation is compatible with Sacred Principle #3 (era-agnostic engine) **if and only if**:

1. **No engine code branches on era or branch.** No `if branch == "scifi"`, no `if era == "settled_reach"`. Anywhere in the engine. The engine is branch-blind and era-blind.
2. **All mechanical variation lives in per-era config parameters** the engine consumes uniformly. The parameters themselves are content, not code. Relevant parameter families:
   - Unit stats (HP, damage, speed, range multipliers)
   - Sensor parameters (cross-layer sight range, fog density, pulse cooldown, detection thresholds)
   - Engagement duration targets (used for balance validation, not engine behavior)
   - Reinforcement parameters (structure density, production time, capture mechanics, cost scaling)
   - Movement parameters (cross-layer transition cost, layer-change preconditions)
3. **Branches are a monetization and progression catalog concept, not an engine concept.** Branching structure is a *catalog* organization, not an *engine* organization. The catalog knows branches; the engine knows eras and their config.
4. **The 6 base unit types are present in every era.** The skeleton is preserved. Only their stats differ.
5. **The production structure is a single universal type** (the settlement — a population center). The *amount, placement rule, and production rate* per era are configurable.

This is the era-agnostic engine doing its job: mechanical variation expresses as content-layer configuration, not as hardcoded engine logic. Branch identity at the engine level is emergent from config signatures, not baked in. See the Sacred Principle #3 clarifying note in `game_bible.md` hub.

**Land/sea combined warfare — hallmark-exclusive feature. RESOLVED April 22, 2026.** Hallmark eras (Settled Reach, 1980s) feature combined land/sea warfare as a hallmark-exclusive mechanical differentiator. Sea units have their **own production structures** separate from land production structures. Sea and land units have **distinct models and movement styles** — they are visibly and mechanically different systems, not reskinned ground units. Work eras do NOT have combined land/sea warfare; their combat occurs on a single domain (land or space) even if partial layer access exists for other reasons (paratroopers, orbital bombardment, etc.). This gives hallmark eras a visible, aspirational feature that work eras lack: two parallel military systems (land + sea) with their own production, their own unit roster, their own movement rules, interacting on the same battlefield. Implementation: the engine supports both land and sea unit types universally; hallmark eras enable both via per-era config, work eras enable only one. Compatible with Sacred Principle #3 — no engine code branches on era.

**Reference models for the branching system:** World of Warships tech tree (navigation + preview + commitment pattern), Destiny 2 subclass rollout (branches launch as expansion events), Stellaris ascension paths (mechanical identity per path, orthogonal to difficulty). The "branches need a visible apex that rewards commitment" lesson (WoWS Superships, WoT Mastery Tanks, CoD Mastery Camos) informs per-branch terminal-mastery reward design — see open question Q-mon8.5 (cross-branch prestige).

### Access Key System

The game uses a **variable-length key system** for managing access to premium eras beyond the standard subscription and one-time purchase channels. Keys are alphanumeric codes redeemable through the in-game Era Library or a web redemption page.

**Three key types:**

- **Permanent key** — unlocks a specific era (or set of eras) forever. Equivalent to a one-time purchase. Used for giveaways, contest prizes, influencer partnerships, and promotional bundles.
- **Time-limited key** — grants full subscription-equivalent access for a fixed duration (e.g., 1 week, 1 month, 3 months). Used for promotional campaigns, social media giveaways, and Discord community events.
- **Mission-limited key** — grants access to a specific era for a fixed number of missions (e.g., 3, 5, 10). The new-player trial is implemented as an automatically redeemed mission-limited key. Used for targeted marketing (e.g., "try the 1980s era for 5 missions" codes distributed on social media).

**Key infrastructure.** Keys are generated server-side and tracked in the same entitlement database as subscriptions and purchases. Each key has: a unique code, a type (permanent/time/mission), a scope (which eras it unlocks), an expiration parameter (duration or mission count), and a redemption status. Keys are single-use — once redeemed, they are bound to the player's account. The system is lightweight and runs on the same infrastructure as the subscription auth server.

**Distribution channels.** Keys are distributed through social media posts, YouTube stream giveaways, Discord community events, influencer partnerships, and cross-promotion with other indie developers. Keys are the primary tool for zero-budget grassroots marketing — every key is a targeted conversion opportunity.

### Marketing Strategy — Product-Led Growth

The game's marketing model is **product-led growth (PLG)** — the product sells itself through quality, word of mouth, and community engagement. There is no paid advertising budget. Every marketing dollar equivalent is invested in the product itself.

**The 2.5D layer system is the hero marketing feature.** The vertical layer mechanic is visually distinctive, immediately comprehensible, and unlike anything in the current RTS market. Marketing material leads with the 1980s era (submarines/ships/aircraft across layers) and the Settled Reach era (full 3D space combat across layers) as demonstrations of the layer system. These two eras are the premium showcase — they exist in marketing material before the player downloads the game, and they are the first thing the player experiences in the trial period after download.

**Marketing channels (zero-budget):**

- **Social media** — short-form video content showing the layer system in action. Before/after comparisons (flat combat vs. layered combat), time-lapses of the generational simulation, and clips of decisive tactical moments. The 1980s era's submarine/ship/aircraft layer split is the most intuitive visual for a 15-second clip.
- **YouTube streams** — live gameplay sessions showing the simulation running, missions being played, and the generational arc unfolding. Commentary focuses on emergent stories — what the simulation produced, not what was scripted.
- **Discord** — community server for feedback, bug reports, and feature discussion. Active developer presence. Players who feel heard become evangelists.
- **The trial period is the conversion funnel.** The 3–5 mission trial of premium eras is the core marketing mechanism. The free Roman era gets players in the door. The trial shows them what they're missing. The subscription keeps them.

**UI as marketing.** The game's interface is part of the product-led growth strategy. The UI should be visually impressive and distinctively modern — clean, animated, and polished in a way that makes screenshots and clips shareable. Inspiration: Claude's interface aesthetic (simple foundations, hyper-modern execution), ASCII-rooted animations with smooth transitions. The Era Library, mission briefing screens, and chronicle scroll are all marketing surfaces — they should look good enough that players screenshot and share them unprompted.

### Target Audience

**Platform:** PC-only at launch. The game's command interface — control groups, modifier hotkeys, layer assignment shortcuts, box-select, right-click commands — is designed for keyboard and mouse. Controller adaptation would compromise the design. Console is not on the roadmap.

**Primary audience: the Stellaris-curious RTS player.** Someone who plays StarCraft 2 or Age of Empires and wishes the campaign had a living world behind it. Someone who plays Stellaris and wishes the combat was real-time tactical instead of auto-resolve. Someone who reads a Dwarf Fortress emergent story on Reddit and thinks "I want that, but I want to actually command the armies." These are PC strategy gamers, roughly 20–35, who follow indie games on Steam, watch strategy game content on YouTube, and are comfortable with mechanical depth but don't want StarCraft-level APM demands. The low micro barrier (units handle basics competently, player skill expression is in strategic direction) is critical for this audience — they want the *feel* of commanding without the APM tax.

**Secondary audience: the Homeworld/Sins of a Solar Empire crowd.** Drawn by the 2.5D vertical layer combat specifically. The layer system is visually distinctive and mechanically novel in the current RTS market. This audience clicks because the game *looks* different. They stay because the simulation gives each mission weight.

**Tertiary audience: grand strategy players** who want tactical combat that actually feels like commanding an army, not auto-resolving battles. The Crusader Kings and Europa Universalis players who have always wished the war screen was a real game. They care most about the simulation and the chronicle — the tactical combat is a bonus.

**Competitive vs. narrative.** The game serves both drives through natural rhythm separation. During missions: pure tactical skill expression. Low micro floor, high strategic ceiling. Competitive players optimize force positioning, ability timing, layer control, and reinforcement structure capture. Between missions: narrative reward. The chronicle scroll, the simulation advancing, the generational arc unfolding. Narrative players savor the emergent stories, the named characters, the faction dynamics. The two modes don't compete — they alternate. The mission briefing screen is the hinge point: it's where the narrative context becomes the tactical setup.

**The hook that gets them to click:** The 2.5D layer system in 1980s/Settled Reach marketing clips. Visual distinctiveness is the door-opener.

**The hook that gets them to stay:** The generational simulation — watching their actions ripple across centuries.

**The hook that converts free to paid:** The trial missions. Three to five missions in 1980s-era layer combat, then back to Roman ground-only. The absence of layers after the trial is the conversion pressure — they've tasted the full game and the free tier feels flat by comparison.

### UI Aesthetic — Terminal Meets Glass

The game's visual identity is **terminal aesthetics meets glass UI** — the feeling of a classified military dossier being rendered on a hyper-modern command terminal. Every screen should feel like it belongs in a world where someone built beautiful software for a civilization-spanning military command structure. The aesthetic is dark, information-dense, and precisely typeset, with warmth coming from the gold brand color and era-specific accent colors rather than from bright backgrounds or playful elements.

**Core aesthetic principles:**

- **Dark-first.** Near-black backgrounds (#0a0a0c base, #111114 surface, #18181c card, #1f1f24 elevated). The darkness creates contrast for the data and makes the accent color sing. No light mode. The game is played in the dark.
- **Two-font system.** Monospace (JetBrains Mono) for all data, labels, system text, stat readouts, timestamps, and UI controls. Serif (Newsreader) for all narrative text — mission briefings, chronicle entries, intelligence reports, flavor prose. The split creates the "machine presents the data, a human wrote the narrative" feel. The monospace is the terminal. The serif is the dossier. A third display font is permitted for the OUROBOROS MANIFOLD wordmark and logo treatment only — this is a branding element outside the in-game typography system and does not break the two-font rule for in-game surfaces.
- **Gold is the Westopoli / Ouroboros Manifold brand color.** Warm gold (approximately #c9a84c, final value TBD during logo design) owns the master brand: the main menu, the studio splash, the logo, the wordmark, the title treatment, and the Steam page meta-identity. Gold represents the game at the meta-level — it is the color of *Ouroboros Manifold as a product*, not of any specific era. This follows the Nintendo/Fortnite/CoD master-brand-plus-seasonal-variants model: the overarching brand stays fixed and individual content chapters (eras) introduce their own accents without displacing it. Era packs cannot claim gold as their era accent, and era accent color selection must account for coexistence with a gold brand frame.
- **Era-owned accent colors.** Each era owns a single accent color, distinct from the gold brand color, that permeates in-era screens (Era Library era detail, mission briefing, tactical HUD, chronicle when viewing that era's content) when that era is active. The era accent stacks on top of the gold brand rather than replacing it — gold brand elements and era accent elements coexist within the same screen with clear hierarchy (gold for brand/meta context, era accent for active in-era context). Settled Reach: **cool steel blue with teal shift**, approximately `#446e7e` — the color of brushed titanium oxidation tinted toward teal, desaturated enough to coexist with the warm gold brand frame while still reading as a distinct era identity. 1980s: **olive drab**, approximately `#5a5f3a` — US Army OD from the Cold War era, warm enough to harmonize with the gold brand frame (warm-on-warm). Roman: **oxblood**, approximately `#6b2a2a` — the deep desaturated red of Pompeian frescoes, Roman military leather, and red ochre pigment, historically authentic to the Roman material world and harmonizing cleanly with the gold brand frame (warm-on-warm). The accent color is used sparingly within its era — classification headers, faction names, key action buttons, the blinking cursor, important stat values. Everything else is near-monochrome (primary text #e8e6e1, secondary text at 55% opacity, tertiary at 30% opacity). The restraint makes both the brand gold and the era accent pop.
- **Semantic color only for data.** Red (#c44a3f) for danger/threat/high values. Blue (#5a8fbf) for info/player-side/neutral. Green (#4a9e6e) for healthy/positive. These appear only in stat readouts and progress bars where they carry meaning — never as decoration.
- **Subtle scanlines.** A faint repeating linear gradient (transparent/rgba(0,0,0,0.03) alternating at 2px/4px intervals) overlays every screen as a full-screen pseudo-element. Barely visible — felt more than seen. It's the ghost of a CRT monitor in a modern interface. It never interferes with readability.
- **Borders at 0.5px, rgba white at 6–10% opacity.** Thin enough to define structure without drawing attention. Cards and sections are bounded by structure, not by heavy lines.

**Brand Logo System — Four-Quarter Heraldic Shield:**

The master brand logo for Ouroboros Manifold is an **ornate heraldic shield** in the register of ancient/medieval metalwork — silver, pewter, or bronze finish with dense foliate borders, scrollwork, and finials. The visual target is "ancient artifact" rather than "illustration" or "modern logo." The shield reads as something unearthed from a civilization that cared deeply about ornament and meaning.

The shield is divided into **four quarters**, each representing a phase in humanity's arc, named in Latin in the heraldic tradition: **Genesis** (birth, beginning, the founding spark), **Imperium** (rule, command, dominion at peak), **Crepusculum** (twilight, decline, the long evening), and **Aurora** (dawn, the return, the cycle beginning anew). The order tells an imperial arc — birth, rule, decline, rebirth — that loops naturally back to itself: Aurora feeds Genesis. The Latin labels deepen the ancient-artifact register of the shield and align with real heraldic convention, which used Latin mottos almost universally. Quarter content is **esoteric, abstract, and open to interpretation** — stylized symbolic forms rendered as metalwork, not literal illustrations. A viewer should feel the weight of meaning without being able to point at a quarter and name specific imagery. This ambiguity is intentional and protects the logo from feeling era-locked.

The Latin labels do not necessarily appear as visible text on the shield itself — the reference aesthetic suggests visual quarters speaking for themselves. The labels are the **canonical names** for the four phases when discussed in marketing copy, press materials, codex entries, and internal documentation. A future in-game codex or "lore of the logo" reveal can surface the names and translations to curious players.

A **central ouroboros** sits at the shield's heart as the primary brand mark — the serpent eating its tail, rendered as a stylized metalwork charge. The ouroboros is the single invariant across every era variant of the logo and is the brand's most valuable visual asset.

**Style: Greco-Roman form with Celtic/Norse compositional accent.** The serpent's proportions and stance are classical Mediterranean — naturalistic-but-stylized curvature, clearly serpentine head, recognizable as "a snake" rather than as pure ornament. But the composition carries one Northern European interlace move: rather than completing a single clean loop, the body **weaves once or twice over itself** before completing the bite, or the head-and-tail meeting point is anchored with **knot work** that decoratively binds the closure. The result is a serpent that reads as classical at first glance and reveals an interlaced complexity on closer inspection. This pairs the Mediterranean register of the Latin quarter labels (Genesis / Imperium / Crepusculum / Aurora) with the ornamental density of the shield's foliate borders, producing a single coherent piece of metalwork rather than a clean charge floating against a busy frame.

**Production note.** The interlace composition is more demanding than a clean classical loop — the over-under crossings must read correctly at full logo scale, and the closure point (where the head meets the tail through the knot work) is the visual anchor that the entire mark hinges on. At small icon scale (favicon, Steam capsule corner, in-game UI badge) the interlace detail will partially drop out, leaving the underlying classical serpent loop as the legible form. This is acceptable and intentional — the mark must function at both scales, with the full ornamental complexity visible at logo size and a simpler classical reading at icon size. The two readings should not contradict each other.

**Era-agnostic exception (documented carve-out).** The four-quarter "phases of humanity" framing is a mild exception to Sacred Principle #3's era-agnostic mandate. The carve-out is narrow and explicit: the master brand logo represents *Ouroboros Manifold as a product* at a meta-layer above the engine — made by humans, marketed to humans, presented on human storefronts. The engine, simulation, mission generator, event chains, content templates, and all in-game systems remain strictly era-agnostic per Sacred Principle #3. Only the master brand logo carries this humanist framing. This exception is named here so that future design work does not read it as license to relax era-agnosticism anywhere else in the product.

The abstract, symbolic rendering of the quarters further softens the exception: a player viewing the logo may read it as "phases of humanity," as "phases of any civilization's arc," as "the four cardinal stages of the Ouroboros cycle," or as pure ornament. All readings are valid simultaneously.

**Era variants — Assassin's Creed model.** Each era that releases as a paid expansion gets a logo variant that modifies only the **frame material, border motif, and color treatment** around the master shield — the four quarters and the central ouroboros remain fixed across all variants. Each era variant is low-effort per era (the expensive master work ships once) and preserves strong brand recognition across the catalog.

**Material-per-era principle.** Each era picks a **frame material appropriate to its own register** — not just a different color or motif applied to the same metal substrate, but a genuinely different material that says something about what that era *is*. The master shield's ancient-artifact register (ornate metalwork dug from some civilization's ruins) anchors the brand; era variants are rendered as if the same shield were re-cast or re-carved in the material voice of a specific civilization's moment. This gives each era a material identity rather than just a color swatch, and establishes a visual system that can carry the full 20-era catalog without every variant feeling like a reskin of the last. The material choice for each era is a design question answered when that era's visual identity work is undertaken, not a mechanical default. When an era's material is non-obvious (e.g. a far-future or heavily abstract era), the choice is made by asking *"what material would this civilization have considered permanent, valuable, or ceremonial?"* and rendering the shield in that.

**Roman era variant.** Frame material: **carved travertine marble**, the creamy off-white stone of Roman public architecture (the Colosseum, the Forum, temple pediments). The frame is rendered as if carved in low relief directly from the marble, with architectural ornament — egg-and-dart, meander (Greek key), or similar classical architectural patterning — replacing the metalwork scrollwork of the master. This positions Roman as the *civic monument* era: the civilization that built things meant to outlast it. Roman's accent color is **oxblood**, approximately `#6b2a2a`, the deep desaturated red of Pompeian frescoes, Roman military leather, and red ochre pigment — historically authentic to the Roman material world and harmonizing cleanly with both the gold brand frame and the travertine material of the frame itself (warm-on-warm across the full mark). The marble-and-oxblood pairing is visible in any Pompeian fresco, where red ochre walls sit against travertine architectural elements, and provides a coherent Roman visual identity that is distinct from the generic "bronze-and-laurel" register of most Roman-themed strategy games.

**1980s era variant.** Frame material: **heavy military canvas / cotton duck**, the coarse-weave tan-olive fabric of Cold War field packs, combat uniforms, and equipment covers. The frame is rendered as if the shield were stitched onto or patched into a piece of canvas gear — visible weave texture, stitched edges, slight wear consistent with issued-and-carried kit. Border motif: **stenciled military equipment markings** — serial numbers, unit designations, hazard diamonds, arrow-and-T inventory marks, classification bars — applied around the frame as if the shield were property of the state. The visual target is *issued kit with history*, aligning with the bible's Cold War geopolitical framing of the 1980s era (proxy wars, intelligence agencies, classified operations) rather than any consumer/pop 1980s register. 1980s accent color is **olive drab**, approximately `#5a5f3a` — US Army OD from the Cold War era, the most universally recognized Cold War military color, warm enough to harmonize with the gold brand frame (warm-on-warm across the full mark). The canvas-over-metal material dissonance with the master shield's ornate metalwork is intentional: each era's material is what *that civilization* considered significant, and the Cold War considered a combat uniform significant in a way that Rome considered marble significant. The dissonance is managed by keeping the shield's quarters and central ouroboros fixed and visually dominant — the canvas and stencils are clearly a frame treatment around the master mark, not a replacement of it.

**Settled Reach era variant.** Frame material is **brushed titanium** — directional grain matching the aerospace-grade metal of station hulls, ship plating, and precision instruments, with the faint warm-cool iridescence titanium develops as it oxidizes. Border motif is **engraved orbital mechanics** — fine-line inscribed diagrams of orbital paths, Lagrange point notation, delta-v calculations, and trajectory geometry, treated as the ornament tradition of a civilization that permanently records mathematics onto its ceremonial surfaces. This is the specific civilizational register Settled Reach occupies: not generic sci-fi, but *the civilization whose sacred inscriptions are engineering*. The master shield's heraldic metalwork is reframed as titanium; the foliate scrollwork is replaced by engraved math. Accent color is `#446e7e`, a cool steel blue shifted slightly toward teal to pick up the titanium frame's natural oxidation tint. Desaturated enough to coexist with the warm gold brand frame per the cool-palette-coexistence constraint, while carrying enough chroma to read as a distinct era identity rather than as neutral steel. **Production test flag:** titanium iridescence is a moderate Recraft V4 test — the subtle warm-cool specular shift is a material property vector rendering tends to flatten. First production run should generate a plain brushed-stainless variant side-by-side with a titanium variant to verify whether the iridescence is rendering or whether the tool has collapsed it to generic steel. If it flattens, the fallback reading is still "brushed steel," which remains an acceptable baseline — no catastrophic failure mode, unlike a glass or dark-anodized pick would have carried.

**Ornamentation density — master vs. variants.** Only the master shield carries the full heraldic metalwork density described above (dense foliate borders, scrollwork, finials, the "ancient artifact" register). Era variants are deliberately **significantly less ornate** than the master — they are identifiable as members of the same family but rendered in a lighter, cleaner heraldic register appropriate to their frame treatment. This is both an aesthetic choice (the master should stand alone as the one maximally-ornate mark in the catalog, reinforcing its position as *the* brand anchor) and a production choice (era variants must be cheaply generable at scale across a 20-era catalog, which is only realistic if the ornamentation target per variant is restrained).

**Wordmark — OUROBOROS MANIFOLD typographic treatment.** The wordmark is a **custom-ligature drawn letterform**, not a pure font pick. The base is a classical display serif in the Trajan / Cinzel family (wide proportions, strong serifs, chiseled-capital stance derived from Roman imperial inscriptions), generated via Recraft V4's vector typography mode. The two **O** letterforms in OUROBOROS are then replaced with closed-loop ouroboros serpent marks in Inkscape, visually echoing the central ouroboros of the master shield so that the wordmark and the shield share a motif, not merely a register. The serpent-O's are rendered at the same level of abstraction as the shield's central ouroboros — Greco-Roman classical closed loop with a single Celtic/Norse knot crossing at the closure point, *no* literal serpent detail (no eyes, no scales, no ornamental features beyond the single knot). The failure mode to avoid is the serpent-O reading as cartoon or cute rather than as an elegant mark; the abstraction level prevents this. The remaining letters are unmodified from the base serif.

**Wordmark production path.** Stage 1: generate the base wordmark ("OUROBOROS MANIFOLD" in a classical display serif) in Recraft V4, expect to discard generations for spelling errors as well as for aesthetic reasons, iterate until a strong base is in hand. Stage 2a: generate standalone abstracted ouroboros serpent loop marks in Recraft V4, pick the one whose proportions and abstraction match the shield's central ouroboros, composite into the two O positions in Inkscape. Stage 2b (fallback if 2a cannot produce acceptably abstracted serpent loops): hand-draw the serpent loops directly in Inkscape using the shield's central ouroboros as visual reference. **Fallback-to-option-A clause:** if the custom ouroboros-O treatment cannot be landed cleanly via either 2a or 2b, the wordmark falls back to the base classical serif (Cinzel or equivalent licensing-clean display serif) with no custom modifications. This ensures the wordmark ships regardless of whether the custom ligature succeeds.

**Wordmark scale requirement.** The wordmark must remain legible down to Steam capsule size. Specifically, the custom serpent-O's must remain recognizable as closed loops even when fine details drop out — at small scales the knot crossing may disappear but the closed-loop silhouette must survive. This is why closed-loop serpent abstraction is preferred over serpents with elaborated detail: closed loops scale, detailed serpents do not. At sizes where even the closed-loop reading breaks down (favicons, avatars), the shield drops in as the primary mark and the wordmark is omitted.

**Wordmark production test flag (low).** The wordmark pipeline is a low-risk Recraft V4 test — classical display serifs are well-represented in Recraft's training data and typography mode outputs native vector paths, making this significantly easier than the master shield's ornate metalwork. Generating the wordmark base early in the first production month is a useful confidence-builder before the harder material tests (master metalwork, Roman marble, 1980s canvas). The one quirk to expect: Recraft V4 occasionally misspells, drops letters, or runs letters together on long strings, and "OUROBOROS MANIFOLD" is sixteen letters with a repeated-O pattern that can confuse typographic generation — plan to over-generate in stage 1.

**Future option — full per-era quarters.** The system reserves the option for marquee era releases (post-launch, revenue permitting) to go further and redraw the four quarters themselves in era-specific symbolic imagery. This is a premium marketing moment, not a launch commitment, and should only be pursued for flagship expansions where the additional production effort is justified by the launch profile.

**Production pipeline — Recraft V4 as primary.** The entire visual identity pipeline — master logo, era logo variants, era card template art, Steam marketing assets, and AI-generated in-game decorative art — is produced via **Recraft V4** (Basic plan, $10/month, commercial license). Recraft was chosen over Midjourney, Nano Banana Pro, and GPT Image 1.5 for four reasons specific to this project's needs: (1) **native SVG output** with real editable Bezier paths, which scales cleanly from favicon to billboard and imports without resolution loss into Godot and Canva; (2) **brand-style reference upload** that locks a base identity and propagates its visual DNA across every subsequent generation, mechanically enforcing series consistency across era variants; (3) **RGB palette constraint injection** per generation, which mechanically enforces the gold-brand + era-accent coexistence rule from the UI Aesthetic section; (4) **clean commercial licensing** with no training-data lawsuit exposure, which matters for a paid Steam product. The master shield is produced via a planned escalation path: first-pass generation in Recraft V4 with self-polish in Inkscape as needed, escalating to a cheap freelance touchup (~$50–$150, refinement-not-invention brief) only if Recraft alone cannot reach the bible's "ancient artifact" target. A from-scratch commission ($200–$500) is not on the production path. Era variants are generated entirely in Recraft with the finalized master shield uploaded as the brand-style reference, ensuring every variant inherits the master's visual family without manual intervention.

**Animation philosophy — simple roots, hyper-modern execution:**

- **Staggered fade-slide-in on screen load.** Every element fades from 0 opacity and translates 8px upward, easing into position. Elements appear in visual hierarchy order: classification label first (0.2s delay), then title (0.4s), then subtitle (0.55s), then divider expands horizontally (0.5s), then narrative body (0.65s), then sidebar cards cascade top to bottom (0.7s, 0.85s, 1.0s, 1.15s), then footer action bar (1.2s). The total load sequence is ~1.5 seconds. Each element's animation is 0.5–0.6s with ease timing.
- **Stat bars animate to their values.** Horizontal progress bars (military strength, parity, tension) scale from 0 to their target width over 1 second with ease timing. The animation starts when the parent card fades in, so the bar grows as the card appears.
- **Blinking cursor on live data.** A 1px-wide accent-colored cursor blinks (1s step-end infinite) after timestamps, simulation tick counters, and any value that represents "the simulation is running." This is the heartbeat of the interface — the signal that the world is alive.
- **No gratuitous animation.** Hover effects are subtle (border opacity shift, text color brighten). No bounce, no overshoot, no parallax, no particle effects in the UI. The game world has visual effects. The UI is calm.

### UI Personality — Celebration Animations and Flavor Text

The game's UI has a **personality layer** — moments where the interface breaks its military-professional tone to acknowledge the player with warmth, humor, or congratulation. These moments are rare and deliberate, which is what makes them land. The personality is expressed through ASCII-consistent animated sequences and contextual flavor text rendered in the monospace terminal aesthetic. Think Claude Code's buddy character — a small personality that lives inside the system and occasionally speaks to you.

**Key redemption personality.** When a player redeems an access key, the UI responds with a brief animated sequence and a context-aware flavor message before the standard entitlement confirmation. The animation and message vary based on key type:

- **Permanent full-access key:** The terminal aesthetic renders a brief ASCII animation — a wink, a nod, a conspiratorial gesture built from monospace characters. The flavor text acknowledges the key's origin with personality: something in the vein of *"huh — you must know somebody"* rendered in monospace with the accent color, followed by a beat, then the standard confirmation ("1980s Era — permanently unlocked"). The tone is playful and knowing — the player received this key from a person or event, and the game acknowledges that human connection. The specific ASCII art and message pool are authored content, not AI-generated — a curated set of 5–10 variants per key type so the experience doesn't feel canned on repeat.
- **Time-limited key (1 week, 1 month, etc.):** Similar personality beat but with a time-aware flavor: the system acknowledges the temporary nature with warmth rather than pressure. No countdown timers, no urgency language — just a welcome and a wink.
- **Mission-limited trial key (new player trial):** Lighter touch — a brief welcome animation that doesn't overwhelm a new player who's still learning the interface. The personality ramps up on subsequent key redemptions once the player is familiar with the game's tone.

**Era unlock celebration.** When a player gains access to a new era — through any channel (key redemption, one-time purchase, subscription activation, or tier/mastery milestone) — the Era Library plays a **celebration animation** specific to that era. The animation is ASCII-rooted and consistent with the Terminal Meets Glass aesthetic: monospace characters assemble, transform, or cascade into a visual that evokes the era's identity (Roman columns assembling from pipe characters, submarine silhouettes surfacing from tilde waves for 1980s, starfield resolving from dots for Settled Reach). The animation is brief (2–4 seconds), visually impressive, and runs once per unlock event.

The celebration sequence:
1. The Era Library card for the unlocked era pulses with the era's accent color.
2. A full-screen ASCII animation plays — era-themed, monospace-built, smooth-animated. The animation fills the screen briefly and resolves into the era's title rendered large in the serif font.
3. A flavor message appears below in monospace: congratulatory, context-aware (different text for purchased vs. key-redeemed vs. mastery-earned), and brief.
4. The animation fades and the Era Library returns to its normal state with the new era now accessible.

**Design principles for the personality layer:**

- **Rarity is the point.** These moments happen only at key redemption and era unlock — events that occur a handful of times over the player's lifetime with the game. The personality is memorable *because* the rest of the UI is composed and professional.
- **ASCII-consistent.** All animated sequences are built from monospace characters, box-drawing characters, and simple geometric shapes rendered at high resolution with smooth CSS/shader animation. The roots are plaintext. The execution is cinematic. This is the same "simple roots, hyper-modern execution" principle from the broader UI aesthetic.
- **Context-aware, not random.** The system knows how the player obtained the era (purchase, key, subscription, grind) and tailors the message accordingly. A player who ground out mastery over 3 months gets a different message than one who redeemed a giveaway key. The grinder gets acknowledged for their dedication. The key holder gets the knowing wink.
- **Curated, not generated.** Celebration animations and flavor text are hand-authored content, not AI-generated at runtime. A pool of variants per context (5–10 per key type, 1–2 per era unlock method) is sufficient. Quality over volume — each message should feel like it was written by a person who cares.

### Marketing Implications

The skeleton/skin model is the core marketing story. Every trailer, every Steam page screenshot, every community post reinforces the same message: **one game, infinite settings.** The simulation is deep enough that each era feels like a full game, not a reskin — but the player only needs to learn the mechanics once. The expansion catalog is the long tail. The subscription is the recurring revenue. The free version is the funnel.

### Distribution Infrastructure — Hybrid Steam + Direct Model

The game ships on Steam. Steam provides discoverability, community infrastructure, and a trusted storefront that players already have payment info on file with. However, era packs and subscription access are available through **two parallel purchase channels** — Steam DLC and a direct in-game storefront — with the player experience identical regardless of which channel they use.

**Why hybrid:** Steam takes a 30% cut on all sales including DLC. At the $2–3 price point per era pack, that means netting roughly $1.40–$2.10 per sale through Steam versus ~$1.94–$2.91 through direct payment processing (Stripe at ~3%). Across thousands of players buying multiple eras, the margin difference is significant. The hybrid model captures Steam's discoverability advantage for the base game while offering a higher-margin direct channel for ongoing content purchases. Players who prefer the Steam ecosystem buy there. Players who use the in-game store save a small amount and the project keeps more margin. Both channels deliver the same content, the same way, with no difference in the player's experience.

**Era pack delivery.** Era packs are JSON content libraries — naming conventions, chronicle templates, mission briefing prose, unit stat profiles, event templates, and visual asset references — plus the associated 3D model files and texture assets for units and environment objects. A complete era pack is a few megabytes for the data files, plus the 3D assets (low-poly models are lightweight — typically under 50MB total for a full era). This lightweight content profile means era packs do not require a dedicated content delivery network or game distribution infrastructure. Era pack files are hosted on **Cloudflare R2** (the same infrastructure already planned for the narrative content library). When a player purchases an era pack through either channel, the game downloads the content package from R2 on next launch, unpacks it into the local content directory, and the era becomes available for selection. Steam DLC purchases trigger the same download-from-R2 flow as direct purchases — Steam handles the entitlement check, R2 handles the content delivery.

**Subscription implementation.** Steam does not offer a native monthly subscription system. The subscription is handled through **external payment processing** (Stripe) via a lightweight account system. The infrastructure required is minimal: a basic authentication endpoint, a Stripe integration for recurring payments, and a database table tracking active subscriptions. This is not a content delivery server — Steam still delivers the base game, and R2 delivers era pack content. The subscription server answers one question: "does this player have an active subscription?" The game checks entitlement status on launch and unlocks the full feature set and included era catalog accordingly. Hosting costs for this service are negligible.

**Steam DLC as parallel channel.** Era packs are also listed as individual DLC items on the Steam store page. Players can browse and purchase era packs through Steam's native storefront without ever touching the direct purchase flow. The game checks both Steam DLC ownership and direct purchase entitlements at launch — if the player owns an era through either channel, the content is available. This means a player could buy some eras through Steam and others through the in-game store with no friction or inconsistency.

### In-Game Era Library

The game includes an **Era Library** screen accessible from the main menu. This serves as the in-game storefront for browsing, previewing, and purchasing era packs directly. The Era Library shows all available eras with preview content (thematic description, sample chronicle prose, visual style preview), the player's owned eras, and purchase options. Clicking "buy" on an unpurchased era redirects to a web checkout page (Stripe-hosted) for payment processing. After purchase, the game detects the new entitlement and downloads the era pack content automatically. The subscription option is also accessible from the Era Library — players can subscribe, manage their subscription, or check their subscription status from the same screen.

The Era Library is the primary surface for ongoing monetization after the initial Steam purchase. It is where returning players discover new eras, where the subscription value proposition is visible, and where bundle promotions and seasonal offers are presented. The library should feel like browsing a curated catalog — inviting, not aggressive.

### Platform-Agnostic Entitlement Principle

**The player's experience is identical regardless of purchase channel.** An era purchased through Steam DLC, through the in-game Era Library, or included via active subscription all appear in the game identically — same content, same integration, no extra activation steps, no license keys, no manual file management. The entitlement system is invisible to the player. If it works correctly, the player never thinks about *how* they bought something — they just have it.

This principle is non-negotiable. The moment a direct-purchase era feels like a second-class experience compared to a Steam DLC era (or vice versa), the hybrid model breaks. Both channels must resolve to the same local content state with zero player-facing friction.

### Future Consideration — Steam Workshop

If the game eventually supports community-created era packs or modding, **Steam Workshop** provides free hosting and distribution for user-generated content. The era-agnostic architecture — where the engine consumes theme pack JSON without caring about its origin — is directly compatible with Workshop distribution. This is a long-term affordance, not a near-term feature. No design or implementation work should target Workshop integration until the base game and first paid expansions have shipped and a modding community has expressed interest. It is noted here so the entitlement system and content directory structure do not make Workshop integration impossible.

## Player Progression System

The game uses a **three-layer progression system** operating at different timescales: tier progression (short-term, within an era), commander doctrines (medium-term, across games), and era mastery grind (long-term, across months). All three layers serve the same design goal: give players a reason to come back beyond the intrinsic pull of the simulation's emergent stories.

### Tier Progression — Per-Era Unit Tier Unlock

> **RESOLVED (v2.05).** Players start each era at Tier 1 and earn access to Tier 2 and Tier 3 through play. Resets per era.

When a player begins a new era (whether the free Roman era, a trial era, a subscribed era, or a purchased era), they start with access to **Tier 1 units only** for that era. Tier 2 and Tier 3 units are locked behind progression milestones earned through mission play within that era. This is a per-era reset — unlocking Tier 3 in the Roman era does not grant Tier 3 in the Settled Reach era. Each era has its own tier progression.

**What the player experiences:** Early missions feel scrappier. Tier 1 units have the roughest stat profiles and the baseline version of each ability. The player commands more numerous but less individually capable forces (the force parity system compensates for lower per-unit strength by allocating more units). As the player unlocks Tier 2 and eventually Tier 3, their forces become fewer but more capable, abilities potentially gain variant parameters, and the visual aesthetic shifts from the era's Tier 1 look (rough, early) to its Tier 3 look (refined, advanced). The progression is felt through both gameplay and visuals.

**Interaction with the simulation's tech level.** The simulation continues to track tech_level per faction (0–100) and map it to three tiers as currently designed. The player's tier unlock acts as a **cap** on the force allocation algorithm's unit selection. If the player has unlocked Tier 2 but the simulation's tech level has reached Tier 3, the force allocation draws from the Tier 2 library. If the simulation is at Tier 1 and the player has Tier 2 unlocked, the simulation's state wins — the civilization hasn't developed the technology yet. The player's unlock is a ceiling, the simulation's tech level is the floor. The player can never field units above the lower of (their unlock tier, the simulation's current tier).

**Tier unlock progression triggers.** The specific criteria for earning Tier 2 and Tier 3 are open tuning questions. Likely candidates include: missions completed within the era, success bands achieved (higher bands accelerate progression), generational arcs completed, or a combination weighted toward mission performance. The progression should feel like natural play — "I've been commanding Roman legions for a while and my forces are improving" — not like a grind checklist.

**Tier skip purchase ($1).** Players who want immediate access to all three tiers within a specific era can purchase a **tier skip** for approximately $1 per era. This is a one-time micro-purchase that permanently unlocks all tiers for that era (permanent means tied to the era purchase/subscription, not independent of it — if the player's subscription lapses and the era locks, the tier skip is preserved for when they regain access). The tier skip respects the player's time without undermining the progression for players who enjoy it.

**Open design questions (tier progression):**

- **Ability variants across tiers** — should each tier have differentiated ability parameters (e.g., Tier 1 Raider Surge is a simple speed burst, Tier 3 Surge leaves a sensor interference trail)? Or should abilities be identical across tiers with only stat changes? Ability variants create more compelling progression but require more design work per era. **Decision deferred.**
- **Progression pace** — how many missions to reach Tier 2? Tier 3? The target feel is: Tier 2 unlocked after roughly 5–10 missions in an era (the player has learned the era's character), Tier 3 after roughly 20–40 missions (the player is deeply invested). These are tuning values.
- **Progression currency or milestone-based?** — Does the player accumulate XP/points toward tier thresholds, or do they hit specific milestone conditions (e.g., "complete 3 missions at Decisive Victory or better")? A mixture is likely — base progression from missions completed, bonus acceleration from high performance.

### Commander Doctrines — Horizontal Tactical Customization

> **Future consideration.** Not a launch feature. Listed here to reserve the design space.

Commander doctrines are **small tactical modifiers** the player selects before each mission from their unlocked pool. Doctrines are horizontal (tradeoffs and preferences, not power increases) and persist across games. They give the player a customizable playstyle identity without distorting the force parity balance.

**Examples (illustrative, not final):** "Vanguard doctrine — Brace windup reduced by 0.5s." "Raider doctrine — Surge cooldown reduced by 10s but Surge duration halved." "Logistics doctrine — reinforcement structures produce 15% faster." "Intelligence doctrine — sensor pulse cooldown reduced." Each doctrine shifts how the player plays without making them strictly stronger.

**Unlock conditions** are tied to play achievements: completing missions with specific unit compositions, achieving specific success bands, winning with specific tactical approaches. Unlock conditions teach the player about the game's depth — "to unlock the Raider doctrine, win a mission with a Strike-heavy force" encourages the player to try compositions they might otherwise ignore.

**Limited slots** — the player picks 3–5 doctrines from their pool before each mission, creating a deck-building decision. **Era-specific doctrines** can be included in era packs alongside universal doctrines, adding another axis of content value per era.

Active subscribers have access to all eras. In addition to playing them, subscribers can **earn mastery progress** toward individual eras through sustained play. Era mastery is a long-term progression track — visible in the Era Library as a progress bar per era — that rewards the player for deep engagement across hundreds of hours.

**What mastery earns.** Era mastery is not an alternative to purchasing or subscribing — it is a **subscriber-exclusive engagement reward** layered on top of access. Mastery progress unlocks cosmetic rewards, era-specific doctrines (when the doctrine system ships), achievement badges visible in the Era Library, and other recognition content. The specific reward track per era is a design question for post-launch. The mastery grind gives subscribers a long-term goal ("I'm 60% of the way through 1980s mastery") and a reason to return session after session, modeled after Deep Rock Galactic's extended progression pacing.

**Mastery pacing.** Full mastery of a single era should take approximately 2–4 months of active subscription play (hundreds of missions, dozens of generational arcs). This pacing ensures mastery feels like a genuine achievement, not a checkbox. When the era catalog reaches 5–10–20 eras, completing mastery across all eras becomes a multi-year project — the kind of long-tail engagement that sustains a subscription game.

**Mastery progress requires an active subscription.** If the player's subscription lapses, they cannot access non-purchased eras and therefore cannot advance mastery. Progress is preserved server-side — resubscribing restores all progress. Mastery rewards already earned remain visible (badges, cosmetics) but era access still requires either an active subscription or a one-time purchase.

**Mastery progress triggers.** Mastery advances through natural play within each era: missions completed, success bands achieved, generational arcs completed, tier progression milestones reached, specific tactical achievements. The progress formula weights variety and depth over raw repetition — the player who completes 50 different missions across multiple archetypes progresses faster than the player who grinds the same mission type 50 times.

### Future Consideration — 1980s Era Pack

The 1980s era is the primary marketing showcase for the 2.5D layer system and should be one of the first premium era packs developed. Its layer mapping is the most intuitive demonstration of vertical combat:

- **Layers 4–5 (bottom):** Underwater. Submarines operate here. Surface units cannot enter. Sensor interference is high (sonar-based detection with limited range).
- **Layer 3 (middle):** Surface. Ships and land units operate here. The "ground plane" of the era. Full sensor coverage at surface level.
- **Layers 1–2 (top):** Airspace. Aircraft operate here. Fast movement, high sensor range, vulnerable to surface-based anti-air.

The era's unit-type layer restrictions are strict: submarines cannot surface, surface vessels cannot submerge, aircraft cannot land. This creates clean, readable layer separation where each layer has a distinct force composition and the tactical decisions are about cross-layer interaction — how air power supports surface engagements, how submarines threaten ships from below, how surface escorts screen against submarine attacks.

The 6-unit roster maps naturally: Strike units become fast attack craft (Raiders as fighter jets on layers 1–2, Interceptors as ASW helicopters screening between layers), Line units become the surface backbone (Vanguards as destroyers/cruisers on layer 3, Wardens as support ships), Siege units become the heavy hitters (Bombards as strategic bombers on layers 1–2 or battleships on layer 3, Bastions as submarines on layers 4–5). The exact roster mapping is a design question for the era pack's development phase.

The 1980s era is also the trial-period showcase — new players experience this era for 3–5 missions before the trial expires, making it the single most important era for first impressions and conversion.

### Future Consideration — Unit Skins

The engine's abstract type → visual asset mapping naturally supports cosmetic variants. A "skin" is an alternate 3D model (or material/texture variant) for a given unit type within a given era. The engine already loads models by looking up the abstract type in the theme pack's asset map — skins extend that lookup to check whether the player owns an alternate visual and which one they've selected.

**Two tiers of skin complexity.** Material/color variants (same geometry, different color palette — a "winter" variant, a "gilded" variant) are trivial to produce: ~10 minutes per skin in Blender, priced at $0.50–$1.00. Model variants (different geometry that still reads as the same unit type at tactical zoom distance — a "veteran" unit with battle damage, an "elite" variant with a distinct silhouette) go through the standard Tier 1 asset pipeline at 30–45 minutes each, priced at $1.50–$2.00.

**Per-force, not per-unit-type.** The player picks a visual theme for their entire force within an era, rather than skinning individual unit types. This fits the narrative identity (you're a commander choosing your force's livery, not a unit customizer), simplifies the UI, and can be extended to per-unit-type granularity later if player demand justifies it.

**Distribution.** Skins flow through the existing hybrid purchase model — available both as Steam micro-DLC and through the in-game Era Library's customize tab via Stripe. Same platform-agnostic entitlement principle applies. Skins are high-margin, low-effort content that creates a steady drip of small-ticket purchases. A release cadence of 3–5 skins per era per month is achievable with minimal production time and compounds over the era catalog.

**When to build.** Not until the base game has finished 3D models for at least the sci-fi era, a working visual rendering pipeline in Godot, and the Era Library storefront. The engine architecture does not need to change to support skins — when the time comes, it's an extension of existing systems, not a new system.

### Future Consideration — Multiplayer

Multiplayer is not a launch feature. It is documented here because the game's architecture has properties that make multiplayer viable as a post-launch addition if player demand justifies it, and because certain architectural decisions made now (simulation determinism, symmetric faction agents) should be preserved to keep the option open.

**Why the architecture supports multiplayer.** The faction agent system is symmetric — every faction (player's civilization, Conqueror, breakaway factions) runs the same decision framework. Replacing an AI agent's `act()` call with a human player's decision is mechanically straightforward; the simulation doesn't care which entity made the choice. The simulation is also deterministic given the same inputs: same seed and same sequence of decisions produce the same world state. This property is the foundation that makes all multiplayer models work, because clients can compute state locally given synchronized inputs rather than streaming full state every tick.

**Three viable multiplayer models, in order of implementation cost:**

**Model 1 — Asynchronous shared world (cheapest, build first).** Players each run their own missions in their own time, sharing the same world. During time jumps, all human-controlled factions' autonomous agents run alongside the AI factions. When any player completes a mission, the world state syncs. This is "play-by-mail" at civilizational scale — players don't need to be online simultaneously. The shared chronicle scroll becomes the social glue: players see each other's mission outcomes, watch each other's civilizations rise and fall, and experience the Ouroboros together across centuries.

Infrastructure: a lightweight server stores the shared world state (a JSON blob — simulation state is small) and coordinates turn order. This can run on the same infrastructure as the subscription auth server. A single small cloud instance at $20–50/month supports hundreds of concurrent games. No real-time networking, no tick synchronization, no latency concerns. Engineering time: 2–4 weeks.

**Model 2 — Simultaneous missions in shared world (moderate).** Multiple players are active in the same world at the same time, each commanding their own faction's forces on independent missions. Missions run independently (no real-time interaction between players' tactical combat), but the world state reflects all players' outcomes. When Player A completes a mission in one region, the consequences are visible to Player B when their next mission generates.

Infrastructure: a persistent game server holds the authoritative world state, processes time jumps, and distributes mission briefings. Since missions themselves are single-player tactical experiences (no real-time PvP combat), standard web servers with WebSocket connections handle this — no low-latency game servers required. $50–200/month per active game cluster. Engineering time: 1–2 months.

**Model 3 — Real-time PvP combat (expensive, build last if ever).** Players encounter each other's forces during missions — two human commanders fighting over the same region in real-time tactical combat. This is the full multiplayer experience and the most complex to implement.

Infrastructure: real-time combat requires authoritative game servers with low latency (<100ms round-trip), tick-synchronized simulation, prediction and reconciliation netcode, dedicated server instances per active battle, and geographic distribution (US East, US West, EU, Asia minimum). $500–2,000+/month scaling with concurrent player count. Engineering time: 3–6 months.

**Why real-time PvP requires 3–6 months of engineering.** The core problem is latency. When Player A moves a Strike squad to flank, Player B needs to see that movement within ~100ms or the game feels broken, but the internet introduces 30–150ms of delay. Two established solutions exist, both substantial engineering efforts:

*Lockstep networking* (Starcraft's approach): both clients run the same simulation and only exchange player inputs. Bandwidth-efficient, but requires absolute cross-platform determinism (any floating-point divergence causes desync) and the game runs at the speed of the slower connection. Requires a deterministic command queue, synchronization protocol, desync detection and recovery, and extensive cross-platform testing.

*Client-server with prediction* (most modern real-time games): the server runs the authoritative simulation while clients predict outcomes locally and reconcile when the server's state arrives. Hides latency through prediction but produces visible corrections (rubber-banding, position snapping) when prediction is wrong. Requires a prediction system, a reconciliation/rollback system, an interpolation layer for smooth rendering, server-side hit detection, and bandwidth optimization.

Both approaches require rethinking how every combat interaction communicates about time. Contested actions (both players attack the same tick), layer transitions (one player sees stale position data and attacks a ghost), and ability timing (50ms disagreement on activation means a barrage hits or misses) all become negotiation problems between client prediction and server authority. The 2.5D layer system and unit abilities add additional synchronization complexity on top of the base netcode.

The timeline breaks down roughly as: month one for the networking layer (server, protocol, state sync, disconnect handling), month two for prediction and reconciliation (the iterative "feels right" tuning), month three for testing and edge cases, and months four through six for optimization, geographic deployment, and matchmaking.

**Recommendation.** If the game experiences high demand, launch Model 1 (async) first — it's cheap, fast to build, and the generational structure makes asynchronous play feel natural rather than compromised. Upgrade to Model 2 if sustained demand justifies a persistent server. Only commit to Model 3 if the player base is large enough to justify the infrastructure spend, and at that point the revenue likely supports hiring a dedicated netcode engineer — someone who has solved these problems before and knows which corners can be cut safely.

**Architectural preservation.** To keep multiplayer viable as a future option, the following properties of the simulation must be maintained: deterministic output given the same inputs (same seed + same decisions = same state), symmetric faction agent interface (human and AI agents are interchangeable), serializable world state (the full simulation state can be exported and imported as a compact data structure), and clean separation between the simulation backend and the Godot frontend (the WebSocket interface already enforces this).
