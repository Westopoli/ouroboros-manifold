# Era Style: Colonial / Independence Era

## Setting

The 18th-century Atlantic world and its global ripples. European colonial powers, plantation economies, independence movements, revolutionary rhetoric, indigenous displacement. The tension between Enlightenment ideals and brutal exploitation. Declarations are being written, constitutions drafted, armies raised from farmers. The map is being redrawn by people who believe they are building something new — often on top of something old they're destroying.

## Tier Identity (visual/tonal)

- **Tier 1 — Colonial Outpost:** Raw timber, stockade walls, improvised. The edge of empire, undersupplied and overextended.
- **Tier 2 — Established Colony:** Proper stone buildings, trade infrastructure, civic institutions. Colonial wealth visible in architecture.
- **Tier 3 — Revolutionary State:** New nation aesthetics — classical revival architecture, declaration-era rhetoric made physical, the self-conscious grandeur of a people proving they deserve sovereignty.

## Naming Philosophy

Names in this era carry the weight of competing traditions: the colonial power's naming conventions (British, French, Spanish, Dutch, Portuguese), indigenous names that survived colonization (adapted, anglicized, or preserved), and the new naming that comes with revolution — places renamed for ideals, people taking new names to mark new identities, military units named for the cause.

There's a performative quality to naming in revolutionary contexts: names as political statements, as claims of legitimacy, as acts of defiance. But the everyday names — of farms, of families, of small towns — are more prosaic. The best names from this era sit at the intersection of the grand and the ordinary.

## Per-Category Philosophy

### People
A mix of European colonial naming traditions (English, French, Spanish, Dutch), indigenous naming patterns that have been adopted into the colonial framework, and African-derived names from enslaved and freed populations. Revolutionary figures take classically-inspired names or titles. Family names often derive from occupation, geography, or patronage. The era produces both "John Adams" types (plain, English, landed) and "Toussaint L'Ouverture" types (a single name that IS a political act).

### Places
Colonial place names: geographic descriptions (Cape Fear, Long Island), royal/aristocratic dedications (Charlestown, Georgetown), religious names (San Salvador, Providence), indigenous names retained or corrupted (Manhattan, Chesapeake), plantation/estate names, and revolutionary re-namings. The best carry a sense of who named them and why.

### Units
Militia companies, continental armies, naval squadrons, guerrilla bands. Named after their home region ("Virginia Rifles"), their commander ("Marion's Brigade"), their cause ("Liberty Guards"), or their composition ("The Minute Men"). Some units carry formal royal designations; others are deliberately informal as a political statement. Mix of professional military tradition and revolutionary improvisation.

### Factions
Colonial administrations, independence movements, trading companies, indigenous confederacies, plantation owner blocs, abolitionist societies, revolutionary committees, royal loyalist organizations. Names reflect the era's political vocabulary: "congress", "committee", "company", "confederacy", "republic", "society", "league."

## Anti-Patterns for This Era

- No modern military terminology (no "special forces", "battalion" in modern sense)
- No anachronistic technology references
- No single colonial power dominates — mix English, French, Spanish, Dutch, indigenous
- No sanitized version of colonialism — names should reflect the era's full complexity
- No fantasy elements or made-up languages
- No 19th-century terminology (no "Victorian", no industrial revolution vocabulary)
