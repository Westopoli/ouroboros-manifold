# Era Style: 1980s

## Setting

Cold War geopolitics at their most tense and baroque. Proxy wars, intelligence agencies, satellite surveillance, nuclear brinkmanship. The aesthetic of espionage, defense contractors, think tanks, and classified programs. NATO reporting names for Soviet equipment. Coded transmissions. The world divided into blocs with entire civilizations caught between them. Technology is advancing rapidly — computers, guided missiles, stealth aircraft — but the culture hasn't caught up to the technology.

## Tier Identity (visual/tonal)

- **Tier 1 — Surplus:** WWII hand-me-downs, mismatched equipment from different suppliers. Names feel improvised, third-world-practical, hand-me-down.
- **Tier 2 — Superpower Client:** Standardized modern equipment from one bloc. Names feel professional, institutional, bloc-aligned.
- **Tier 3 — Cutting Edge:** Stealth geometry, smart weapons, satellite uplinks. Names feel classified, technical, ominous.

## Naming Philosophy

1980s naming is dominated by the institutional languages of the Cold War: intelligence agency terminology, defense contractor jargon, NATO classification systems, diplomatic euphemisms, and the propaganda vocabulary of both blocs. People have ordinary names but their organizations have coded, bureaucratic, or ideological names. Places in this era are often proxy-war battlegrounds — third-world locations caught between superpowers.

The key aesthetic: mundane surfaces hiding extraordinary danger. A "consulting firm" is an intelligence front. A "research institute" develops weapons. A "liberation movement" is a superpower proxy. Names should carry this double-meaning quality — anodyne on the surface, loaded underneath.

## Per-Category Philosophy

### People
Drawn from the global Cold War theater: American, Soviet, British, French, Middle Eastern, Southeast Asian, African, Latin American, Central European. Given names and surnames reflect the genuine global reach of Cold War geopolitics. Intelligence operatives use cover names and code names alongside real names. The era produces both "ordinary spy" names (the banality of espionage) and "larger-than-life" names (dictators, arms dealers, defectors).

### Places
Cold War geography: proxy-war zones, missile bases, radar installations, classified research facilities, neutral meeting points, divided cities. Some are real geography repurposed (mountain passes become defensive positions, harbors become naval bases). Others are institutional designations (Site 4, Station X, The Compound). The best 1980s place names feel like they're on a classified briefing map.

### Units
Task forces, special operations groups, intelligence sections, rapid deployment forces. Named with military precision but often with classified codenames layered on top. Mix of formal designation (5th Special Forces Group) and operational codenames (Task Force Orange). Some units are deniable — their names are designed to be forgettable or misleading.

### Factions
Intelligence agencies, defense ministries, liberation fronts, puppet governments, multinational alliances, arms cartels, research directorates, clandestine services. Names should feel like Cold War institutions — formal, bureaucratic, occasionally ideological, always with something hidden behind the bland title.

## Anti-Patterns for This Era

- No post-Cold-War terminology (no "war on terror", no "cyberwar", no "drone strike")
- No sci-fi or fantasy elements
- No medieval/ancient references
- No internet/digital-age vocabulary — computers exist but the culture is pre-digital
- No single bloc dominates — draw from both sides and the non-aligned world
- No movie/TV character names from the era (no "Bond", no "Rambo")
- Avoid overt propaganda language — the era's naming is euphemistic, not bombastic
