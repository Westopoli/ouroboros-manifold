# Era Style: Roman

## Setting

The Roman Mediterranean and its sphere of influence: Italy, Gaul, Hispania, Britannia, North Africa, the Levant, Greece, Egypt. Spans Republican grit through Imperial height to late-Imperial excess. A civilization that built roads, aqueducts, and legal systems — and also enslaved millions and fed people to animals for entertainment. Deeply practical, deeply brutal, deeply sophisticated. The Latin language as administrative technology. Provincial diversity under imperial unity.

## Tier Identity (visual/tonal)

- **Tier 1 — Republican Grit:** Practical, utilitarian, earth-toned. Names feel sturdy, Latin, plain.
- **Tier 2 — Imperial Might:** Red and gold, organized power. Names feel formal, confident, properly Roman.
- **Tier 3 — Late Imperial / Divine:** Ornamental excess, purple and gold. Names feel grandiose, hybrid, slightly decadent.

## Naming Philosophy

Roman naming follows real Roman conventions: the *tria nomina* (praenomen + nomen + cognomen) for citizens, single names for slaves and freedmen, provincial adaptations for non-Roman subjects. But this isn't a history textbook — it's a game. Names should FEEL Roman without being a Wikipedia list of actual Roman names. Draw from Latin phonetic patterns, Mediterranean linguistic traditions (Greek, Punic, Celtic, Aramaic), and the rhythms of Roman bureaucracy.

The key quality: Roman names feel *institutional*. Even personal names carry family, clan, and political weight. A Roman name tells you where someone fits in the social order.

## Per-Category Philosophy

### People
Follow Roman naming conventions loosely: given name + family name + distinguishing name. But vary the structure — not every name needs to be three parts. Provincial characters might use two-part names from their native tradition. Freedmen might use a single Romanized name. Women's names follow their own conventions (typically the feminine form of the family name). The pool should include names from across the Roman world — Gauls, Greeks, Syrians, Egyptians, Hispanics — not just Italians.

### Places
Roman place names derive from: geography (Lugudunum = "fortress of Lugus"), founders (Augusta Treverorum), function (Aquae Sulis = "waters of Sulis"), military camps that became cities (Castra → Chester), and pre-Roman indigenous names Latinized. Mix Latin-constructed names with adapted provincial names. Avoid only using famous real Roman cities — invent places that FEEL like they belong on a Roman road map.

### Units
Legions, cohorts, auxilia, naval squadrons. Roman military naming: numbered legions with honorific cognomina (Legio VII Claudia), auxiliary cohorts named for their ethnic origin (Cohors I Batavorum), naval detachments named for their base (Classis Misenensis). Invent units that follow these patterns without copying real ones. Include both citizen legions and provincial auxiliaries.

### Factions
Senatorial factions, provincial administrations, merchant guilds (collegia), religious orders, military commands, client kingdoms. Roman political naming: family-derived (the Cornelii), geographic (the Province of Asia), institutional (the Praetorian Prefecture), or ideological (the Optimates, the Populares).

## Anti-Patterns for This Era

- No medieval elements (no "knights", no feudal terminology, no Gothic architecture vocabulary)
- No modern military terminology (no "battalion", "regiment", "brigade")
- No fantasy-Latin (no made-up words that just add -us or -ium to English words)
- No Greek-only names for Roman contexts (Greek names are valid for freedmen and easterners, but the pool shouldn't be exclusively Greek)
- No famous real Roman names (no Julius Caesar, no Augustus, no Nero, no Cicero)
- No Christian terminology for the Republic/early Empire — Christianity appears only in late Imperial contexts
