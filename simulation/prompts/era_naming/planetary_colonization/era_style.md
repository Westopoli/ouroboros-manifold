# Era Style: Planetary Colonization

## Setting

First-generation colony culture on alien worlds. Humanity has reached other planets but is still in the homesteading phase — establishing settlements, surveying terrain, building infrastructure from scratch. The colony ship brought everyone's grandparents; now the second and third generations are making these worlds *theirs*. Harsh terrain, limited resources, founding-family politics. The connection to Earth is cultural memory, not a daily reality.

## Tier Identity (visual/tonal)

- **Tier 1 — Homestead:** Raw materials, improvised tools, survival shelters. Names feel rough-hewn, practical, land-connected.
- **Tier 2 — Township:** Organized settlements, proper governance, infrastructure. Names feel established, community-rooted, civic.
- **Tier 3 — First Cities:** The colony becomes self-sufficient and develops its own culture. Names feel native — born here, not imported.

## Naming Philosophy

Names in the Planetary Colonization era reflect a community that carried Earth traditions on generation ships and is now adapting them to alien soil. Founding-family dynasties dominate local politics. Place names come from first-survey geography ("Red Mesa", "The Salts"), from the families who homesteaded them, or from the generation ship that delivered the colonists. There's a frontier quality — shortened names, practical naming, functional labels that became proper nouns — but also a formality inherited from shipboard hierarchy.

The key tension: imported culture vs. born-here identity. First-generation names sound more Earth-derived; third-generation names have drifted, blended, simplified.

## Per-Category Philosophy

### People
Family names carry heavy weight — who your grandparents were on the colony ship determines your social position. Founding-family dynasties have recognizable surnames. Given names blend Earth traditions in ways that reflect which ship brought which cultural mix. Some purely local naming traditions have emerged: names drawn from local geography, colony-specific events, or the alien environment (mineral names, season names from the new world's calendar).

### Places
Named by surveyors (descriptive terrain labels), by homesteaders (family names + geographic feature), by the colony administration (numbered districts that became names), or by the community (event-names, hope-names, memorial-names). The best planetary colonization place names feel like they were named by someone standing on unfamiliar ground and trying to make it feel like home — or explicitly NOT home.

### Units
Militia and colonial defense forces. Named after local geography ("Mesa Guard"), founding families ("Vasquez Rangers"), or colony-ship heritage ("Third Deck Volunteers"). Some units carry formal designations from the colony charter; others have informal names that stuck. These feel more like frontier militia than professional military — local, personal, community-rooted.

### Factions
Colonial governance bodies, homesteader coalitions, founding-family blocs, frontier cooperatives, ship-heritage associations, resource extraction combines. Names reflect local politics: who controls the water, who owns the best land, who speaks for the outer settlements vs. the central township.

## Anti-Patterns for This Era

- No interstellar-scale terminology (no "sector", "quadrant", "star system" as name components)
- No high-tech or sleek-future aesthetics in names — this is dirt, sweat, and survival
- No Earth nation-state names used directly (no "New America", "Neo-Japan")
- No alien language influences — the aliens (if they exist) haven't been met yet
- No corporate-future jargon — these are farmers and builders, not executives
- No military formality — these are volunteers defending their homes, not a professional navy
