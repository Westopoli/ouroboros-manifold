# Era Style: Interplanetary

## Setting

Humanity's first steps beyond Earth orbit. Early spacefaring — stations, asteroid mining operations, lunar and Martian settlements, Jovian exploration. The optimism of the space age meets the grinding reality of living in tin cans. International cooperation is real but fragile. Space agencies, multinational consortiums, and early private ventures compete and collaborate. Everything is dangerous, expensive, and slow.

## Tier Identity (visual/tonal)

- **Tier 1 — Hab Pods:** Pressurized cylinders, solar panel arrays, modular survival architecture. Names feel functional, technical, bureaucratic.
- **Tier 2 — Settlement:** Printed structures, proper airlocks, maglev tracks. Names feel established, institutional, professional.
- **Tier 3 — Terraformed Edge:** Partial atmosphere, hybrid structures. Names feel aspirational, poetic, forward-looking.

## Naming Philosophy

Names in the Interplanetary era carry the fingerprints of Earth's space agencies and international institutions. Station designations that became proper names. Mission codenames that stuck. Explorer surnames immortalized on the things they discovered. An optimistic internationalism where every major Earth culture contributed — reflected in a genuine diversity of linguistic origins rather than one dominant tradition.

The bureaucratic layer is strong: consortium names, mission designators, station numbers that became place names (Station 7 becomes "The Seven"). But the human layer pushes through — people name their habitats after home, after hope, after grief.

## Per-Category Philosophy

### People
A genuinely international mix reflecting early 21st-century Earth demographics, slightly shifted by who self-selects for space: engineers, scientists, military pilots, miners, doctors. Surnames from every major Earth tradition coexist. Given names trend practical and short — radio-friendly. Some families have been spaceside long enough for naming conventions to drift slightly from Earth norms.

### Places
Named by agencies (alphanumeric designators that became names), by the people who built them (Kowalski Dome, Chen Station), by what they do (The Refinery, Exchange Hub), or by what they hope to become (New Geneva, Promise). The best interplanetary place names feel like they were assigned by a committee and then humanized by the people who actually lived there.

### Units
Military and paramilitary designations from early space forces. Patrol squadrons, security details, mining escort flotillas. Names reference operational zones (Belt Patrol, Lunar Watch), notable incidents, or parent organizations. Some units carry Earth military heritage (numbered squadrons, named operations); others reflect the new environment (vacuum-specific terminology, orbital mechanics references).

### Factions
International agencies, mining consortiums, station confederacies, early independence movements from Earth-orbit populations, corporate alliances, scientific cooperatives. Names should feel like real institutions from an era of international cooperation under pressure — formal, bureaucratic, but occasionally idealistic.

## Anti-Patterns for This Era

- No faster-than-light terminology — this is pre-FTL, everything is slow and dangerous
- No alien species or alien language influences — humanity is alone
- No far-future transcendence (no glowing hulls, no post-singularity concepts)
- No fantasy/medieval intrusions
- No single nation dominates — this is international
- Names shouldn't feel like Star Trek (no USS Enterprise, no Federation)
