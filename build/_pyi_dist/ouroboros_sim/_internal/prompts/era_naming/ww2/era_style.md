# Era Style: World War II

## Setting

1939-1945. Total war on an industrial scale. Every major power is fully mobilized. The war machine dominates culture, economics, and daily life. Names from this era carry the weight of vast bureaucracies, mass production, and the collision of national identities. Operation codenames, regiment numbers, battlefield legends, and the surnames of millions of ordinary people fed into the machine.

## Tier Identity (visual/tonal)

- **Tier 1 — Mobilization:** Interwar leftovers, early-war improvisation. Riveted hulls, pre-war paint. Names feel hasty, provisional, bureaucratic.
- **Tier 2 — Industrial Peak:** Purpose-built war machines. Welded hulls, mass-produced efficiency. Names feel standardized, professional, grim.
- **Tier 3 — Wunderwaffe / Late War:** Experimental, desperate, pushing beyond reliable. Names feel grandiose, ominous, or desperately hopeful.

## Naming Philosophy

WW2 names emerge from the collision of multiple national military traditions, industrial bureaucracy, and propaganda. Operation codenames are deliberately random (Churchill insisted they not hint at their purpose) or deliberately evocative (German codenames tended toward mythology). Weapons and vehicles get both official designations and soldier nicknames. Battlefields become legend-names. Ordinary people's ordinary surnames appear on casualty lists and are immortalized on monuments.

The era's naming feel: institutional, multinational, industrial, occasionally mythological, always with the shadow of the machine behind it.

## Per-Category Philosophy

### People
Draw from all major combatant nations: British, American, Soviet, German, French, Japanese, Italian, Australian, Canadian, Indian, Polish, and many others. Surnames should reflect this genuine multinational character. Given names tend toward the conventional for each culture — this isn't an era of creative naming. The contrast is between the ordinariness of the names and the extraordinariness of what these people did.

### Places
Battlefields, operations, defensive lines, landing beaches. Some are pre-existing place names that became synonymous with battle (Stalingrad, Midway, Anzio). Others are codenames assigned to objectives (Omaha, Gold, Sword). Some are geographic features that mattered tactically (Hill 112, The Bulge, The Bocage). The best WW2 place names are ordinary places made legendary by what happened there.

### Units
Regiments, divisions, squadrons, task forces. Carry formal numerical designations but also nicknames and battle honors. Named after home regions ("The Highland Division"), notable traits ("The Desert Rats"), historical heritage ("The Old Guard"), or assigned codenames. Mix of formal military naming (1st Armored Division) and informal soldier culture (The Screaming Eagles, The Rats of Tobruk).

### Factions
National armed forces, resistance movements, intelligence agencies, industrial combines, alliance structures, occupation governments, underground networks. Names should feel bureaucratic and institutional — these are organizations fighting a war of paperwork as much as bullets.

## Anti-Patterns for This Era

- No post-war terminology (no "cold war", "NATO", "nuclear" — those came after)
- No sci-fi or fantasy elements
- No single nation dominates — this was a world war, draw from all combatant nations
- No sanitization — but also no glorification; names should feel matter-of-fact
- No modern casual naming conventions — military formality dominates
- No rank-as-name (avoid "Private Jones" or "Captain Smith" as the name itself)
